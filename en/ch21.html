Chapter 21 Higher-Order Beliefs, Bubbles and Coordination

Abreu and Brunnermeier (2003) open their paper on bubbles with the internet bubble of the late 1990s. Their results on market timing, they write, seem to fit well with popular accounts of how hedge fund managers behaved during that bubble. Their example is Stanley Druckenmiller, who managed George Soros’s $8.2 billion Quantum Fund. Asked why he did not get out of internet stocks earlier, although he knew that technology stocks were overvalued, he replied that he thought the party was not going to end so quickly: “We thought it was the eighth inning, and it was the ninth.” Facing mounting losses, he resigned as the fund’s manager in April 2000 (as reported by Abreu and Brunnermeier). The sample is one manager of one fund, the period the internet bubble up to April 2000, and the source a popular account that the authors retell, with no data behind it (our judgment).

The authors argue that a bubble can persist even though all rational arbitrageurs know that the price is too high and together could correct it. This raises a question (our judgment): if many professionals know that a price is too high and together could correct it, why does the price not move? Section 1.6.3 gave their setting as one of three untested answers for EntreMed.1

21.1 Higher-order beliefs and common knowledge

The root model needs only first-order knowledge, the default that traders need to know only the fact (Table 5.1). Section 12.9 dropped it for announcements; this section drops it in general.

21.1.1 Knowing that others know

In a model of Chwe’s that Easley and Kleinberg retell, each person in a social network will join a protest if she is sure that at least kk people in total, herself included, will show up; she knows the thresholds of her neighbors and, because talk of dissent is risky, nobody else’s, and everyone knows what the network looks like (as reported by Easley and Kleinberg; working-paper version). In one network of four people, each knows that three people have thresholds of 3, enough for a protest, yet each holds back, unsure that the others know it; shift one link, and among those three the fact becomes common knowledge (working-paper version). The three know the fact in both networks, and the network decides whether it becomes common knowledge (our judgment).

Aumann (1976) gave the definition: an event is common knowledge between two people, 1 and 2, when both know it, 1 knows that 2 knows it, 2 knows that 1 knows it, 1 knows that 2 knows that 1 knows it, and so on, as when both are present at the event and see each other there. His model also takes the ways in which information reaches each person to be common knowledge, no loss of generality in his view, since they belong to the full description of a state; it is an assumption about the model’s structure (our judgment).

His example shows that knowing each other’s beliefs falls short. Take four equally likely states, α\alpha, β\beta, γ\gamma and δ\delta. Person 1 learns whether the state is one of α,β\alpha, \beta or one of γ,δ\gamma, \delta; person 2 learns whether it is one of α,β,γ\alpha, \beta, \gamma or is δ\delta. The event of interest is “α\alpha or δ\delta”, and the true state is α\alpha. Then 1 knows that 2’s posterior for the event is 1/3, and 2 knows that 1’s is 1/2, but 2 thinks that 1 may not know what 2’s posterior is, 1/3 or 1. Person 1’s posterior is 1/2 in every state, so it is common knowledge; person 2’s is 1/3 in three states and 1 in the fourth, so it is not (our judgment). Each knows the other’s posterior, and the two still differ; in Aumann’s words, his result is not true if the two merely know each other’s posteriors.2

21.1.2 Agreement, trade and common knowledge

Aumann’s theorem states that if two people have the same priors and their posteriors for an event are common knowledge, the posteriors must be equal, even though they may base them on quite different information. Its conclusion concerns posteriors and says nothing about trade, and the four-state example shows why it needs the posteriors to be common knowledge (our judgment).

Milgrom and Stokey’s (1982) no-trade theorem rests on common knowledge too. At a rational expectations equilibrium of any voluntary trading process, they write, it is common knowledge among all agents at the time of trading that the agreed trade is feasible and mutually acceptable, and this suffices to rule out trading based solely on differences in private information, under the theorem’s other premises, among them strict risk aversion and an allocation that no trade could improve before anyone learns anything (Sections 1.6.3 and 4.4).3

21.1.3 Two kinds of higher-order belief

A higher-order belief is a belief about what others believe. This book separates two kinds (our judgment). An inference-based higher-order belief arises when a trader reads others’ information from prices, orders or actions, which needs a view of how they act on what they know; it is the common ingredient of rational expectations (Chapter 16), market makers (Chapter 17) and information cascades (Chapter 14). The book’s map counts this kind as dropping first-order knowledge only where Table 5.1 lists that default, for Andrei and Cujean’s traders, who read others’ information from prices (Box 5.2 and Chapter 19), and in this chapter; it counts learning from prices, orders and actions in Chapters 14, 16 and 17 under the defaults that Table 5.1 gives those chapters, no learning from prices and no observational learning (our judgment). A payoff-based higher-order belief arises when a trader’s payoff depends on what others do, as in Morris and Shin’s models below, and the map counts it under first-order knowledge in Section 12.9, for Pedersen’s short-term traders in Chapter 19 and in this chapter. When the outcome that matters is itself set by what people do, as the peg’s fall is in their 1998 model, such a model also drops the root model’s single exogenous fact, one fact whose true value trading does not touch (our judgment). For models of this kind Table 5.1 records that relaxation in this chapter, where selling pressure can burst a bubble (Section 21.2), and counts the coordination models of Chapters 11 and 12 only under the defaults it gives those chapters. This chapter takes up mainly the second kind.

The two kinds can sit in one model (our judgment). In Abreu and Brunnermeier’s model (Section 21.2.2) the bubble bursts when enough others sell, a payoff-based dependence, and the authors also note that traders infer information from the fact that the bubble still exists, an inference-based one.4

21.1.4 Two ladders of public information

Beside the three steps of processing of Chapters 1, 4 and 10, publicly available, seen and digested information, orders of mutual knowledge make a second ladder, from publicly available information through information seen by everyone and mutual knowledge of order kk to common knowledge (Table 21.1). “Seen” means those who set the price on the first ladder and every person on the second (our judgment).

The two ladders answer different questions (our judgment): news can be seen by everyone and still fall short of common knowledge, as in Aumann’s example, and it can be common knowledge while the price has not yet digested it correctly. When people attend to only part of what is published, public news can fail even to be seen by everyone (our judgment; Chapter 10).5

21.1.5 Global games and a single equilibrium

Morris and Shin’s (1998) currency model is a payoff-based case. A state of fundamentals θ\theta is uniformly distributed between 0 and 1, and without intervention the exchange rate would be f(θ)f(\theta); the government pegs it at e*e^*, at least f(θ)f(\theta) in every state. A speculator may attack by selling one unit of the currency short at a cost tt, earning e*−f(θ)−te^* - f(\theta) - t if the government gives up the peg and losing tt if it defends, while not attacking pays zero. The government’s best course is to give up the peg only if the share of speculators who attack is at least a critical mass a(θ)a(\theta).

When θ\theta is low, defending the peg costs more than it is worth whatever speculators do; when θ\theta is high, not attacking is a dominant action. These two ends are the dominance regions. In between, if all speculators know θ\theta exactly, the region “ripe for attack” has multiple equilibria, because beliefs fulfill themselves.

Now each speculator sees a signal drawn uniformly from [θ−ε,θ+ε][\theta - \varepsilon, \theta + \varepsilon], independent across speculators given θ\theta, with 2ε<min⁡{θ_,1−θ¯}2\varepsilon < \min\{\underline{\theta}, 1 - \overline{\theta}\}, where θ_\underline{\theta} and θ¯\overline{\theta} bound the dominance regions. Their Theorem 1 states that there is a unique θ*\theta^* such that, in any equilibrium, the government gives up the peg if and only if θ≤θ*\theta \le \theta^*. The theorem rests on four premises together (our judgment): attacks are strategic complements, the payoff to attacking rising with the share of others who attack; both dominance regions exist; the prior is uniform with no public signal (Section 12.9); and the private noise is small. The model has no price (our judgment); the government observes the share who attack and θ\theta.

What matters, the authors write, is whether there is common knowledge, and they set the amount of information as such aside. There is nnth-order knowledge that θ≥θ_\theta \ge \underline{\theta}, everyone knowing that everyone knows it nn times, exactly if everyone’s signal is at least θ_+(2n−1)ε\underline{\theta} + (2n - 1)\varepsilon, so for any fixed ε\varepsilon some order fails, and it is never common knowledge that θ\theta lies outside the unstable region.

Their Theorem 2 states that as ε\varepsilon tends to zero, θ*\theta^* solves f(θ*)=e*−2tf(\theta^*) = e^* - 2t, the marginal speculator giving the peg’s fall and its defense equal probability. Heinemann (2000) published a correction of this limit. Box 21.1 derives the limit in general and finds that a(⋅)a(\cdot) enters it and that it agrees with Theorem 2 only when a(θ*)=1/2a(\theta^*) = 1/2 (our derivation). In that limit a shift in a(⋅)a(\cdot) still moves θ*\theta^*, so the paper’s statement that it has no effect fails, and capital controls keep an effect, so its statement that they seem far less effective when fundamentals are transparent loses its basis, while its abstract’s point that the equilibrium depends on the quantity of hot money holds, since hot money works through a(θ)a(\theta), and the limit does not depend on ε\varepsilon (our derivation), so the reading that common knowledge matters stands (our judgment).

In Box 12.2’s version with normal signals, a public signal precise enough relative to the private ones brings several equilibria in threshold strategies back for some values of the public signal (our derivation; Box 12.2); restoring common knowledge and selecting the good equilibrium are two steps (Section 12.9), and without both dominance regions the selection has nothing to start from (our judgment). Abreu and Brunnermeier note that the global games approach does not apply to their model, which lacks strategic complementarity because only some arbitrageurs can sell before the crash and traders infer information from the bubble’s survival.6

21.1.6 Finite-order knowledge and strong bubbles

A strong bubble (Section 21.2.1) is a price that everyone knows to be above any possible dividend stream. Brunnermeier (2001) reports a result of Morris, Postlewaite and Shin (1995), in the setting of Allen, Morris and Postlewaite (1993) with all traders risk neutral. Where it is mutual knowledge that the final dividend, paid at date TT, is zero, any strictly positive price is a strong bubble; if that is (T−t)(T - t)th-order mutual knowledge, a strong bubble can be ruled out from period tt onwards, so a bubble can exist at or after tt only if the true value is not (T−t)(T - t)th-order mutual knowledge at tt (as reported by Brunnermeier). We have not read their paper.

Both statements rule a strong bubble out and neither shows that one exists: mutual knowledge up to the number of trading rounds left suffices to rule it out, short of common knowledge, while with information seen by everyone a strong bubble is not ruled out as long as more than one round is left, since the definition already has everyone knowing that the price is too high (our judgment). Allen, Morris and Postlewaite give four examples of strong bubbles, with at least three traders, at least three periods left and prices that are not fully revealing (as reported by Brunnermeier).7

21.1.7 Public information that lowers welfare

Section 12.10 gave Morris and Shin’s (2002) result for coordination, one of the four ways of Section 4.7 in which public information can lower welfare (working-paper version); here are its three conditions, with Svensson’s (2006) reading, both read in drafts.

The first condition is that the coordination motive enters each person’s payoff and stays out of social welfare. The second term of the payoff is a zero-sum game of guessing others’ actions, Keynes’s beauty contest, and welfare, the average payoff rescaled, is minus the mean squared distance of actions from the fundamental θ\theta, so it depends only on the part of the payoff that concerns the fundamental (working-paper version). The second is the information structure: the public signal’s realization is common knowledge; each private signal is θ\theta plus normal noise independent across agents and unobservable to others; and θ\theta is drawn from an improper uniform prior over the real line, with normal noise in the public signal (working-paper version). The third is that private information is precise enough. With α\alpha the public signal’s precision, the inverse of its noise variance, β\beta each private signal’s and rr the weight on the coordination motive, welfare falls as α\alpha rises if and only if α/β<(2r−1)(1−r)\alpha/\beta < (2r - 1)(1 - r) (working-paper version). For r≤1/2r \le 1/2 the condition always fails, and its right side is largest at r=3/4r = 3/4, where it is 1/8, so private signals must be more than eight times as precise as the public one (working-paper version).

In equilibrium agents give the public signal’s noise relatively more weight and the private signal’s relatively less, which Morris and Shin attribute to the coordination motive, and welfare always rises with the precision of private signals (working-paper version); Svensson’s reading changes the judgment of welfare and leaves this prediction about behavior as it was (our judgment).

Figure 21.1 redraws the welfare curve of Svensson’s Figure 2, with β\beta held at one (working-paper version). At r=3/4r = 3/4 welfare falls while α/β<1/8\alpha/\beta < 1/8, is lowest at 1/8 and rises after; with no public signal it is −1/β-1/\beta, and it is back at that level when α=β(2r−1)\alpha = \beta(2r - 1), so at r=3/4r = 3/4 public information beats none once α/β>1/2\alpha/\beta > 1/2 (working-paper version). Between 1/8 and 1/2 a little more public precision raises welfare, yet welfare stays below its level with no public signal (our derivation), so helping at the margin and beating no public information are two different things (our judgment).

Svensson judges it extremely unlikely that central-bank information is more than eight times as noisy as an individual agent’s private information and concludes that for reasonable parameters welfare increases with transparency in Morris and Shin’s model (working-paper version). That is his judgment; it compares central-bank noise with one agent’s private noise, as the model’s condition does, while how a trader’s private information maps onto β\beta in a market is a separate question, and carrying a model without a price to a market needs a model of how prices form (our judgment; Section 12.10).8

21.1.8 The guessing game and steps of thinking

Section 1.2.1 gave Bühren and Frank’s guessing game among chess players (working-paper version) and the cognitive hierarchy model. The game is payoff-based, since to win a player must guess what the others will guess (our judgment). Camerer, Ho and Chong’s Poisson cognitive hierarchy model, they write, does not impose common knowledge of rationality, so it contradicts the no-trade theorem, which they call the Groucho Marx theorem, and predicts that speculation will occur even when hedging and spectator fun do not matter. The Nash answer of 0 corresponds to rationality being common knowledge, and thinking a few steps ahead is reasoning about others cut off at a finite order; steps of thinking count rounds of reasoning and orders of mutual knowledge count layers of knowing, two different quantities (our judgment).9

21.2 Bubbles and coordination

Abreu and Brunnermeier’s (2003) bridge model changes several of the root model’s defaults, in this book’s reading (our judgment). A window of awareness replaces the one-time injection of news; selling pressure that can burst the bubble drops the single exogenous fact; a mispricing that is never common knowledge drops first-order knowledge; capped positions drop frictionless trading; and arbitrageurs who infer information from the bubble’s survival drop no learning from prices together with the others. Outside the relaxations that Table 5.1 lists for this chapter, the arbitrageurs are risk neutral in place of log utility, and the authors write that the all-or-nothing positions which risk neutrality and fixed transaction costs give are not essential for their main results, risk-averse arbitrageurs leaving the market gradually; the behavioral traders’ fixed belief that the price will grow at gg forever takes the place of Bayesian updating for them (our judgment).

21.2.1 Three definitions of a bubble

The word bubble has three definitions in this chapter’s sources, each with its own benchmark (our judgment; Table 21.2). In the first, agents pay more than their own valuation of future dividends because they believe they will later find a buyer willing to pay even more, the resale option of Section 20.1.3. In the second, which Brunnermeier reports from Allen, Morris and Postlewaite (1993), an expected bubble occurs when at some date and state the price is higher than every agent’s marginal valuation of the asset, the fundamental value being the valuation of a buy-and-hold strategy that rules out any resale (as reported by Brunnermeier). In the third, a strong bubble occurs if in some state all agents know that the price is higher than the value of any possible dividend stream (as reported by Brunnermeier). Abreu and Brunnermeier (2002) give the original definition as situations in which all traders know with probability one that the price is too high (as reported by Abreu and Brunnermeier), and add that, as Allen, Morris and Postlewaite stress, a bubble’s existence then cannot be commonly known among traders. Strong bubbles can occur only if each trader has private information, and not with fewer than three traders (as reported by Brunnermeier).

Brunnermeier also reports that Tirole (1982) shows that in a dynamic rational expectations equilibrium bubbles cannot occur if the initial allocation is ex ante Pareto efficient, so interim Pareto inefficiency is the first necessary condition for expected bubbles (as reported by Brunnermeier); an allocation is Pareto efficient when no trade can make someone better off without making another worse off, ex ante before traders get private information and interim after. Section 20.1.5 gives the short-sale constraints that an expected bubble also needs.

Abreu and Brunnermeier (2003) use the word in a working sense too: they call any mispricing that persists beyond t0+ηκt_0 + \eta\kappa a bubble, the time from which enough arbitrageurs know of it to correct it together (Section 21.2.2 gives the symbols).10

21.2.2 Arbitrageurs riding a bubble

Abreu and Brunnermeier’s (2003) model gives the opening story a form. From time 0 the stock price grows at a rate gg greater than the interest rate rr, pt=egtp_t = e^{gt}. At a random time t0t_0, exponentially distributed with rate λ\lambda, the price stops being justified: from then on only a fraction 1−β(⋅)1 - \beta(\cdot) rests on fundamentals, and β(⋅)\beta(\cdot) is the bubble component; this β\beta and this rr are unrelated to Morris and Shin’s. “Irrationally exuberant” behavioral traders, who believe in a “new economy paradigm” and think the price will grow at gg forever, keep it above its fundamental value. Rational arbitrageurs become aware of the mispricing one after another, a new cohort of mass 1/η1/\eta in each instant from t0t_0 to t0+ηt_0 + \eta, the awareness window, and none knows whether she learned early or late. When their cumulative selling exceeds κ\kappa, the behavioral traders’ absorption capacity, the price drops by the fraction β(⋅)\beta(\cdot); if selling never exceeds κ\kappa, the bubble bursts for exogenous reasons when it reaches its maximum size β‾\bar{\beta}. A condition on the parameters keeps arbitrageurs from selling before they are aware (Box 21.2); when, for instance, λ\lambda is very high or g−rg - r very small, the authors write, arbitrageurs sell out right away.

Delay comes from four features together, on parameters that meet that condition (our judgment). First, the mispricing is not common knowledge: before the burst it is never common knowledge even among a mass κ\kappa of arbitrageurs that a bubble exists; the usual backward induction against bubbles begins at a terminal date that is common knowledge, and the companion paper adds that in its setting backward induction has no “commonly known starting point”. Second, selling pressure bursts the bubble only when a sufficient mass of arbitrageurs have sold out, yet at most a fraction κ<1\kappa < 1 of them can leave before the crash, so coordination and competition sit together. Third, financial constraints limit each arbitrageur’s position, while their resources taken together suffice to correct the price. Fourth, the price does not reveal t0t_0: under the authors’ assumption on the burst, arbitrageurs do not become aware of others’ selling until it crosses κ\kappa, and the authors add that a nonstochastic price strictly monotonic in selling pressure would reveal t0t_0 at once. The authors expect their principal results to hold qualitatively in a fuller model with noisy prices, without proving it (our judgment; Section 1.6.3); the companion paper calls its critical mass a reduced form for the many uncertainties that make it hard to infer every variable from the price.

The four together give delay, and the paper does not show that each is needed; symmetric information removes the first, in this book’s reading, and without the third a single arbitrageur could correct the price alone (our judgment). With symmetric information, the authors write, the wish to sell first in their dynamic model gives a unique equilibrium, and as the asymmetry of information goes to zero the outcome converges to the symmetric one, with no bubble.

The bubble bursts in one of two ways. Under the condition of their Proposition 2 (Box 21.2), a unique trading equilibrium has every arbitrageur sell a fixed time τ1\tau^1 after becoming aware, yet for every t0t_0 the bubble bursts for exogenous reasons exactly when it reaches its maximum size, and the arbitrageurs’ selling has no influence on when it bursts. Under the reverse condition, their Proposition 3, arbitrageurs who become aware at ti≥ηκt_i \ge \eta\kappa sell a time τ*\tau^* later and earlier ones at ηκ+τ*\eta\kappa + \tau^*, and the bubble bursts when it is a fraction β*\beta^* of the pre-crash price, which suitable parameters can make arbitrarily close to β‾\bar{\beta}.

The companion paper’s Proposition 2 states that the mispricing is never common knowledge among a positive mass of arbitrageurs, and its proof climbs the ladder of Section 21.1.4: at t0+ηt_0 + \eta everybody knows of the mispricing, but only the first trader knows that everybody knows; at t0+2ηt_0 + 2\eta even the last to learn know that everybody knows, yet they do not know that everybody knows that everybody knows; and as time passes the order of mutual knowledge rises without ever reaching infinity. That paper’s arbitrageurs pay a holding cost (Section 20.1.6).

Knowing, acting and the price come apart here. In Proposition 3’s equilibrium, when t0≥ηκt_0 \ge \eta\kappa, the share who have sold is the share aware shifted later by τ*\tau^* (our derivation; Box 21.2), and the price stays on its path until that share reaches κ\kappa and then drops at once, a step function of the share acting, one of the rules of Section 5.3 for how the price responds to action, which answers the rows “Strategic timing” and “How the price responds to action” of Table 19.3 (our judgment). For EntreMed (Section 1.6.3), an untested candidate, the four features are the questions to ask (our judgment).11

21.2.3 Awareness on a fixed timetable

The authors fix the spreading side of the model from outside (our judgment): awareness arrives at a uniform, deterministic rate over the window, with no word of mouth and no slowing as those who know grow in number, and in the base model the only randomness is the start t0t_0. They add that one can also view sequential awareness as a metaphor for asymmetric information, differences in viewpoints and other factors that show up in the model as miscoordination in time. Hong and Stein’s newswatchers also learn on a fixed timetable, each piece of news reaching one more group per period until all have it (Section 20.2.1; working-paper version). Neither timetable is the root model’s S-curve, and each of these bridges goes only halfway (our judgment).

Against the root model, the uniform window is word of mouth switched off and an external source that rises over the window (our derivation; Box 21.3). The closed forms also rely on a feature of the uniform window with an exponential start: a posterior over t0t_0 whose shape, for ti≥ηt_i \ge \eta, does not depend on when an arbitrageur learned (our derivation; Box 21.2), a step that another awareness process would need redone (our judgment).

Whether the delay from synchronization survives word of mouth, as in the Bass model or an SI epidemic, is one form of the book’s open problem (Section 1.7; Chapter 19), which the paper does not take up; Box 21.3 states the question, with the companion paper’s condition for a decreasing absorption capacity and an analog of it on an S-curve that their proof does not cover, and gives no answer (our derivation).12

21.2.4 Delay and its boundary

Abreu and Brunnermeier’s (2002) Proposition 3 gives the comparative statics of their model: the mispricing lasts longer the longer the awareness window η\eta, the larger the initial absorption capacity κ0\kappa_0 and the higher the holding cost cc, and shorter the higher the arrival rate λ\lambda and the larger the mispricing β\beta. When the mispricing exceeds bounds set by the dispersion of opinion among arbitrageurs and other parameters, arbitrageurs act on their information without delay. Their main result is that synchronization risk combined with holding costs typically makes arbitrageurs delay acting on their information; they do trade in the end, but arbitrage can be significantly delayed.

Neither paper measures the window, the absorption capacity or the time limits, or gives parameter values, so the papers give no way to estimate the length of a delay from measured quantities, and one can test only ordered comparisons such as the boundary (our judgment). A candidate test, this book’s and untested: set the size of a mispricing against its explicit holding cost and compare cases with a large ratio and a small one, other things alike; Proposition 3’s comparative statics point to correction close behind awareness when the ratio is large and well behind it when the ratio is small, a comparison that gives no magnitude, and the timing of first analyst coverage, of short sellers’ reports or of search volume might stand in for how fast awareness spreads (our judgment).13

21.2.5 Silence, triggers and avalanches

Lee (1998) gives a second way in which knowing need not move the price. Trading is sequential: in each round a new agent arrives, while those who arrived earlier remain active and may readjust their positions. A market maker posts the price and trades against any order at it, setting each round’s price from all the information in the history of orders. Transaction costs may prevent many traders from revealing their private information, so hidden information accumulates and may be released by a small trigger, with high volatility and no accompanying event (Sections 16.6 and 20.1.9). Lee’s information cascade, unlike Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer and Welch’s, can be partial: once public belief leans one way, traders with mild signals stay out, and their information is hidden as silence (our judgment). An information avalanche, in Lee’s definition, takes place when agents who arrived earlier and have not yet traded place orders. A trader pays the transaction cost only once, at the first trade, whatever the order’s size, which drops frictionless trading; each agent decides on his own information and the history of earlier agents’ decisions, with the whole price history in his information set, so the model also drops no observational learning and no learning from prices (our judgment).

Lee traces crashes through four phases, boom, euphoria, trigger and panic, the euphoria corresponding to the information cascade and the panic to the avalanche. In the euphoria, agents with signals H or L arrive one after another and place no orders. In the trigger an agent with the signal R places a sell order, and in the panic the agents who arrived during the euphoria place orders; if they are mostly agents with signal L selling, a crash occurs. The example assumes H and L frequent and R rare in both states, and Lee calls his argument for the four phases heuristic.

After an avalanche, Lee writes, the price is likely to be closer to the correct one; the correction is imperfect, since with strictly positive probability the price fails to reflect all the information in the market even in the long run. Lee adds his own caveat: the model executes a possibly large number of trades at a single price in a single period during an avalanche, with an unchanged transaction cost, while in reality the price may adjust after part of the orders and the bid–ask spread may widen, so the price movement could be smaller than the model predicts. Brunnermeier likewise writes that the avalanches hinge on the market maker’s being unable to adjust his quote within a trading round.

Abreu and Brunnermeier’s model and Lee’s give two mechanisms by which people know and the price does not move (our judgment): strategic timing, waiting because the mispricing is not common knowledge and arbitrageurs must sell together yet each wants to sell first, and a friction threshold, mild news that does not cover the trading cost, even though the price adjusts to the history of orders. Both sit between knowing and acting in Table 19.3, beside a belief threshold (Chapter 11), waiting to learn from those who move first (Chapter 14) and an attention cycle (Section 19.3.3).14

21.2.7 Four kinds of crash

Brunnermeier groups theoretical models of crashes into four categories: liquidity shortage, multiple equilibria and sunspots, bursting bubbles, and lumpy information aggregation. Each category, he writes, can explain crashes even when all agents act rationally, and they differ in the price path after the crash, which may be a correction, with the market low for a long time, or an immediate bounce. A crash driven purely by liquidity shortage is temporary, so he would expect a fast recovery. A sunspot may signal a shift from a high-price equilibrium to a low one. A move between multiple equilibria need not come with a sunspot, he adds, and Gennotte and Leland (1990) give a crash without one: as the parameter values change slightly, the high-price equilibrium vanishes and the economy jumps to the low-price one, where the price can stay even when supply returns to its old level (as reported by Brunnermeier); he doubts whether this jump in a static setup would arise in a fully dynamic model. In a bursting bubble the price exceeds fundamental value and all participants know it, yet it is not common knowledge; the crash is a correction, after which he would not expect a rebound. Under lumpy information aggregation the economy may be in a partial cascade until a small event shatters it; such a crash often follows a steady rise in prices and corrects the mispricing, so he expects no fast recovery.

This book takes “does the price rebound after the crash?” as a first test (our judgment). A fast rebound points to a liquidity shortage, though Brunnermeier’s overview states no path for the multiple-equilibria class, so a rebound does not rule that class out; a price that stays low points to one of the other kinds, which need further tests, whether an identifiable sunspot or a small change in conditions came first, and whether the overpricing was mutually known beforehand, which separates a bursting bubble from lumpy information aggregation, since a rise can come before either (our judgment). The test cannot separate everything. Abreu and Brunnermeier extend their model with random temporary price drops, possibly from mood changes of the behavioral traders, to show how a large decline can turn into a crash or a rebound, in what they call a simple and highly stylized model; if the bubble survives the next round, the behavioral traders regain their confidence. In the equilibrium of their Proposition 5, which they note is not necessarily the one in which the bubble bursts earliest, a price drop not followed by a crash leads to a rebound and strengthens the bubble for a while, so a fall followed by a recovery can occur in a bubble model too (our judgment).16

21.2.8 Coordination at GameStop

Section 11.6 gave Lucchini and coauthors’ study of the GameStop squeeze on Reddit, a single event, and four readings of it. The authors define a user’s commitment as an exhibited proof that the user has financial stakes in the asset and note a widely shared norm on the forum of providing proof of one’s own position when starting a discussion about investments. Showing positions in public turns who has already bought from private information into a fact that anyone reading the forum can see, a step toward common knowledge, and since each buyer stood to gain more the more others bought (Section 11.6), commitment posts could serve to coordinate buying; the paper neither models nor tests coordination (our judgment; Section 11.6).17

21.2.9 Coordinating sellers and persuading holders

Public information can correct a mispricing in two ways, with different premises (our judgment). It can coordinate those who would sell, an announcement serving as a coordination device among arbitrageurs only where limits to arbitrage are not too tight (our judgment; Section 12.9). Or it can persuade those who hold: after Ljungqvist and Qian’s short sellers published their reports, the authors see no increase in shorting, the short-sale constraints on the targets being quite severe, and in their reading the reports apparently reached the targets’ shareholders, whose selling brought the price falls (working-paper version; Sections 1.6.3, 12.9 and 20.1.11). Where short sales are tightly constrained, correcting an overpricing rests on persuading holders to sell; where they are not, coordinating short sellers is a second route (our judgment).18

21.4 A trader’s checklist

None of these questions says what to buy or sell, and good answers do not make a trade safe (our judgment).

  1. Who knows that the others know? Check: news seen by everyone can fall short of common knowledge, as in Aumann’s example, and an outcome that needs many people to act at once turns on what they know of each other’s knowledge (our judgment; Sections 21.1.1 and 21.1.4).
  2. Is there one equilibrium? Check: in Morris and Shin’s (1998) currency model, with attacks that are strategic complements, both dominance regions, a uniform prior with no public signal and small independent private noise, the outcome turns on whether the fundamental lies below a single threshold (Section 21.1.5); in a normal version a public signal precise enough relative to the private ones brings several threshold equilibria back for some values of the public signal (our derivation; Box 12.2).
  3. Do those who know need each other? Check: in Abreu and Brunnermeier’s (2003) model, with awareness arriving from outside one arbitrageur after another at a fixed, uniform rate and parameters that meet the authors’ condition, correction waits when the mispricing is not common knowledge, a critical mass must sell yet only some can sell first, positions are capped and the price does not reveal when the mispricing began, all four together; in their 2002 model, the ratio of a mispricing’s size to its holding cost is an untested candidate test of the boundary (our judgment; Sections 21.2.2 to 21.2.4).
  4. Is silence hiding information? Check: in Lee’s heuristic example, with fixed trading costs, sequential trading, a market maker who keeps one price within a round and a signal rare in both states, mild news may stay out of the price and come out at once after the rare signal, though Lee writes that a real avalanche could move the price less (Section 21.2.5).
  5. Did the price come back? Check: in Brunnermeier’s grouping a fast rebound points to a liquidity shortage, though his overview states no path for the multiple-equilibria class, and a price that stays low to one of the other kinds, while a temporary drop not followed by a crash also rebounds in an equilibrium of Abreu and Brunnermeier’s stylized extension (our judgment; Section 21.2.7).

21.5 Summary

  • Common knowledge means that everyone knows, everyone knows that everyone knows, and so on without end; in Aumann’s four-state example with a common prior, two people who each know the other’s posterior still hold different posteriors (Section 21.1.1).
  • Orders of mutual knowledge, from publicly available information through information seen by everyone and mutual knowledge of order kk to common knowledge, form a ladder apart from publicly available, seen and digested information, and the two ladders answer different questions (our judgment; Section 21.1.4).
  • In Morris and Shin’s (1998) currency model, with strategic complements, both dominance regions, a uniform prior without a public signal and small independent private noise, the unique equilibrium’s threshold, as the noise vanishes, depends on the critical mass a(⋅)a(\cdot), and so on hot money, and agrees with the paper’s Theorem 2 only where a(θ*)=1/2a(\theta^*) = 1/2 (our derivation; Section 21.1.5 and Box 21.1).
  • In Morris and Shin’s (2002) model, with a coordination motive that is zero-sum for society, a public signal whose realization is common knowledge, independent normal private signals and an improper uniform prior with normal public noise, more precise public information lowers welfare if and only if α/β<(2r−1)(1−r)\alpha/\beta < (2r - 1)(1 - r), at most 1/8, which Svensson judges unlikely for reasonable parameters (working-paper version); the model has no price, and how a trader’s private information maps onto β\beta in a market is a separate question (our judgment; Section 21.1.7 and Figure 21.1).
  • In Abreu and Brunnermeier’s (2003) model, with awareness arriving from outside at a fixed, uniform rate, parameters that meet the authors’ condition, a mispricing never common knowledge, a critical mass that must sell while only some can sell first, capped positions and a price that does not reveal when the mispricing began, a bubble can persist after enough arbitrageurs know of it to correct it (Section 21.2.2); whether the delay survives word of mouth is open (our judgment; Section 21.2.3).
  • In Lee’s (1998) heuristic example, with fixed trading costs, sequential trading, a market maker who keeps one price within a round and a signal rare in both states, mild information can stay hidden as silence and come out at once in an avalanche after the rare signal, which Lee writes could be smaller in reality (Section 21.2.5).
  • By Brunnermeier’s four kinds of crash, a fast rebound points to a liquidity shortage and a price that stays low to one of the others, a first test that cannot separate everything, since his overview states no path for the multiple-equilibria class and a temporary drop in an equilibrium of Abreu and Brunnermeier’s stylized model can also rebound (our judgment; Section 21.2.7).
