Chapter 2 Measuring Information

From 1984 to 1999, on days when the Wall Street Journal’s daily stock market column read gloomier than usual, the Dow Jones Industrial Average tended to fall the next day, and within a week it had made up the fall, by Paul Tetlock’s estimates (working-paper version). Whether the column carried information, and how much, depends on what the information is about and how it is measured. This chapter gives the book’s measures, in bits: entropy, mutual information, conditional mutual information and relative entropy. It keeps three pairs apart. One is the average over all the messages a source might send and the one message that has arrived; another is information about the state of the world, the fact a trader wants to know, and information about prices; the third is an amount of information and its value.

2.1 A gloomy column and the next day

The Wall Street Journal’s “Abreast of the Market” column was written immediately after the closing bell, and in Tetlock’s words it read “like a post-mortem of the market’s life on the prior day” (working-paper version). He judges it unlikely to hold anything that the specialists on the exchange floor, the dealers in charge of trading each stock, did not know at the close (working-paper version).1

Tetlock (2007) counted the words of each day’s column with the General Inquirer, a program that sorts words into categories, over the 16 years 1984 to 1999 (working-paper version). From the counts he built one measure of pessimism with a standard statistical method; it is roughly a weighted sum of four of the 77 categories, Negative, Weak, Fail and Fall (working-paper version).

His main series is the Dow Jones Industrial Average, an index of large US stocks on which the column focuses, with daily returns from January 1, 1984, to September 17, 1999 (working-paper version). In his regressions, a rise in pessimism of one standard deviation, its typical swing, predicts a Dow return 8.1 basis points, hundredths of a percent, lower the next day, more than the unconditional mean, the plain average, of the Dow’s daily returns, 5.4 basis points, with a t-statistic of 3.94, a value chance alone would rarely give (working-paper version). Daily moves in the Dow, Tetlock notes, are very large relative to its average return, so the effect is small beside a typical day’s move (our judgment), though Tetlock calls it economically significant (working-paper version). Over the following four trading days the index made up 6.8 basis points, significant at the 5% level, and the five-day sum, −1.3 basis points, is not significantly different from zero (working-paper version). Tetlock concludes that the effect is “fully reversed within a week”, a reversal being a move back the other way; on small stocks the effect seems larger and longer-lasting (working-paper version). He also reports the trading volume of the New York Stock Exchange, which unusually high or low pessimism predicted to be high the next day (working-paper version).

Tetlock weighs two readings in which the column is information. A fall caused by new information about fundamentals, the facts that decide what a company is worth, would not be followed by a complete reversal, and information already in prices would not move returns significantly; the evidence fits neither (working-paper version). It fits “temporary downward price pressure caused by pessimistic investor sentiment”, sentiment being optimism or pessimism unconnected with news about fundamentals, or, as his conclusion allows, risk aversion (working-paper version; Section 2.5). In his conclusion, the idea that pessimism stands for bad fundamental news not yet in prices “receives very little support from the data” (working-paper version).

The study covers one column, mainly one index, and one country, and its sample ends in 1999, so it says nothing directly about markets since (our judgment). Estimates for the 1990s appear to dominate the full sample, though Tetlock cannot rule out a qualitatively similar effect in the earlier period (working-paper version). He allows that the column may hold some information to which traders over- or underreact (working-paper version). His pessimism-based strategy would have earned 4.4 basis points a day on average from 1985 to 1999 (working-paper version), an edge that trading costs may exceed and whose profit after costs is unclear.

2.2 Surprise and entropy

Information theory measures uncertainty and information in bits. The unit comes from the base of the logarithm: with base 2 the units are binary digits, or bits, a name suggested by J. W. Tukey. One bit halves the possibilities, as the answer to a yes-or-no question with two equally likely answers does.

An outcome that had probability pp carries log⁡2(1/p)\log_2 (1/p) bits when it happens; we call this its surprise.2 The rarer the outcome, the larger the surprise.

Entropy is the average surprise. Cover and Thomas introduce it as a measure of the uncertainty of a random variable, a quantity whose value is unknown until it is observed, and define it for a variable XX that takes the value xx with probability p(x)p(x) as H(X)=−∑xp(x)log⁡2p(x),H(X) = -\sum_x p(x) \log_2 p(x), the expected value of log⁡2[1/p(X)]\log_2 [1/p(X)]. With base 2, entropy is in bits, and a fair coin toss has an entropy of 1 bit. Shannon (1948) treated quantities of this form as measures “of information, choice and uncertainty”.

Entropy is zero only when the outcome is certain, and among nn possible outcomes it is largest, log⁡2n\log_2 n, when all are equally likely. A fair die has an entropy of log⁡26\log_2 6, about 2.585 bits (our calculation). Entropy also counts questions. A variable with four values, of probabilities 1/2, 1/4, 1/8 and 1/8, has an entropy of 7/4 bits, and it can be found with 1.75 yes-or-no questions on average, the fewest possible. In general the fewest lies between HH and H+1H + 1.

Chapter 1’s contract pays $1 if a new drug passes its final round of trials and trades at $0.30, so the price-implied probability of success, the probability the price assigns (Section 1.3 gives its limits), is 30%. With that probability, success has a surprise of log⁡2(1/0.3)\log_2 (1/0.3), about 1.737 bits, and failure one of about 0.515 bits; weighted by their chances, they give an entropy of about 0.881 bits (our calculation), less than the 1 bit of a fair coin because one outcome is likelier.

Entropy is computed from the probabilities alone; the values the variable takes, a drug’s sales or a contract’s payoff, do not enter it. A contract paying $1 and one paying $1 million on the same 30% chance have the same entropy (our judgment). An amount of information, counted in bits, therefore does not by itself fix what the information is worth, a point taken up in Section 2.6 and in Chapter 4. Shannon noted that HH has the form of entropy in certain formulations of statistical mechanics, the physics of systems of many particles; Section 2.8 gives the physical readings.

2.3 Mutual information

Mutual information measures how much one quantity tells about another. Cover and Thomas describe it as the fall in uncertainty about one random variable that comes from knowing the other. Write θ\theta for the state of the world, the fact to be known, here whether the drug succeeds, and YY for a signal about it. The uncertainty left about θ\theta once YY is seen, averaged over what YY might say, is the conditional entropy H(θ∣Y)H(\theta \mid Y), and the mutual information is what the signal removes: I(θ;Y)=H(θ)−H(θ∣Y).I(\theta; Y) = H(\theta) - H(\theta \mid Y). Box 2.1, in Section 2.12, gives the definitions. Shannon (1948) wrote the same difference as the rate of actual transmission over a noisy channel, a link that garbles some of what it carries. Kelly (1956), whose betting rule Section 1.4 used, showed that the same quantity is the highest rate at which a gambler betting on the channel’s messages can make capital grow, if the odds leave the bookmaker no margin and match the true chances, which Kelly called “fair” odds.3

Mutual information is symmetric: “XX says as much about YY as YY says about XX”. It is never negative, and it is zero only when the two are independent. Figure 2.1 draws these quantities after Cover and Thomas’s Venn diagram, in which mutual information is the overlap.

Suppose a report on the drug says “success” or “failure” and is right 80% of the time, whatever the outcome. It says “success” with probability 0.38, so its own entropy H(Y)H(Y) is about 0.958 bits, and since it errs one time in five either way, H(Y∣θ)H(Y \mid \theta) is about 0.722 bits. After the report, the uncertainty left about the drug is on average H(θ∣Y)H(\theta \mid Y), about 0.645 bits, against 0.881 before, so the report carries I(θ;Y)I(\theta; Y) of about 0.236 bits (our calculation). Box 1.1 reached the same 0.236 bits for this example by another route.

What a signal adds to what one already knows is the conditional mutual information: how much knowing YY reduces the uncertainty about XX when ZZ is already given. The book’s amount of information is a quantity of this kind, measured against what is already known; for the market, against what the price-setters have seen (Chapter 1). A second copy of the same report adds 0 bits, since once the first is known the copy removes no uncertainty (our derivation). A second report that is also right 80% of the time, but errs independently of the first, adds about 0.162 bits, less than the first one’s 0.236, because both bear on the same outcome and the first has already removed part of the doubt the second would remove; together the two carry about 0.398 bits (our calculation). Knowing something can also raise what a signal tells. Two independent fair coins tell nothing about each other, but once their sum is known, either one gives half a bit about the other.4 A signal useless on its own may be useful next to what a trader already holds (our judgment). Section 1.4 read mutual information as a signal’s value, but only under strict conditions; here it is an amount.

2.4 One message and the average

Mutual information averages over every message a signal might send, weighted by how often each is sent. A trader receives one. Cover and Thomas note that “specific new evidence might increase uncertainty, but on the average evidence decreases uncertainty”. The drug report shows both (Table 2.1). “Success” raises the chance of success from 0.30 to about 0.632 and the entropy from 0.881 to about 0.949 bits, while “failure” lowers both (our calculation). The entropy falls by 0.236 bits on average, yet rises after “success”, because 0.632 is nearer an even chance than 0.30 is.

Relative entropy, met in Chapter 1 as the cost of acting on a wrong distribution, measures one message instead. The relative entropy of the posterior, the probabilities after the message, from the prior, those before it, is never negative, and its average over the messages is the mutual information. It is about 0.337 bits after “success” and 0.174 after “failure”, and its average is 0.236 bits (our calculation).5

In statistics, relative entropy arises as an expected logarithm of a likelihood ratio. A related ratio sets how far one message moves the odds, the chance of success divided by that of failure, 3 to 7 for the drug. It is the message’s likelihood ratio, how much likelier the message is if the drug succeeds than if it fails. By Bayes’ rule for updating a probability on new evidence, the logarithm of the odds, the log-odds, after the message is the log-odds before it plus the logarithm of the likelihood ratio (our derivation): log⁡2py1−py=log⁡2π1−π+log⁡2P(y∣success)P(y∣failure),\log_2 \frac{p_y}{1 - p_y} = \log_2 \frac{\pi}{1 - \pi} + \log_2 \frac{P(y \mid \text{success})}{P(y \mid \text{failure})}, with π\pi the prior probability of success and pyp_y the posterior after message yy. The last term, the log-likelihood ratio, is log⁡2(0.8/0.2)=+2\log_2 (0.8/0.2) = +2 bits for the report’s “success” and −2-2 bits for its “failure”, and each bit doubles or halves the odds. One “success” takes 3 to 7 to 12 to 7, a probability of about 0.632; two independent ones add up to 4 bits and about 0.873, three to 6 bits and about 0.965 (our calculation).

Duffie and Manso (2007) build information percolation, a model in which people meet at random and pool what they know, on this sum; Box 2.1 gives their form of it.6 A report copied from another is not independent of it, and adding both counts the same evidence twice (our judgment).

Suppose, in Chapter 1’s terms, that the price of 0.30 is the correct probability given what the price-setters have seen. A trader who alone hears “success” then believes 0.632, a belief gap, her belief minus the price, of about 0.33 (our calculation). Judged by her own belief, betting in line with it grows her capital at D(p∥q)D(p \| q), the relative entropy of this one message (Box 1.1), about 0.337 bits per bet on a logarithmic scale, a factor of about 1.26 a bet on a geometric average (our calculation). What she actually earns depends on the true chance, which she cannot see (Section 1.4). Averaged over both messages, the figure is 0.236 bits (our calculation), the first term of the information gradient, the information the price has not yet reflected (Box 1.1).

2.5 Information about the world and about prices

Shannon noted that messages frequently have meaning, but “these semantic aspects of communication are irrelevant to the engineering problem”. Mutual information can be computed between a signal and any quantity one chooses, so how much information a signal carries depends first on what it is about (our judgment). This book measures information about the state of the world θ\theta unless it says otherwise. The bounds on the value of information in Section 1.4 take another object, the returns themselves, the ratios of the stocks’ prices at the end of a period to those at the start.7

On Tetlock’s reading, the column he studies is unlikely to hold anything the specialists did not know at the close, much of the response to any true information in it has probably come before it is written, and the data give very little support to its being fundamental news not yet in prices (working-paper version). Its pessimism still predicted, from 1984 to 1999, the next day’s return and trading volume (working-paper version; Section 2.1). In the book’s terms, the column carries information about prices, since its words help predict them, and, on Tetlock’s reading, almost no new information about θ\theta (our judgment). Whether news media cause, amplify or simply reflect how investors read the market, Tetlock calls unclear (working-paper version). The measure’s last five days explain 1.52% of what Tetlock calls the residual variation in Dow returns (working-paper version), and a fit within the sample may overstate what could have been forecast in advance (our judgment).8 That share is not a mutual information (our judgment), so the chapter gives no figure in bits for it. Tetlock finds the long-run reaction consistent with market efficiency on the assumption that the column holds no relevant information about fundamentals, and allows that it may hold some information to which traders over- or underreact (working-paper version).

Trade for reasons other than information, such as liquidity needs, selling to raise cash, or sudden changes in risk aversion, a preference for steadier outcomes, predicts the same as sentiment does (working-paper version). Telling the two apart, Tetlock writes, requires reading pessimism as a proxy for either investor sentiment or risk aversion, a debate he calls more philosophical than economic and leaves to the reader, and his conclusion speaks of a measure that “appears to correspond to either negative investor sentiment or risk aversion” (working-paper version). On the first reading the column tells about demand unconnected with θ\theta, on the second about the price of risk, and on neither about θ\theta (our judgment). Chapters 1–5 assume that the fact traded on is unrelated to aggregate risk, the risk of the market as a whole, so that a price can differ from the correct probability only through mistakes, with no reward for bearing risk. The Dow moves with the market as a whole, so here the assumption fails, and part of a fall may be a higher reward for bearing risk with no change in anyone’s beliefs (our judgment). We use the column only to tell the two objects apart (our judgment). Chapter 4 takes up value from trading on things other than θ\theta, Chapter 13 sentiment, and Chapter 16 prices that mix sentiment with information.

2.6 Amount and value

An amount of information is computed from probabilities alone (Section 2.2), so it does not by itself say what the information is worth. For a stock market, Barron and Cover (1988) note that wealth depends on the values the returns take, and information on the returns and the signal only through their joint probabilities.

Barron and Cover give an example, cited in Section 1.4 beside their general bound, in which a full bit is worth nothing. Of two stocks, the first always keeps its price, and the second falls to half or to three quarters of it, with equal chances; a signal says which. The first stock does better either way, so the best portfolio holds only the first, with the signal or without it. The signal carries 1 bit, and the growth rate is zero with it and without it (our derivation), so the bit adds nothing. The example allows only portfolios with no short positions that invest all the wealth, judged by the growth rate of wealth on a logarithmic scale. In a trader’s terms, a forecast of whether a stock will lose half its value or a quarter is worth nothing to someone who neither holds it nor can sell it short (our judgment). The example is extreme by design, since with short sales allowed, holding the first stock and selling the second short would gain whatever happened, an arbitrage (our judgment); it shows only that a bit does not by itself fix value.

Stein’s (2008) model gives a second gap, in which signals lose value one after another while each adds the same number of bits (our derivation). In a footnote he maps his model of conversations among competitors onto a security whose payoff is a sum of signals, the ii-th normal with mean zero and variance βi\beta^i, for a decay rate β\beta below 1 (working-paper version). Each further signal’s marginal value, counted as the fraction of the payoff’s variance it explains, declines geometrically (working-paper version). Counted in bits, every signal adds the same 12log⁡2(1/β)\frac{1}{2} \log_2 (1/\beta), whatever its place (our derivation). In his first example the decay rate is 0.95 (working-paper version); the first signal then explains 5% of the variance and the seventh about 3.7%, while each carries about 0.037 bits (our calculation). In the book’s terms, the fraction explained is closer to a value (our judgment), since to a forecaster whose loss is the squared error it is the share of the expected loss removed (our derivation).9 Ideas that travel far hold only a few signals, and Stein says they tend to have relatively little real information content (working-paper version); Section 2.10 gives his two conditions for such ideas to move prices little, and Chapters 15 and 19 take up how they spread among competitors.

Chapter 4 sets out the two ways of valuing a signal and when each equals an amount of information (Box 1.1).

2.7 Channel capacity and its borrowed name

Chapter 3’s capacity law, which holds that each person can take in only so much information per unit of time, takes its name from Shannon’s channel capacity. Shannon (1948) defined the capacity of a noisy channel as its largest possible rate of transmission, the maximum over all sources that could feed it, in bits per use of the channel. Because the input is chosen to suit the channel, capacity describes the channel alone. A source whose rate is below capacity can be sent with an arbitrarily small frequency of errors by some coding system, and one above it cannot. In general, capacity is approached only by coding over ever longer blocks, strings of symbols coded together. Box 2.1 gives the formulas.10

The drug report of Section 2.3 is a binary symmetric channel, which flips each symbol with the same probability, here one time in five. It reaches its capacity with equally likely inputs, about 0.278 bits per use (our calculation). The drug’s prior of 0.30 is not that input, and the report carries the 0.236 bits of Section 2.3. Kelly found the transmission rate meaningful “even though no coding is contemplated”, and a trader’s case is the same: the world sends signals with its own probabilities, nobody codes them for her, and she gets the rate at the input the world supplies, in general below capacity (our judgment).

The capacity law borrows only the ceiling, the side of Shannon’s theorem that says what no code can exceed (our judgment). It also holds that tracking anything, prices included, draws on the same budget. That is an assumption about attention, a person’s limited capacity to process information. Shannon (1948) gives each channel its own capacity, and its results do not by themselves give one budget shared by everything a person tracks (our judgment); Chapter 10 treats the shared budget of attention.

2.8 Physical readings of mutual information

Section 1.4 compared a trader with Maxwell’s demon, an imagined being that sorts molecules by looking at them. Physics reads mutual information in three further ways, set out with their conditions in Box 2.2. The first is Parrondo’s (2023). He reads measuring as creating correlations between the demon and the system, which raises the free energy, the part of a system’s energy that can be turned into work, and so takes work unless something offsets it; on his reading the Szilard engine, a heat engine with one molecule, trades that stored free energy for work. This holds at one temperature, with an ideal measurement that is then used. A trader’s private signal is likewise a correlation, between her records and θ\theta, which betting against the price turns into return (our analogy). The analogy stops at the temperature, since nothing in a market plays the part of kTkT, the typical thermal energy (Section 1.4). In the second, with no loss in the cycle, the work from one outcome of a measurement has the same form as the growth after one message (Section 2.4), with the price in the place of the equilibrium distribution, the system’s chances when left alone (our analogy), but only if the price is calibrated, equal to the true probability, the counterpart of a start in equilibrium (our analogy).

The third rests on directed information, which sums what the signals so far tell about each new outcome given the earlier outcomes. If the outcomes are independent and alike from round to round, a signal that reports only the previous outcome, a mere recap of the past, helps neither gambler nor engine, though as a channel it has the capacity of a perfect one. Tetlock’s column reads like such a recap, a post-mortem of the prior day, and still predicted the next day (working-paper version). Daily returns need not be independent, so the rule does not carry over to it (our judgment), and its pessimism predicts the next day even with five lags of Dow returns among the controls (working-paper version). Transfer entropy, which measures the information exchanged between two time series, has been criticized as a tool for finding causal links, so a measured flow of information between two price series, such as bitcoin’s and ether’s, does not by itself show which drives the other (our judgment).11

Tetlock (2007), the empirical study this chapter rests on, set the Wall Street Journal’s market column against daily Dow returns from January 1, 1984, to September 17, 1999 (working-paper version); estimates for the 1990s appear to dominate the full sample. Tetlock (2011), in Section 2.10, covers news about S&P 500 firms from 1984 to 2004 (working-paper version).12 The 2007 study covers one column, mainly one index and one country, and does not measure in bits what the column tells about θ\theta or about prices (our judgment). The chapter’s other numbers are worked examples, and it tests no quantitative prediction of its own.

2.10 A trader’s checklist

The questions below check a piece of news; four come with a testable implication and one with a direct check. None says what to buy or sell, and good answers do not make a trade safe (our judgment).

  1. About what? Does the news tell about the state of the world θ\theta or about how others will trade (Section 2.5)? Testable: Tetlock argues that a fall caused by new information about fundamentals would not be fully reversed (working-paper version).
  2. New to whom? What does it add to what those who set the price have seen, its conditional mutual information (Section 2.3)? Testable: in Tetlock (2011), price reactions to stale stories, marked by signs such as another story about the firm in the previous week, partially reversed in the next week, and those to news with new earnings information did not reverse (working-paper version).
  3. How far should it move the odds? How much likelier is the message if the event happens than if not, and does it share a source with messages already counted (Section 2.4)? Testable: counting copies as independent messages pushes probabilities too far the way the copies point, so fewer than 90% of events raised to 90% by repeated reports happen (our inference).
  4. Would it change a trade? A forecast can carry a full bit and change no position (Section 2.6). Check: in a backtest, a test of a trading rule on past data, compare the positions with and without the signal; where they match, the signal adds no growth, as in Barron and Cover’s example (our inference).
  5. How far has it traveled? In Stein’s model, ideas that reach the most investors tend to have relatively little real information content and so move prices little when those who share them compete, if investors are rational Bayesians who do not overreact to news from conversations (working-paper version). If news heard face to face seems particularly salient, even low-quality rumors might move prices significantly (working-paper version). Testable: among investors who compete, an idea shared by many should predict a security’s payoff less well than one shared by a few (our inference); this book has not tested it, and the item rests on Stein’s model alone.13

2.11 Summary

  • From 1984 to 1999, gloom in one Wall Street Journal column predicted a lower Dow return the next day, made up within a week (working-paper version). In the book’s terms it carried information about prices and, on Tetlock’s reading, almost no new information about θ\theta (our judgment); whether it stood for sentiment or risk aversion, he leaves open.
  • The book measures information about θ\theta by default. Entropy, the average surprise, depends on probabilities alone; mutual information is the average fall in uncertainty a signal brings, and conditional mutual information, the book’s amount of information, what it adds to what is known.
  • One message is measured by the relative entropy of the posterior from the prior, which averages to the mutual information. Log-likelihood ratios add for messages independent given the outcome.
  • In Barron and Cover’s example a bit adds nothing to the log growth rate of a portfolio that cannot sell short and invests all its wealth, so an amount of information does not by itself fix value (Chapter 4).
  • Channel capacity is the most a channel can carry, approached in general only by coding long blocks; a trader gets the rate the world supplies (our judgment), and Chapter 3’s capacity law borrows the name for attention.
  • Parrondo reads each bit of mutual information as kTln⁡2kT \ln 2 of free energy stored in correlations, at one temperature and with an ideal measurement whose result is used; the analogy with a trader stops at the temperature (our analogy). A measured flow of information between two price series does not by itself show which drives the other (our judgment).

Conditional mutual information. I(X;Y∣Z)=H(X∣Z)−H(X∣Y,Z)I(X; Y \mid Z) = H(X \mid Z) - H(X \mid Y, Z), and the chain rule for information is I(X1,…,Xn;Y)=∑iI(Xi;Y∣Xi−1,…,X1)I(X_1, \ldots, X_n; Y) = \sum_{i} I(X_i; Y \mid X_{i-1}, \ldots, X_1). Conditioning can raise it: for independent fair bits XX and YY and their sum ZZ, I(X;Y)=0I(X; Y) = 0 but I(X;Y∣Z)=1/2I(X; Y \mid Z) = 1/2 bit.

Units. With natural logarithms the unit is the nat, Shannon’s natural unit; one nat is 1/ln⁡2≈1.4431/\ln 2 \approx 1.443 bits (our calculation).

Log-odds. In Duffie and Manso’s model of information percolation, one person’s signals about a high or low outcome are independent given the outcome, and different people’s signals are independent. The posterior probability of the high outcome after signals SS is [1+1−νν(12)ℓ]−1[1 + \frac{1 - \nu}{\nu} (\frac{1}{2})^{\ell}]^{-1}, with ν\nu the prior and ℓ\ell the type of the signals, which they write θ\theta, a sum of log-likelihood ratios taken to base 1/2. Equivalently, the posterior odds are the prior odds times 2ℓ2^{\ell} (our derivation), so ℓ\ell is the evidence for the high outcome in bits, the sum of the signals’ log-likelihood ratios (our derivation). For independent sets of signals the sum of the types is all that matters for the posterior, so two people who meet and tell each other their types both end with the sum.

Capacity. Cover and Thomas write the capacity of a channel as C=max⁡p(x)I(X;Y)C = \max_{p(x)} I(X; Y), the mutual information between input XX and output YY, where the maximum is taken over all input distributions. A binary symmetric channel that flips each symbol with probability pp has C=1−H(p)C = 1 - H(p) bits; for p=0.2p = 0.2, H(0.2)≈0.722H(0.2) \approx 0.722 and C≈0.278C \approx 0.278 bits (our calculation).

Numbers. For the drug example, with prior π=0.30\pi = 0.30 and a report right 80% of the time, H(θ)=0.881H(\theta) = 0.881, H(Y)=0.958H(Y) = 0.958, H(Y∣θ)=0.722H(Y \mid \theta) = 0.722 and I(θ;Y)=0.881−0.645=0.958−0.722=0.236I(\theta; Y) = 0.881 - 0.645 = 0.958 - 0.722 = 0.236 bits (our calculation). Box 2.2 Mutual information in physics

Information here is in nats; kTkT times nats is an energy, and one bit corresponds to kTln⁡2kT \ln 2. Parrondo writes entropy in units of energy over temperature, leaving Boltzmann’s constant kk implicit.15

Measurement. Averaged over outcomes, a measurement MM of a system XX changes the nonequilibrium free energy by Δℱmeas=ℱpost−ℱpre=T[S(X)−S(X∣M)]=TI(X;M),\Delta \mathcal{F}_{\text{meas}} = \mathcal{F}_{\text{post}} - \mathcal{F}_{\text{pre}} = T [S(X) - S(X \mid M)] = T \, I(X; M), and a state ρ\rho out of equilibrium has more free energy than the equilibrium state ρeq\rho^{\text{eq}}, by F−Feq=TD(ρ∥ρeq)≥0F - F^{\text{eq}} = T \, D(\rho \| \rho^{\text{eq}}) \ge 0. Parrondo interprets the Szilard engine as an exchange between work and the free energy stored in the correlations a measurement creates, kTkT times the mutual information in nats. His reading assumes an isothermal feedback process; an ideal classical measurement, which leaves the system unaffected; and a protocol, the plan for moving the partition, that uses the information, failing which the standard second law returns. He identifies Shannon’s entropy with thermodynamic entropy through two assumptions about what an outside observer knows, an identification not correct in general; the readings in this box rest on it (our judgment). Counting the work of measuring and of resetting the memory restores the second law, Wtot=Wmeas+Wfb+Wreset≥0W_{\text{tot}} = W_{\text{meas}} + W_{\text{fb}} + W_{\text{reset}} \ge 0; there WW is the work needed, done on the system, and below it is the work extracted.

One outcome. A single outcome in general leaves a system that was in equilibrium out of it. In Vinkler, Permuter and Merhav (2016), if a cycle loses energy f(x)f(x), the best protocol after the outcome yy is still matched to the posterior, QX∣y*=PX∣yQ^*_{X \mid y} = P_{X \mid y}, and E[W∣Y=y]=kTD(PX∣y∥PX)−E[f(X)∣Y=y];E[W \mid Y = y] = kT \, D(P_{X \mid y} \| P_X) - E[f(X) \mid Y = y]; the cycle is worth running only if the first term exceeds the second. With f=0f = 0, one outcome yields kTD(PX∣y∥PX)kT \, D(P_{X \mid y} \| P_X) (our derivation), and nn cycles yield on average nkTI(X;Y)n kT \, I(X; Y). The one-outcome yield matches, up to the factor kTln⁡2kT \ln 2, the growth D(py∥q)D(p_y \| q) of Section 2.4 (our analogy). On the physical side the match needs control fine enough to make the posterior the equilibrium state. Esposito and Van den Broeck (2011) build it by first retuning the energies at once so that a nonequilibrium state becomes the equilibrium one, a construction that “may need a (infinitely) large number of parameters”; reaching the maximum work above also requires an end in equilibrium, reached reversibly. Memory costs are left out.

Directed information. When rounds depend on one another, the gain from signals used as they arrive is set by the directed information from signals to outcomes, which sums what the signals so far tell about each outcome given earlier outcomes. With Xi−1X^{i-1} the outcomes before round ii and YiY^i the signals up to it, it is I(Yn→Xn)=∑i=1nI(Xi;Yi∣Xi−1),I(Y^n \to X^n) = \sum_{i=1}^n I(X_i; Y^i \mid X^{i-1}), which gives the gain in expected log wealth and, times kTkT, in work. The capacity of a channel with memory is set instead by I(Xn→Yn)=∑i=1nI(Yi;Xi∣Yi−1)I(X^n \to Y^n) = \sum_{i=1}^n I(Y_i; X^i \mid Y^{i-1}). For Yi=Xi−1Y_i = X_{i-1}, with independent fair binary XiX_i and a constant Y1Y_1, I(Yn→Xn)=0I(Y^n \to X^n) = 0 and I(Xn→Yn)=(n−1)ln⁡2I(X^n \to Y^n) = (n - 1) \ln 2 nats, that is n−1n - 1 bits (our calculation); the source says only that the second is positive.
