Chapter 13 Disagreement, Persuasion and Biased Beliefs

Tetlock (2011) asked whether investors react to stale news, stories that repeat what has already appeared, in public financial news about S&P 500 firms from 1984 to 2004 (working-paper version; Section 10.7). He found that market reactions to stale stories partially reverse in the next week, while reactions to stories with more new information reverse to a much smaller extent, or even continue (working-paper version). For news about a firm that had another story in the five days before, the reversal over the second to fifth trading days came to a cumulative 27 basis points, hundredths of a percent, after adjustment for risk (working-paper version).

He started from psychology. In the studies he cites, a person’s belief in an ambiguous statement increases as he or she encounters it more often, the “truth effect”, whether or not the repetition conveys any new information (as reported by Tetlock; working-paper version). The model in his appendix, which he offers as one possible mechanism, assumes that irrational investors’ perceptions of their signal conflate old and new information, citing DeMarzo, Vayanos and Zwiebel (working-paper version).

In that model everyone sees the same signals, and investors differ in how they process them (Section 10.7). The root model of Chapter 5 assumes Bayesian updating, a common prior, Bayes’ rule and a processing rule that never changes; Table 5.1 in Section 5.8 lists this default with the others, and this chapter drops it. It also drops the exogenous counterparty in Section 13.3, since biased beliefs supply trade without outside noise traders; the single exogenous fact in Sections 13.6.2 and 13.9, where averaging ties several issues into one and where opinions have no fact to learn; and, in Section 13.6.3, where the same information returns along several paths, the part of homogeneous mixing about sources (our judgment; Table 5.1).1

13.1 Copying and pooling

The root model spreads one message from one source: all the informed have seen the same signal, and the more who know, the faster the rest hear it (Section 5.2). Here each person starts with a signal of her own about the same fact. In DeMarzo, Vayanos and Zwiebel’s (2003) model, each starting signal is the fact plus a normally distributed error with mean zero, independent across people (working-paper version). Acemoglu and Ozdaglar (2011) ask in their survey whether social learning will effectively aggregate such dispersed information about an underlying state.

The measure changes with the question (our judgment). In place of how many have heard, one asks how much each person’s starting information counts where the beliefs, or the price, end up: its influence weight. In the root model’s price, under the conditions of Section 5.3, wealth sets the influence weights; in the averaging models of this chapter the network sets them (Section 13.6).

Acemoglu and Ozdaglar sort the literature on two coordinates. Information may come from a person’s own experience, from observing others’ actions and experiences, or from communication with others. In Bayesian models, people use Bayes’ rule to form the “best” estimate of what they do not know, given their priors and their understanding of the world, and non-Bayesian models are all the others. Table 13.1 crosses the two, with learning from others’ actions and learning through communication as separate columns, as in the survey.

The two coordinates are Acemoglu and Ozdaglar’s, and placing each model in a cell is this book’s classification; copying a neighbor’s opinion, as in the voter model, sits with actions because the copier takes over a stated choice without the information behind it (our judgment). This chapter covers the non-Bayesian row, the Bayesian cell for communication (Section 13.2) and, for comparison, two models from the column of actions (Section 13.7); Chapter 14 covers Bayesian learning from others’ actions.

Acemoglu and Ozdaglar warn that each non-Bayesian rule leads to different types of behavior, often with at least some implications that depend on the parameters chosen within a class of rules, and that until empirical work narrows the choice there is a high degree of arbitrariness. This chapter therefore sorts the rules by what each changes in Bayesian updating and picks none as the right one (our judgment).2

13.2 Exchanging beliefs and exchanging signals

Aumann (1976) gives a coin example. Two people share a uniform prior on the coin’s chance of heads, and each may toss it once; the tosses come up heads and tails. If each knows only her own toss, their posteriors for heads on the next toss are 2/3 and 1/3, and if each then tells the other her posterior, both conclude that the tosses gave one head and one tail and revise to 1/2. Now let each make several tosses without knowing how many the other makes. Suppose both toss 4 times, with HHHT and HTTT; they again report 2/3 and 1/3. These posteriors may come from one toss, from 4 or from more, and each may give more weight to her own observations; Aumann writes that some revision would certainly be called for but that it does not seem clear that it would necessarily lead to equal posteriors. He adds that his result implies that the exchange of posteriors will continue until they are equal, and the example does not say how many rounds that takes (our judgment). The result itself, that two people with the same priors whose posteriors for an event are common knowledge have equal posteriors, belongs to Chapter 21.

A posterior summarizes private signals. When each posterior can come from only one signal, as with one toss each, one exchange recovers the signals; when several signals give the same posterior, as 2/3 comes from H or from HHHT, a posterior drops facts such as the number of tosses behind it, and one exchange need not bring agreement (our judgment). In this book’s reading DeMarzo, Vayanos and Zwiebel’s Theorem 3 is a network version of this cell (our judgment): if people are fully rational, the network is common knowledge and strongly connected, meaning that a chain of listeners leads from anyone to anyone, and everyone judges each person’s precision correctly, all reach the correct pooled belief after at most N2N^2 rounds, NN being the number of people (working-paper version). The authors stress that even then the calculations can be very complicated (working-paper version).3

13.3 Differing priors and differing readings

Two traders read the same earnings release, one taking it as strong evidence of a good year ahead and the other as weak evidence. Milgrom and Stokey’s (1982) no-trade theorem of Section 4.4 rules out trade on private information when all its premises hold, among them concordant beliefs, under which traders may start from different priors but agree, roughly, how the information should be interpreted. If all information is public, beliefs need not be concordant to preclude trade from a start that was ex ante Pareto optimal for trades on θ\theta; with concave, differentiable utility, a public announcement precludes further trade if and only if every trader gives it the same likelihood ratio, the same answer to how much more likely it is in one state than in another. The two traders give the release different likelihood ratios, so the theorem no longer rules out trade after it; whether they trade depends on more than this condition (our judgment).

Heterogeneous priors, views that differ before any news, show up in trades made before the news where markets let people bet on the fact; where a friction such as a ban on short sales stops those trades, the differing views can move the price (our judgment). Harrison and Kreps (1978) build such a market. All investors have the same economic information, though different classes may arrive at different subjective probabilities from it; each class is risk-neutral; and, most importantly, short sales are forbidden. Investors show speculative behavior, in their definition, when the right to resell a stock makes them willing to pay more than they would if obliged to hold it forever. In their equilibrium, typically the lowest consistent price exceeds every investor’s expected present worth of the dividends, a “speculative premium” paid for anticipated capital gains. The precise results depend on risk neutrality, infinite wealth and no short sales, and the authors argue that the model is a very good approximation to one in which only the ban on short sales remains. Chapter 20 takes up this resale option.

A trade that loses on average needs someone willing to lose, and biased beliefs supply a counterparty with a motive of its own, so trade can go on without the outside noise traders of Section 4.4 (our judgment; Table 5.1). In Dhawan and Putniņš’s framework, when all participants are alike, rational individuals with correct beliefs do not take part in cryptocurrency pumps, while participants who know they are fast enough, and are not too risk-averse, can rationally take part, which shifts the puzzle to the less skilled or slower (working-paper version; Section 4.4); Section 13.5 takes up overconfidence.4

13.4 Fixed overconfidence and self-attribution

A trader who buys on her own reading of a drug trial and then sees good results may credit her skill, and after bad ones blame luck.

In Daniel, Hirshleifer and Subrahmanyam’s (1998) model, informed investors underestimate the error variance of their private signal, while all investors estimate correctly the variance of the noise in the public signal. Their Proposition 2 states that if investors are overconfident, price changes are unconditionally negatively autocorrelated at both short and long lags, so with constant confidence the model accords with long-run reversals, a price moving back over long horizons, and does not accord with short-term momentum, a price that keeps moving the same way over short horizons, both read as unconditional autocorrelations. The authors state that these implications rest on a fixed confidence level; returns after events selected on the mispricing behave differently (Section 12.5).

Confidence can also move with outcomes. According to attribution theory, people too strongly attribute events that confirm the validity of their actions to high ability, and events that disconfirm them to external noise or sabotage. In the model a public signal confirms a trade made on a private signal if it has the same sign, good news after a buy or bad news after a sell, and the authors assume that confirming news raises confidence while disconfirming news lowers it only modestly, if at all. Their Proposition 7 gives the result with its premises: if confidence changes because of biased self-attribution, and if overreaction or correction is sufficiently gradual, price changes show unconditional positive autocorrelation at short lags and negative autocorrelation at long lags.

Momentum followed by reversal does not single out self-attribution (our judgment). In Hong and Stein’s (1999) model of Section 10.7, the newswatchers’ bounded rationality creates a tendency for prices to underreact to private information in the short run, so momentum traders can profit by chasing trends, and if these are restricted to simple strategies, their trading must lead to overreaction at long horizons (working-paper version). Chapter 20 sets the two side by side and takes up what overconfidence does to momentum in prices.

In the model the overconfident informed traders lose money on average. The authors add at once, citing other work that includes a paper of their own, that if risk-averse traders are overconfident about genuine information signals, overconfidence lets them exploit information more effectively, so the expected profits of the overconfident can be greater than those of the fully rational. Table 1.1 says that trading on a wrong belief earns less and can lose, a cost measured there in the growth of a bettor’s capital; here the measure is expected profit under risk aversion, and the information is genuine and only overrated (our judgment). Overweighting private signals is one of the three mechanisms of underreaction of Section 12.5 (Table 12.1).5

13.5 A worked example on pump participation

Return to the pumps of Section 4.1, in which a Telegram group names a coin at a set time and its members rush to buy. In Dhawan and Putniņš’s framework, a participant who does not know her place in the queue expects to lose β(M+2)/2\beta(M + 2)/2, where β\beta is the price impact of one order and MM the number of units the administrators buy first (working-paper version). In their baseline illustration the price starts at $5, M=10M = 10, there are N′=100N' = 100 participants and β=0.2\beta = 0.2 (working-paper version), so the expected loss is $1.20 (our calculation).

The authors consider overconfidence in the form of the better-than-average effect: here, a belief that one is better than average at “picking the peak” and exiting at a higher price than the average participant (working-paper version). Their parameter ε\varepsilon is the slope of the perceived distribution of exit prices, which it tilts toward high prices (working-paper version). Assuming risk neutrality, individuals whose overconfidence exceeds a threshold εmin\varepsilon^{\min}, their equation (5), would participate (working-paper version). The model also assumes linear price impact and a place in the selling queue that does not depend on the place in the buying queue (working-paper version).

In the baseline illustration, exit prices range over β(M+N′−1)\beta(M + N' - 1), which is $21.80, and εmin\varepsilon^{\min} is about 0.00139 per squared dollar (our calculation; Box 13.2). The threshold falls as β\beta rises, so pumps of less liquid coins tend to have more participation from overconfident individuals; it rises with MM and falls with N′N' (working-paper version).

Overconfidence and gambling preferences are individual characteristics, and the authors’ data do not let them identify individuals, so they use a proxy for overconfidence measured at the level of each pump and a marketwide proxy for gambling (working-paper version). The main proxy is the average start-to-peak return of the two most recent pumps run by the Telegram groups behind the pump, on the reasoning that self-attribution makes members of recently successful groups more likely to be overconfident (working-paper version). Participation is the log of trading volume from the pump signal to three hours after the peak (working-paper version). Their sample is 355 pumps on Binance and Yobit from December 29, 2017, to June 22, 2018 (working-paper version). In their regression with all regressors, which has 211 of those pumps, participation rises by 37.97% when the overconfidence proxy moves from its 25th to its 75th percentile, an increase in prior pump returns by 87%, and by 24.33% when the gambling proxy does the same (working-paper version).

The proxy can move for other reasons (our judgment). The groups’ messages include success stories of people profiting from pumps, which, the authors write, are likely intended to encourage participation (working-paper version), so a group’s recent returns may also track its reputation and its recruiting. The 37.97% is an association at the level of pump groups, and on its own it cannot separate overconfidence from recruitment (our judgment).6

13.6 Averaging and persuasion bias

13.6.1 DeGroot’s committee

DeGroot (1974) modeled how a group might reach a common distribution for an unknown θ\theta simply by revealing their individual distributions to each other and pooling their opinions. Each member revises her distribution to a linear combination of the members’ distributions, with weights that, in his model, she should choose before she learns the others’ distributions, by the importance she gives each member’s opinion, her own included, and she may give her own a large weight. If she still wishes to weight the members as before, and DeGroot sees no basis for changing the weights at that point, then to stay consistent she averages again with the same weights, round after round. The theory applies equally to a point estimate. It assumes that no one can learn whose opinion is closer to the truth, that is, that no outside data about θ\theta are available, and DeGroot leaves unstudied the members who change their weights after seeing the others’ opinions, or how much those move.

Read as a Markov chain, the weights give the consensus. When one is reached, everyone holds the same weighted average of the starting distributions, with weights that solve πP=π\pi P = \pi and add up to 1. A consensus is reached if all the recurrent states communicate with each other and are aperiodic, and it is not reached if the states form at least two disjoint closed sets, groups that listen only within themselves, or if the single closed set is periodic; when a consensus is reached, a member outside the closed set gets no weight in it.

DeMarzo, Vayanos and Zwiebel give the rule a reason and a name. In the first round people hear genuinely new information, so weighting it by the precision they ascribe to each source is optimal and there is no persuasion bias (working-paper version). In later rounds they treat what they hear as new and independent and keep the first round’s relative precisions for those they listen to, without adjusting for repetitions; that failure, treating all information as new, is persuasion bias (working-paper version). Their model lets people change the weight on their own beliefs relative to others over time (working-paper version), while DeGroot’s weights stay fixed.7

13.6.2 Influence set by the network

DeMarzo, Vayanos and Zwiebel’s Theorem 1 holds when the group is strongly connected and people do not become too fixed in their beliefs, that is, the weight they put on what they hear does not go to zero too quickly (working-paper version). Everyone then converges to the same weighted average of the starting beliefs, with weights given by the unique solution of wT=wwT = w, which they call social influence (working-paper version) and this book calls influence weights. A person’s influence depends on how many listen to her and also on whether those listeners are themselves influential (working-paper version; Box 13.1).

In one special setting influence follows a simple count: where communication is two-way, so that whoever listens to a neighbor is listened to in turn, and precision is equal, a person’s influence is proportional to the number in her listening set, herself included (working-paper version).

Whether the consensus is right is a separate question. Suppose everyone judges each person’s precision correctly. Then, by their Theorem 2, the consensus is correct if and only if the listening structure is balanced, meaning that for each person the self-weights of those who listen to her add up to one (working-paper version). Listening structures are typically unbalanced, and then, in the authors’ intuition, the consensus is incorrect because well-connected people have influence in excess of the accuracy of their information (working-paper version). A consensus, like a price, weights what each person knew at the start, and where the weights that the network sets differ from the precision of each person’s information, the pooling goes wrong by that difference (our judgment; Section 5.3).

Speed is a third question. How fast beliefs converge is governed by the second eigenvalue of the matrix of weights (working-paper version; Box 13.1). Golub and Jackson note that whether a society converges to the truth is unrelated to how quickly it reaches a consensus, and that one can have both, neither, or either one without the other (working-paper version).

Under DeGroot averaging each belief moves by a fixed-weight average of its gaps to the neighbors’ beliefs. The rule is linear diffusion on the network, with no product term of the kind that makes the root model speed itself up and gives it its S-curve (our judgment; Section 5.6).

In Figure 13.1, a star with two-way communication and equal precision, the hub holds 0 after one round, the best pooled estimate if the six signals are independent and equally precise (our derivation), yet averaging goes on to 0.25, the hub’s starting belief counting three times as much as an outer person’s (our calculation; Box 13.1). Each round in which the outer people repeat the hub’s view back to her counts it again, persuasion bias in its simplest form (our judgment). In the lower panel one outer person keeps 0.9 on herself instead of 1/2; her influence weight rises to 5/12, and beliefs need 26 rounds instead of 7 to come within 0.01 of the consensus (our calculation). In DeMarzo, Vayanos and Zwiebel’s model a high self-weight comes from a high precision ascribed to one’s own signal, which is the overconfidence of Section 13.4 when that precision is overrated (our judgment).8

The root model has a single fact; with several issues, repeated averaging ties them together. With sufficient communication, and for a general class of networks, DeMarzo, Vayanos and Zwiebel show that a person’s relative position on every issue will be the same, so long-run differences of opinion across all issues can be summarized on a simple “left-right” spectrum; their Theorem 4, which states this, relies on the second eigenvalue being real (working-paper version). The single dimension comes from the Markovian structure of updating and the constant relative weights of persuasion bias (working-paper version).

13.6.3 Repetition counted as news

Section 7.5 gave DeMarzo, Vayanos and Zwiebel’s reading of persuasion bias: well-connected people are influential because their information is repeated many times in the social network (working-paper version), where the same information returns along several paths and the part of homogeneous mixing about sources fails (our judgment; Sections 7.1 and 7.5).

DeMarzo, Vayanos and Zwiebel’s model has no market prices (our judgment). On prices this book reads Tetlock’s double counting as follows: listeners who count a replay of known content as new information push the price too far, and the price then partly moves back, though Tetlock’s partial reversal does not by itself name the cause (our judgment; Section 10.7). Pedersen introduces rational agents and financial markets into an otherwise standard DeGroot model, which Chapter 19 takes up.

This book’s rule is to count independent sources and leave repetitions out, so five accounts that all pass on one original report are worth that one report about the fact (our judgment; Sections 9.5 and 11.3.2). A Bayesian who watches a cascade treats those who imitate as adding no information (Chapter 14), and persuasion bias does the opposite, counting each repetition as more (our judgment). Under persuasion bias, those heard most often weigh more in what the price-setters believe than the accuracy of their information alone would give them (our judgment; Section 9.6).9

13.7 Three kinds of influential few

Three models single out a few people whom everyone, or infinitely many, take in, and each finds that they can stop a group from learning; the harms rest on different features, so the processing rule alone does not rank them (our judgment; Sections 7.5 and 9.5).

  • Prominence. In Golub and Jackson’s DeGroot learning, with independent signals whose mean is the truth and whose variances are bounded away from zero, a bounded number of prominent people, who receive a nonvanishing amount of possibly indirect attention from everyone, make learning fail (working-paper version). Under DeGroot averaging prominence alone is enough (our judgment).
  • The royal family. In Bala and Goyal’s example, where people learn from their neighbors’ results, the bad results of a small set whom everyone observes can overwhelm any good news gathered locally; everyone then switches to the action with known payoffs, no further information is generated, and society is locked into an inferior choice, while without them it chooses the optimal action in the long run (working-paper version). The authors trace the lock-in to priors that are not very dispersed and to the structure of neighborhoods, the example working because the royal family’s negative information exceeds any positive information that a local neighborhood can generate (working-paper version). This book adds that the results are shared (Section 9.5) and that only use of the uncertain action generates information (our judgment).
  • Excessive influence. In Acemoglu, Dahleh, Lobel and Ozdaglar’s Bayesian model of learning from others’ actions, in which everyone prefers the same action given the state (Section 14.6), there is no asymptotic learning where a finite group is all that infinitely many people observe, with probability uniformly bounded away from zero, and there is when no such group exists and private beliefs are unbounded (working-paper version).10

13.8 Sources that rarely or never change their minds

This book sets side by side three grades of sources that listen little or not at all (our judgment). The first grade listens now and then. In Acemoglu, Ozdaglar and ParandehGheibi’s (2010) model of Section 9.5, people meet in pairs and adopt the average of their beliefs, while forceful agents influence some of those they meet without changing their own opinion, though they still update now and then from their own social neighborhoods. In Acemoglu and Ozdaglar’s account, the average of the starting beliefs equals θ\theta, so that enough information is dispersed among everyone. Despite the forceful agents the society ultimately reaches a consensus, whose value is random even given the starting beliefs; without forceful agents the society aggregates information effectively; and in a fast-mixing network the rest of the society moderates the forceful agents’ beliefs before they spread widely.

The second grade never listens. Acemoglu and Ozdaglar’s fully stubborn agents are like forceful ones but never update and continuously influence the rest. Their Theorem 11 assumes that at least two stubborn agents hold different opinions, and then, with probability one, beliefs do not converge. Disagreement persists, and all opinions fluctuate even in the long run, even though the network is strongly connected, because the stubborn agents pull the society in different directions. The fluctuation belongs to their model, in which pairs meet one at a time and the outcome is random (our judgment). Under DeGroot’s deterministic averaging, two stubborn people with different views form two closed sets, so no consensus is reached, and when everyone else listens to them directly or through others, everyone else settles at a fixed belief set by the two views and the weights, with no fluctuation (our derivation). Pedersen’s “fanatic” agents of Section 9.5, stubborn about their own view, belong to this grade (our judgment).

This book places a third grade, the committed minority, among models with no truth to learn, in which opinions compete and the committed never switch (our judgment). It has not read that work and gives no critical size for such a minority. Lucchini et al., who draw on it for GameStop, cite theoretical and experimental evidence that minorities of committed individuals may mobilize large fractions of a population even when they are extremely small (as reported by Lucchini et al.; Section 11.6). What a committed minority changes is which opinion the population ends up holding, a different quantity from the distance between a consensus and the truth in the first two grades (our judgment).

Where a source sits matters differently under averaging and under thresholds (our judgment; Section 11.5). In Acemoglu, Ozdaglar and ParandehGheibi’s averaging, misinformation can be substantial in slow-mixing networks, split into clusters with weak communication in between, where forceful agents get their information from people they influenced before, and one network can lead to very different limits depending on where the forceful agents sit. Chapter 15 takes up a sender’s choice of seeds. None of the three grades distorts what it passes on, so the error, if any, lies in the view and in the weight others give it (Sections 9.1 and 9.5); a stubborn source works on the others like an outside source that never stops, and after the start it adds no new information about the fact (our judgment).11

13.9 Opinion dynamics without a truth

Many models of opinion have no fact to learn, so the root model’s single fact, whose value trading leaves untouched, is gone altogether (Chapter 5). A long argument among traders over whether a coin is a good store of value, which no settlement will decide, is of this kind (our judgment). Castellano, Fortunato and Loreto (2009) review such models as statistical physics, viewing individuals “as adaptive instead of rational agents”. In their account, opinion dynamics deals with the competition between different responses to the same question, alternatives of comparable plausibility, so that either of two people can influence the other. With no fact θ\theta these models carry no information about θ\theta, and only two layers of the framework remain, beliefs and prices (our judgment). The reviewers caution that the field has developed without coordination, turning mechanisms that authors found reasonable into rules, often with no reference to real sociological studies. The results below come from the models they review (as reported by Castellano, Fortunato and Loreto), in four families that this book uses (our judgment).

  • Copying. In the voter model each step picks a person and one of her neighbors, and the person takes the neighbor’s opinion; the reviewers call it a rather crude description of any real process, popular because it can be solved exactly. Averaged over all the ways the process can run, the mean opinion is conserved, so in a finite system the chance that an opinion takes over equals its starting share, on a lattice of any dimension.
  • Majority. With everyone able to talk to everyone, each step picks a group at random, and all its members take the group’s majority opinion. There is a threshold such that if one opinion’s starting share is above it, everyone holds that opinion in the long run, and if below, everyone holds the other, and the time to consensus grows like log⁡N\log N. If groups can have an even number of members and ties go to one opinion, that opinion’s threshold is below one half, so it can prevail even when it starts as a minority.
  • Averaging. DeGroot’s rule belongs here (Section 13.6). In Deffuant’s model two people who meet, if their opinions are close enough (next item), move toward each other by a fixed fraction of the gap between them.
  • Bounded confidence. In Deffuant’s model, opinions start at random between 0 and 1, and two people interact only when their opinions differ by less than a threshold ϵ\epsilon. Simulations find about 1/(2ϵ)1/(2\epsilon) clusters of opinion at the end, and on complete graphs, regular lattices, random graphs and scale-free networks everyone ends at the same opinion, 1/2, when ϵ\epsilon exceeds 1/2. Most results come from numerical simulation, as the model is not analytically solvable.

In these models the outcome for the whole group follows from the update rule, and each result says which opinion prevails or how many clusters form; it is silent on which is right (our judgment). They answer Section 10.8’s question of how views on a question with no true answer rise and fall.12

13.10 Mechanical rules and wrong models

Some models take a rule as given, with no derivation from Bayes’ rule (our judgment): Bass’s imitation term, in which people adopt on contact with those who have adopted (Chapter 5), Granovetter’s thresholds (Chapter 11) and the voter model’s copying (Section 13.9). Acting on how many others have adopted belongs in Table 13.1’s cell for non-Bayesian rules applied to others’ actions, which Chapter 14 sets beside Bayesian cascades.

People can also use a wrong model without knowing it, and this book has met four forms (our judgment): neglect, in Hirshleifer and Teoh’s inattentive investors, who take pro forma earnings at face value (Sections 9.3 and 10.3); counting twice, in Tetlock’s stale news (Section 13.6.3); a missed carry-over of earnings changes, in Bernard and Thomas’s reading of earnings drift (Section 12.5); and staking by a wrong belief, which grows capital more slowly than staking by the truth (Section 1.6.7 and Chapters 2 and 4). In all four the news is seen, apart from Chapter 10’s investor who skips the footnotes, and the fault lies in how it is used, in Chapter 10’s processing layer (our judgment).13

13.11 Sentiment and its spread

Sentiment is optimism or pessimism unconnected with news about fundamentals that moves demand, one reading of Tetlock’s (2007) gloomy column of Section 2.1. In his data, a Wall Street Journal column and the Dow from 1984 to 1999, he concludes that the impact of negative sentiment on Dow returns is temporary and fully reversed within a week (working-paper version). Negative sentiment, he finds, seems to have a longer-lasting and larger impact on small stocks (working-paper version). The pessimism also follows the market: a 1% fall in the Dow’s return on the prior day is followed by a significant rise in pessimism equal to 5.8% of one standard deviation (working-paper version). His measure appears to correspond to either negative sentiment or risk aversion, and whether the media induces, amplifies or reflects investors’ interpretations he leaves unclear (working-paper version; Section 2.1).

Optimism can also spread from one stock to others. When EntreMed rose on old news in May 1998, other biotechnology stocks rose with it, some with no news of their own, and Huberman and Regev (2001) write that the optimism was contagious (Section 1.1).

This book keeps two kinds of spreading apart (our judgment). The spread of information changes what people know about θ\theta, and a price move that reflects it should last unless prices overreact (our judgment; Sections 13.4 and 15.4). The spread of sentiment changes demand and leaves what anyone knows about θ\theta unchanged. Stale news leaves a trace of the same kind, a price move that later reverses, as the Dow’s did in Tetlock’s 2007 data (our judgment; Section 10.7). Whether a move reverses or lasts is the test, though a reversal shows only that the move was temporary and by itself does not tell sentiment from double counting, from buying drawn in by attention or from a passing change in risk aversion (our judgment; Sections 2.1, 10.7 and 12.5). Chapter 16 takes up how to read a price that mixes sentiment with information.14

13.13 A trader’s checklist

None of these questions says what to buy or sell, and good answers do not make a trade safe (our judgment).

  1. How many independent sources lie behind what I hear? Check: trace each report to its origin and count the origins; five accounts passing on one report count as one (our judgment; Section 13.6.3). In Tetlock’s news on S&P 500 firms from 1984 to 2004, market reactions to stale stories partially reversed in the next week (working-paper version).
  2. Whose information does the consensus weigh? Check: in DeMarzo, Vayanos and Zwiebel’s model, with fixed relative weights, repeated averaging, a strongly connected group and weights on others that do not vanish too fast, everyone converges to a consensus weighted by network influence; when everyone judges precisions correctly, it is correct if and only if the listening structure is balanced, and typically it is not (working-paper version; Section 13.6.2). On the star of Figure 13.1 the hub’s starting belief counts three times as much as an outer person’s (our calculation).
  3. Is there a voice that never changes its mind? Check: in Figure 13.1, raising one outer person’s self-weight from 1/2 to 0.9 raised her share of the consensus from 1/8 to 5/12 (our calculation). Two stubborn sources with different views stop a consensus both under DeGroot’s averaging, where the others, if they listen to them directly or through others, settle at fixed beliefs (our derivation), and in Acemoglu and Ozdaglar’s model of meetings, where all opinions keep fluctuating (Section 13.8).
  4. Am I trading on information or on someone else’s mood? Check: demand that moves with public content and past returns and then reverses looks more like sentiment, though a reversal alone does not tell sentiment from double counting or from a passing change in risk aversion (our judgment; Section 13.11). In Tetlock’s data on one Wall Street Journal column and the Dow from 1984 to 1999, the pressure of media pessimism on the Dow fully reversed within a week, and on small stocks minus large stocks it seemed to last longer (working-paper version).
  5. Does my confidence follow my recent wins? Check: in Daniel, Hirshleifer and Subrahmanyam’s model, confidence that rises with confirming news and falls only modestly with disconfirming news gives unconditional short-run momentum and long-run reversal when overreaction or correction is sufficiently gradual; Hong and Stein’s model, with momentum traders restricted to simple strategies, gives the same pattern by another route (working-paper version), so the pattern does not single out self-attribution (our judgment; Section 13.4).

13.14 Summary

  • The root model copies one message, while here each person holds a noisy signal of the same fact and the question is how much of it the group pools; this chapter takes the non-Bayesian rules and Bayesian communication, beside Chapter 14’s Bayesian learning from actions (our judgment; Section 13.1).
  • In Aumann’s coin example, when neither person knows how many tosses lie behind the other’s posterior, the posteriors 2/3 and 1/3 may come from one toss or from four, and Aumann finds it unclear that a revision would necessarily make them equal, though by his result the exchange of posteriors continues until they are (Section 13.2).
  • In Daniel, Hirshleifer and Subrahmanyam’s model, overconfidence with a fixed confidence level makes price changes unconditionally negatively autocorrelated at short and long lags; with confidence that changes by biased self-attribution and sufficiently gradual overreaction or correction, it gives unconditional momentum followed by reversal, a pattern that Hong and Stein’s model also gives when its momentum traders are restricted to simple strategies (working-paper version; Section 13.4).
  • In Dhawan and Putniņš’s framework, with linear price impact, a selling place independent of the buying place and risk neutrality, people whose overconfidence passes a threshold join pumps; in their regression on 211 of 355 pumps of 2017 and 2018, a move of the group-level proxy from its 25th to its 75th percentile goes with 37.97% more participation (working-paper version), an association that on its own cannot separate overconfidence from recruitment (our judgment; Section 13.5).
  • In DeMarzo, Vayanos and Zwiebel’s model of repeated averaging with fixed relative weights, a strongly connected group whose weights on others do not vanish too fast converges to a consensus weighted by network influence, and when everyone judges precisions correctly, the consensus is correct if and only if the listening structure is balanced, which it typically is not (working-paper version; Section 13.6).
  • Sources that rarely or never change their minds come in three grades (our judgment): with forceful agents who listen now and then, Acemoglu and Ozdaglar’s survey reports a random consensus; with at least two fully stubborn agents of different views, disagreement and fluctuation persist in their model of meetings; this book places committed minorities in models with no truth (Section 13.8).
  • Opinion dynamics without a truth says which opinion prevails or how many clusters form, and the spread of sentiment changes demand without changing what anyone knows about θ\theta, so neither should be read as the spread of information (our judgment; Sections 13.9 and 13.11).
