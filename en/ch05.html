Chapter 5 The Root Model

On a prediction market, a binary contract pays $1 if an event happens and nothing otherwise, so its final value is 0 or 1. Before the outcome is known, its price moves as news arrives and opinions change. Across the contracts that Page and Clemen (2013) studied on the Intrade exchange (Section 1.3), prices were reasonably well calibrated, matching how often events happened, when the time to expiration was relatively short, but significantly biased for events further in the future.1

The root model is the book’s simplest information market: one yes-or-no fact, one message about it and one contract. Take the drug example of Section 2.3, whose contract pays $1 if a new drug passes its final trials and is priced at the prior, 0.30. A report that is right 80% of the time says success, and whoever hears of it puts the chance of success at about 0.632. Under the conditions of Section 5.3, the price is about 0.37 once 20% of traders have heard, 0.45 at 46% and 0.57 at 80% (our calculation; Figure 5.1).

5.1 The model in brief

The root model adopts all nineteen default assumptions, settings that later chapters drop one or more at a time to see what changes, which makes it the book’s reference point (our judgment). The defaults fall into four groups: about information itself (A), about how it spreads (B), about how people process it (C) and about prices and trading (D). Their codes, A1 to D6, appear only in this chapter and the appendix; the map of relaxations, Table 5.1 in Section 5.8, lists all nineteen, each with the chapters whose main relaxations drop it.

The root model has four parts (Box 5.1).

  • One fact. A yes-or-no fact θ\theta has a true value that trading does not change, the single exogenous fact (A1), and the contract settles on it when it expires.
  • One source. The informed all learn the same signal, which the book calls a single information source and treats as a candidate default, one kept outside the nineteen numbered defaults (Section 5.2).
  • Spreading in Bass form. As in Bass’s (1969) model of first purchases of new products, those who have not yet heard, the share 1−I1 - I, hear from an external source, such as the press or a filing, at the rate α\alpha, and by word of mouth, from those who know, at the rate βI\beta I (Section 1.5; Box 1.2).2
  • One binary contract on θ\theta. Under the conditions of Section 5.3, its price-implied probability, the chance the price gives the event, is the wealth-weighted average belief, the traders’ beliefs averaged with their wealth as weights, plus a disturbance from noise traders, who trade for reasons unrelated to the news. The informed hold the belief pp, the rest keep q0q_0, and no one learns from the price, the default of no learning from prices (C5), so both groups are newswatchers. At a deadline TT the fact is publicly disclosed and the contract settles, two events that Section 5.4 keeps apart.

Chapter 4 dropped two of the defaults: that getting and processing information costs nothing, unlimited attention (C4), and that someone always trades for reasons other than information, an exogenous counterparty (D3), and with them, in Grossman and Stiglitz’s example, no learning from prices (C5; Table 5.2). The root model puts all three back. Chapter 4 kept price taking (D1), no trader’s orders moving the price, as a premise of its definitions of value, and the root model keeps it too; Chapter 17 drops it.

5.2 The spread of the news

The informed fraction I(t)I(t), the share of people who have heard the news, starts at zero and grows as dIdt=(α+βI)(1−I),I(0)=0.\frac{dI}{dt} = (\alpha + \beta I)(1 - I), \qquad I(0) = 0. Only the 1−I1 - I who have not heard can newly hear. If word of mouth is stronger than the external source, β>α\beta > \alpha, the number newly hearing rises, peaks and falls, and II traces an S-curve, slow, then fast, then slow; with β≤α\beta \le \alpha it falls from the start (Section 1.5).

The equation stands for one case, a single source copied. All the informed have seen the same signal, and people copy the message that carries it, so the more who know, the faster each of the rest hears it; that self-acceleration needs what people copy to carry the information (our judgment). If people copy one another’s actions instead, actions can grow alike while information stalls, the information cascade of Chapter 14. Information spread over many people, to be pooled before use, lies outside the root model (Chapters 13, 14 and 19).

The book reads α\alpha as the rate of observing an external source, which fits the observation law, that new knowledge comes only from observing (Section 3.3); Bass read the same coefficient as people adopting independently of others, and both readings give the same total curve (our judgment; Section 1.5). An external source that sends the same message at a constant rate, with nothing announced later, is the default of one-time injection (B5), which Chapter 12 drops.3

Both α\alpha and β\beta are rates, so neither can be negative, though the coefficients of a regression in Bass form can be. Rzayev, Sakkas and Urquhart (2025) fit the Bass form to the daily number of active addresses of 77 cryptocurrencies. Estimated coin by coin, the coefficient in the place of α\alpha is negative for more mature coins such as Bitcoin, Ethereum and Litecoin, which the authors say suggests a different dynamic. These are coefficients fitted to activity, addresses in use each day, rather than the root model’s rates (our judgment; Chapter 22).4

The root model is also a projection of Duffie and Manso’s (2007) information percolation, in which people meet at random times at a common rate and pool their evidence. Under the conditions of Box 1.2, the share who have heard something, with pooled evidence other than zero, follows the root model with α=0\alpha = 0 and β\beta the meeting rate (our derivation), to be checked in the appendix (Section 19.1.1); the projection drops how much evidence each holds.5

Spreading is random for each person. Under permanent spreading (B3), those who know never stop telling, so the spread cannot die out early on, and chance only shifts the curve in time (our derivation). Under homogeneous mixing (B1), where anyone may meet anyone and two who meet draw on different sources, a cascade from a single seed can die out early with a chance that does not shrink as the population grows only if permanent spreading gives way to spontaneous stopping, spreaders falling silent after a while, whether or not those they meet already know (our derivation; Section 6.4); Sections 6.3 and 7.2 take up spreaders who stop on meeting others who already know. With an external source, α>0\alpha > 0, only a cascade from a single seed can die out, never the whole process (our derivation; Chapter 8).

Sultan, Farley and Lehmann (1990) pooled 213 sets of estimates of diffusion models from 15 articles, all fitted to annual data. Bass’s coefficients of innovation and imitation, which stand where α\alpha and β\beta do, average .03 and .38, though values vary considerably. Reading them as the root model’s rates, for the shape and time scale of the curve, is our judgment, and the chapter cites no estimate of them for news; the fitted coefficient of imitation mixes in marketing and other outside influences, so it cannot be read as the strength of word of mouth (our judgment). On that reading the means give a ratio of the imitation to the innovation coefficient of about 12.7, a time scale 1/(α+β)1/(\alpha + \beta) of about 2.4 years and the most new adopters at about 6.2 years (our calculation), by the authors’ formula for the peak, though they print 5.3 years. Across the single-factor cells of their Table 2, the ratio runs from about 5 to 20, the peak from 3.4 to 6.6 years, and 40% to 47% have adopted at the peak (our derivation).6

Scope. The estimates cover only new products that had at least some market success, fitted to annual data on first purchases. The ranges 5 to 20, 3.4 to 6.6 years and 40% to 47% are the book’s conversion of the single-factor cells of Table 2 (our derivation); the authors report no distribution of the 213 estimates. The coefficient of imitation mixes in marketing and other outside influences and cannot be read as the strength of word of mouth. The peak time quoted is 6.2 years, from the authors’ own formula; the paper prints 5.3 years.

5.3 The price rule and its limits

With equal wealth, and the noise traders’ demand set aside, the price at time tt is qt=I(t)p+[1−I(t)]q0.q_t = I(t)\,p + [1 - I(t)]\,q_0. In Wolfers and Zitzewitz’s (2006) model, prices are a wealth-weighted average of beliefs when traders take the price as given, have no motive to hedge and have log utility, valuing wealth by its logarithm (working-paper version). The rule adds equal wealth that stays fixed, part of the default of log utility (D2); noise traders (D3); a fact unrelated to aggregate risk, the risk of the market as a whole (D5); no learning from prices (C5); and short selling being allowed, part of frictionless trading (D4). Section 1.5 derives the rule, which has six limits.7

  • It needs short selling, selling borrowed shares. In Miller’s (1977) setting, a market with restricted short selling and differing opinions, the price can sit above the average belief (our judgment; Section 20.1.3).
  • It is one of three channels through which awareness reaches a price, beside risk sharing among the investors who know a stock (working-paper version) and Miller’s hypothesis that, in such a market, awareness raises a price (Section 1.5); Section 19.3.6 sets the three side by side. In the root model’s price, the share each person’s starting information gets, its influence weight, is set by wealth; in models that average opinions repeatedly over a network it is set by network position (Chapter 13), and in Pedersen’s model, which adds prices, by thought leadership, a share of wealth plus naive agents’ attention (Section 19.3.4); neither need match how precise the information is (our judgment).
  • Moving the price continuously with the informed fraction is a constitutive assumption, a rule the model chooses for how the price responds to what people know, which another rule could replace (Section 1.5). Under a rule like Abreu and Brunnermeier’s (2003), the price can stay put after enough people to correct a mispricing already know (Section 1.6.3; Chapter 21).
  • It assumes one market with one public price, a candidate default the book calls centralized trading with public prices. In Duffie and Manso’s private auctions, bids reveal information only to the bidders, so trading itself spreads it (Chapter 19).
  • It takes noise traders’ demand to be unpredictable to everyone, the default of an exogenous counterparty. If public content predicts that demand and the push it gives the price later reverses, knowing future prices can pay too (our judgment; Sections 2.5 and 4.1).
  • It has no frictions, so the calibration gaps of prediction markets cannot be read directly as a gap between qq and pp: the cost of money tied up, fees and the bid–ask spread, the gap between the best offers to buy and to sell, come off first (Section 1.3; Chapter 18).8

Knowing, acting and the price are three quantities, to be measured and modeled apart. The root model ties them together, since the informed trade on pp as soon as they know and the price moves with the informed fraction. People who know may still not act, for at least five reasons (our judgment): a belief threshold, waiting to hear from several sources (Chapter 11); strategic timing, waiting until enough others know to correct a mispricing, yet wanting to move first (Chapter 21); a friction threshold, fixed trading costs that mild news cannot cover (Chapter 21); waiting to learn from those who move first (Chapter 14); and an attention cycle, only some investors being present to decide at any moment (Chapter 19).

5.4 Predictions of the model

Differentiating the price rule gives its drift, dqdt=(p−q0)dIdt,\frac{dq}{dt} = (p - q_0)\,\frac{dI}{dt}, so the price path is predictable to anyone who knows pp, q0q_0 and I(t)I(t), and the price moves fastest when the most people are newly hearing (Figure 5.1). No market reports I(t)I(t); search counts and other attention data are possible stand-ins, with the cautions of Chapter 10 (our judgment). Since the price carries noise, the predictable part is a statistical arbitrage, a trade that pays on average but carries risk each time. It does not break the no-arbitrage law, by which public information alone offers no gain without risk or capital (Section 3.7), subject to the qualifications below.

  • The market price of risk, the extra expected return per unit of risk, has to be square-integrable, its square summed over time staying finite. That condition is borrowed by analogy from Harrison and Kreps’s (1979) Theorem 3; it is not a direct application of the theorem (our judgment; Box 5.1).
  • The root model ends at TT, and whoever holds to TT bears only the risk in θ\theta, none in the resale price, which implies a candidate default, holding until disclosure (our judgment). A holder who must sell before the news comes out bears the resale price’s risk instead, a form of noise trader risk, the risk that noise traders push the price further off first (our judgment; Chapter 23).
  • With frictions, a predictable move without risk can also be no arbitrage, while it stays within a band set by the frictions; two examples of Jouini and Kallal give its two halves. With a bid–ask spread (1995b), prices that at one point have no volatility and a drift unequal to the riskless rate, an arbitrage without frictions, offer none once the bid is strictly below the ask. With short sellers paying a cost cc in return (1995a), a security that earns less than the lending rate even in its better state offers none while that shortfall stays below cc, which follows from their condition (our derivation). A risky drift is not bound by this band (our judgment); the root model has no frictions, and the band matters where frictionless trading is dropped (Chapters 20 and 23).9

The drift comes from the orders of those who hear later and trade on news about θ\theta as it reaches them, so whoever knows I(t)I(t) knows who will buy next (our judgment). Section 4.1 called this information about order flow, the net of buy and sell orders. Section 4.1’s pumps, group buying announced in advance, are a counterexample to the root model’s account of value, which counts only information about θ\theta and so assumes holding to settlement or unpredictable noise demand: early buyers knew later buyers’ orders, with no information about the coin’s value (our judgment; Section 4.1).

Settlement, when the contract pays out on how θ\theta turned out, clears all information whoever is watching, while a public disclosure reaches the price only through those who see it (our judgment; Section 1.5). A deadline that means settlement thus does not depend on unlimited attention, and one that means disclosure does (Chapters 10 and 12).

Value follows the two definitions of Section 4.2. Under definition one, a signal’s worth at the same prices against publicly available information, the root model gives exactly the amount of information, because a binary contract amounts to a two-horse race, a bet on either outcome at odds with no bookmaker’s cut, and is complete by construction, which implies the candidate default of a complete market (Section 4.2 gives the other premises). Definition two, betting a correct belief against the price, adds the price’s own error, D(π∥q)D(\pi \| q), from news the price-setters have seen but not absorbed correctly; measured against the same information, the two differ by exactly that term and agree only when the price is calibrated (Box 5.1). The root model also assumes the public cannot work out pp from the price (Box 1.1).

5.5 Range of application and time scales

The root model fits three cases best (our judgment; Section 1.5): soft information, which is hard to quantify; private information; and assets that draw little attention. Four time scales, the time a process takes to run most of its course, mark the edges of its range, each lying outside word of mouth (our judgment): delays in a first reaction to broadcast news or in a pump come from attention and execution, the timing of a drift after earnings news partly from the calendar of announcements, and the speed of a recovery from when capital is present to trade. Spreading’s own time scale, 1/(α+β)1/(\alpha + \beta), is about 2.4 years at the mean coefficients for successful new products, read as the root model’s rates (our calculation; Section 5.2).

Busse and Green (2002) videotaped CNBC’s two segments of analysts’ stock reports from June 12 to October 27, 2000. They find that prices of stocks discussed positively began to respond seconds after the first mention and kept responding for approximately one minute.10

Scope. This holds only for the 125 positive reports of the midday segment; after positive morning reports, the first minute’s return averaged only 6.8 basis points, against 41 basis points after midday ones, and the morning half-hour return was negative. Midday prices began to respond within 15 seconds and finished in about a minute. More than 80% of the reports were on firms in the top two NYSE size deciles, so the authors call their results an upper bound on the speed with which prices respond to information. The roughly 15 minutes after negative reports rest on only 19 morning and 23 midday reports, 42 in all, and may be part of a gradual response to other negative public news, so they are not read as a speed of spreading. Tick sizes and execution speeds in 2000 differed from today’s.

In Dhawan and Putniņš’s 355 pumps on two exchanges in 2017 and 2018, prices took around eight minutes on average to peak after the signal, with a median of 1.54 minutes (working-paper version; Section 4.1); the book has not yet tested whether price impact accounts for the size of the peaks. Bernard and Thomas (1989) measured post-earnings-announcement drift, prices moving for months in the direction of an earnings surprise, in NYSE and AMEX stocks from 1974 to 1986: assuming that all of it occurs within 480 days, approximately 100% occurs within nine months for small firms and six months for large ones; the book has not yet tested an account of this timing from separately measured inputs. Duffie (2010) writes that the arrival of new capital can be delayed by fractions of a second in an electronic limit-order book and by months in catastrophe risk insurance; in his simple illustrative model of slow-moving capital, prices recover from a supply shock as that capital arrives, and the book has not yet tested the recovery time in his model.

5.6 Four sources of S-curves

Young (2009) sorts diffusion models into contagion, social influence and social learning; models driven mainly by outside factors such as price and quality, sometimes called moving equilibrium models, depend on how those factors change (working-paper version). The book counts these as a fourth source of S-curves and tells the four apart by their footprints, since a fit alone cannot (our judgment).11

  • Contagion. People adopt on contact with adopters (working-paper version), as in the root model, over a time scale near 1/(α+β)1/(\alpha + \beta); Bass’s imitation term belongs here (our judgment; Section 5.2). With a positive external influence and rates that differ across people but not over time, the relative hazard rate, new adoption divided by the share adopted times the share not yet adopted, decreases, so the process cannot accelerate beyond half adopted (working-paper version).
  • Social influence. People adopt once enough others in the group have (working-paper version). If adoption driven purely by social influence speeds up at first, it typically grows faster than exponentially for a while, and it peters out near the first fixed point of the distribution of thresholds, where adoption triggers no one new (working-paper version).
  • Social learning. People adopt once earlier adopters’ outcomes convince them (working-paper version). Adoption driven purely by social learning usually begins slowly and may even decelerate at first, and its speed rises with the expected payoff (working-paper version).
  • Moving equilibrium. Adoption depends only on the current values of the outside factors and reverses when they fall back (our judgment).

The footprints are whether a public disclosure cuts the curve short, whether adoption reverses when outside factors fall back, and whether it tracks only their current values; a rising relative hazard rate points to thresholds, learning or a changing external source (our judgment). Growth faster than exponential cannot by itself rule out contagion: Young’s Proposition 1 concerns only the relative hazard rate, and contagion that differs across people can grow that fast (our derivation). A test of shape needs a fixed external source and frequent data from the start; an external source that moves with the price has to be measured first (our judgment; Chapter 22).

In DeGroot learning, people update their beliefs by listening to others in their social network, taking weighted averages. With no βI\beta I term, how fast a view spreads does not depend on how many hold it, so this word of mouth does not speed itself up (our judgment; Chapters 13 and 19).

5.7 The first paradigm switch

Dropping no learning from prices (C5) is the book’s first paradigm switch, a relaxation that changes the state space or the equilibrium concept, here the latter, since beliefs then depend on the price they help set (our judgment).

The new equilibrium need not reveal more. In Manski’s (2006) prediction market, traders are risk-neutral price takers with differing beliefs, budgets are independent of revised beliefs, and each revises his belief on seeing the price, staying on the same side of it, for example by averaging it with the price; then “the equilibrium price using posterior beliefs is the same as the one using prior beliefs” (working-paper version). Collapse into full revelation, a price that expresses everyone’s private information, comes only when traders learn the rational-expectations way, inferring from the price what others know, with a common prior and Bayes’ rule (our judgment; Box 5.2).12

With such learning and the noise traders (D3) dropped as well, Grossman (1976) finds that the equilibrium price summarizes all the information in the market. This full revelation needs eight premises, listed in Box 5.2, including constant absolute risk aversion, a dislike of risk that does not change with wealth, with normal distributions; a known total supply with no noise; and price taking. It describes the limit as noise vanishes: Hellwig (1980) shows that Grossman’s result approximately describes communication through the market when supply noise is small and that with no noise “the communication process simply is not well defined”. Milgrom (1981) adds that “naive mechanisms leading to such equilibria can be severely manipulable”, even by a single trader.

In the root model the collapse would be a relay, the price passing the informed traders’ common pp to the rest: working pp out of the price needs I(t)I(t) (Box 5.1), but with no noise and pp one of two known values, the direction of the price’s move from q0q_0 reveals which (our derivation; Box 5.2). One-dimensional uncertainty and an uninformed public that knows I(t)I(t) thus matter to how revelation proceeds only when noise is kept (our derivation).

Dropping only the noise traders, without rational-expectations learning, still leaves trade, the naive case of Section 4.4; the no-trade theorem needs every premise listed there. With such learning and no noise, each trader’s equilibrium demand depends only on the price, and the private incentive to collect information goes.

Rational expectations need the noise kept, and the root model has it (our judgment). In a large market with noise, the price reflects only information common to many agents, and individuals, unable to tell a high payoff from a low supply, use their own signals as well as the price. This is partial revelation, the price showing only part of what is known. How revelation proceeds then depends on how many unknowns the uninformed face (our judgment): θ\theta (or pp), I(t)I(t), and whether there is news at all, which Easley and O’Hara (1992) call event uncertainty (Chapter 17). In Avery and Zemsky’s model, herding, many acting alike without judging independently, becomes prevalent as the dimensions of uncertainty increase (working-paper version). With three unknowns and one price, revelation in the root model may be neither instant nor monotone (our derivation); the question stays open, unchecked against the models of Chapters 16 and 19.

Reaching Andrei and Cujean’s (2017) model takes four more changes, set out in Box 5.2 (our judgment): signals pooled in random meetings, leaving no informed fraction and no S-curve; constant absolute risk aversion; a risk premium; and inference from prices in place of first-order knowledge (A3), knowing only the fact. Their meetings are uniformly random, so homogeneous mixing stays (our judgment). In their model momentum, recent winners continuing to rise, needs information to flow at an increasing rate; Box 19.3 puts the root model’s Bass curve into that condition as the book’s own modeling (our derivation). Chapter 19 sets this route beside Pedersen’s (2022); tying a real spreading process to prices remains an open problem of the book (Chapters 19 and 21).

5.10 A trader’s checklist

None of these questions says what to buy or sell, and good answers do not make a trade safe (our judgment).

  1. One message or many signals? Check: are people passing on one message carrying the news, or does each hold a signal to be pooled (Section 5.2)? The root model covers only the first.
  2. Is the curve contagion? Testable: in Young’s model, with a positive external influence and rates that differ across people but not over time, the relative hazard rate decreases (working-paper version). A rising one points to thresholds, learning or a changing external source; the test needs a fixed external source and frequent data from the start (our judgment; Section 5.6).
  3. Whose orders make the drift? Check: who will buy after you (Section 5.4)? In the root model, later hearers trading on news about θ\theta; in Section 4.1’s pumps, buyers answering a signal with no information about the coin’s value (our judgment).
  4. Which clock runs? Testable: time how fast prices respond to the broadcasts you trade on. After CNBC’s positive midday reports in 2000, mostly on large firms, prices kept responding for about a minute, an upper bound on speed in the authors’ reading (Section 5.5); if those prices respond as fast, they have finished within about a minute of the broadcast (our inference).
  5. Which default fails first? Check: find in Table 5.1 the default your market breaks first and read the chapters the table lists for it.

5.11 Summary

  • The root model has one yes-or-no fact, one message copied from a single source and spreading in Bass form, and one binary contract. It adopts all nineteen default assumptions, restoring the three that Chapter 4 dropped, unlimited attention, an exogenous counterparty and, in Grossman and Stiglitz’s example, no learning from prices, and keeping price taking.
  • Read as the root model’s rates, Sultan, Farley and Lehmann’s coefficients for successful new products, on annual data on first purchases and with the coefficient of imitation mixing in marketing, put the peak of new adoption 3.4 to 6.6 years out across the single-factor cells of their Table 2 (our derivation; Section 5.2).
  • Under the premises of Section 5.3 the price is the wealth-weighted average belief, with six limits, among them the constitutive assumption that it moves continuously with the informed fraction.
  • The price’s drift comes from later hearers’ orders and offers whoever knows pp, q0q_0 and the unreported I(t)I(t) a risky statistical arbitrage, consistent with the no-arbitrage law under the qualifications of Section 5.4; Section 4.1’s pumps are a counterexample to the model’s account of value (our judgment; Section 5.4).
  • After CNBC’s 125 positive midday reports in 2000, mostly on large firms, prices began to respond within 15 seconds and kept responding for about a minute, an upper bound on speed in the authors’ reading.
  • S-curves have four sources, told apart by their footprints; a test of shape needs a fixed external source and frequent data from the start (our judgment).
  • Dropping no learning from prices need not move the price, as under Manski’s premises (working-paper version). Full revelation needs rational-expectations learning (our judgment); Grossman’s needs eight premises, among them no noise; Hellwig shows it describes the limit as noise vanishes.

Box 5.2 The first paradigm switch in symbols

Full revelation. Grossman’s (1976) price summarizes all the information in the market under eight premises: (1) constant absolute risk aversion, Ui(W̃1i)=−e−aiW̃1iU_i(\tilde{W}_{1i}) = -e^{-a_i \tilde{W}_{1i}} with ai>0a_i > 0; (2) a normal prior and normal signals, (3) whose errors are independent across traders and of equal precision; (4) a common prior, with every trader knowing how the price depends on the signals, argued as a self-fulfilling expectation; (5) a known total supply, since with a random supply the price cannot reveal the average signal; (6) price taking; (7) prices with at least as many dimensions as the sufficient statistic, a summary that keeps all the signals say about the payoff; and (8) an equilibrium shown unique only among linear functions of the signals: “We do not know whether” there are others.

The limit. As supply noise disappears, Hellwig’s (1980) equilibrium price becomes a sufficient statistic for the signals, but Grossman’s result is not exact at zero noise (Section 5.7).

Unknowns. The uninformed face θ\theta (or pp), I(t)I(t) and whether there is news, three unknowns for one price; with no noise and p∈{pH,pL}p \in \{p_H, p_L\} known, sign⁡(qt−q0)\operatorname{sign}(q_t - q_0) reveals which, whatever I(t)I(t) (our derivation).

Andrei and Cujean’s four changes. Agents meet at Poisson times with intensity λ\lambda and pool their signals; utility is exponential (D2), with risk aversion 1/τ1/\tau, their τ\tau being risk tolerance, a different quantity from the time τ\tau of Box 5.1; the price carries a risk premium for noise trading risk (D5); and prices carry public information, from which agents infer what others know (A3).15
