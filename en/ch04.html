Chapter 4 The Value of Information

Most cryptocurrency pump groups, which gather people to buy a coin together and push its price up, reach their members through Telegram or Discord (working-paper version). In the pump that Dhawan and Putniņš (2023) use to illustrate the mechanics, the administrators first announce the exchange, date and time, but not the coin; at that time comes the “pump signal”, just the coin’s name (working-paper version). The organizers openly declare their intention to pump, and in a random sample of 70 pumps whose messages the authors sorted by hand, none carries any suggestion that the coin is undervalued (working-paper version). Early buyers gain with no information about the coin’s value; what they know is that others are about to buy (our judgment).1

4.1 A pump announced in advance

Dhawan and Putniņš identify from hand-collected data 355 cases of pump-and-dump manipulation, a pump followed by a sell-off, in seven months on two cryptocurrency exchanges: 64 on Binance and 291 on Yobit, from December 29, 2017, to June 22, 2018 (working-paper version). They take them from the chat histories of Telegram pump channels, keeping only events in which the administrator says explicitly that the coin is to be pumped (working-paper version). On average a pump takes around eight minutes from signal to peak, with a median of 1.54 minutes, and returns 65.47%; prices then return to their pre-pump levels, which the authors find consistent with their theory (working-paper version). The authors estimate that the administrators buy around $24.38 million of coins in the two hours before the pumps, for a conservatively estimated profit of around $6.04 million, which they read as a wealth transfer from the followers to the administrators (working-paper version).2

In their framework each order moves the price by the same step, and small random delays in sending orders decide each buyer’s place in the queue, the later places paying more (working-paper version). A participant who does not know her place in advance expects a loss, while the administrators gain by buying before the signal (working-paper version); only buyers within the first half of the queue expect to gain (our derivation).3

The finding that no message suggested undervaluation covers only the 70 sampled pumps (working-paper version). The authors write that their evidence suggests a gambling game in which players compete to buy ahead of others and sell ahead of others, and the administrators’ profit is a conservative estimate (working-paper version).4

Write θ\theta for the fact traders want to know, here what the coin is worth. The coin’s name carries no information about θ\theta, so as information about θ\theta (Section 4.2) the signal is worth nothing, and what early buyers know is the later buyers’ orders (our judgment). Valuing information about θ\theta assumes that the trader holds to settlement, keeping the asset until it pays out on how the fact turns out, as a contract on a drug trial pays on the trial’s result, or that trading unrelated to θ\theta is unpredictable to her (Section 1.5). In a pump neither holds, since no one holds to settlement and once the signal goes out the later buyers’ orders are predictable (our judgment). The book calls this information about others’ order flow, the net of their buy and sell orders. The rest of the chapter counts only the value of information about θ\theta, under one of the two assumptions.

4.2 Two definitions of value

Definition one is how much faster a trader’s wealth grows with a signal than without it, at the same prices. Definition two is how fast it grows when she bets a correct belief against the price on every outcome at margin-free odds, with no bookmaker’s cut, so it also counts the price’s own error, which a bettor without the signal who knows what the price-setters have seen collects too.

For side information YY, such as world events or the behavior of a correlated market, Barron and Cover (1988) define the first as the increase Δ\Delta in the doubling rate, the best long-run growth of wealth, counted in doublings per period. The book measures what YY adds against what the investor without it actually uses, its conditioning set: by default publicly available information, what anyone could look up, or, if she has seen only part of it, that part. With XX the returns, each asset’s price at the end of a period over its price at the start, 0≤Δ=W(X∣Y)−W(X)≤I(X;Y),0 \le \Delta = W(X \mid Y) - W(X) \le I(X; Y), where WW is the doubling rate and II the mutual information (Section 2.3). In a general stock market only this bound holds; each bit of information at most doubles wealth, and in the example of Section 2.6 a full bit adds nothing, since the best portfolio is the same with it or without it.

In a horse-race market, where each outcome has its own contract paying its odds if that outcome wins, Δ\Delta equals the mutual information and the bound is tight, whatever the odds. The equality has five premises: a separate bet on every outcome; all wealth invested, or margin-free odds, so that money spread over the outcomes in proportion to their prices works as cash; odds that do not change with the signal, as in Kelly’s (1956) setting; a price taker, whose bets do not move the odds; and a known joint distribution of signal and outcome. It needs no calibrated price, one equal to the correct probability. Reading a growth rate as a value needs log utility, valuing wealth by its logarithm, for a one-off chance, and independent repetition with reinvestment for a repeated one (Box 1.1).5

Definition two is Chapter 1’s information gradient, averaged before the signal is seen: 𝔼Y[D(pY∥q)]=I(θ;Y∣ℱseen)+D(π∥q)\mathbb{E}_Y[D(p_Y \| q)] = I(\theta; Y \mid \mathcal{F}^{\text{seen}}) + D(\pi \| q) (Box 1.1). Here ℱseen\mathcal{F}^{\text{seen}} is what the price-setters have seen and π\pi the correct probability given it. The first term, the undiffused information, is what the signal tells beyond what they have seen; the second, the undigested information, is the price’s own error. This definition equals the amount of information only if the price is also calibrated, q=πq = \pi. The book’s information gradient takes this definition, measured against what the price-setters have seen rather than all publicly available information (Chapter 10). After the outcome, the realized value D(r∥q)−D(r∥p)D(r \| q) - D(r \| p), for a truth rr and a belief pp, can be negative for someone who only thinks she is informed (Section 1.4).

The two definitions compare directly only on the same conditioning set. There definition two exceeds definition one by exactly D(π∥q)D(\pi \| q) in a horse-race market, and by at least that much in a general market (our derivation; Box 4.1). Reading definition two as a value needs a separate bet on every outcome (Box 1.1). Chapters 1–5 assume that the fact traded on is unrelated to the risk of the market as a whole, which makes D(π∥q)D(\pi \| q) pure mispricing; Chapter 18 splits off the risk premium, the reward for bearing risk.

Take Chapter 2’s drug report, with a prior of 0.30 and a report right 80% of the time (Section 2.3), and consider bets on success or failure at margin-free odds, a two-horse race. On the same conditioning set, a bettor without the report knows what the price-setters have seen and holds 0.30. At a price of 0.30 both definitions give 0.236 bits per bet. Over many independent bets with winnings reinvested, the bettor with the report outgrows one without it by a factor of about 20.236≈1.182^{0.236} \approx 1.18 per bet, some 18% (our calculation). At 0.20 definition one gives 0.236 and definition two 0.277; the extra 0.041 is the price’s own error. At 0.40 the extra is 0.031 (our calculation; Figure 4.1, Table 4.1; Box 4.1, in Section 4.12).

4.3 Holding to settlement and trading before revaluation

Section 3.4 called foreknowledge, knowing early what everyone will see in due time, an advantage of timing alone. Hirshleifer (1971) gives numbers in a model with perfect and costless markets, whose “representative” individuals share beliefs, utility functions and endowments, so no one trades and prices must sustain everyone’s endowment. Of two future states, aa is better endowed and has a chance of 0.6, and bb a chance of 0.4; utility is logarithmic.

One individual then learns, privately, in advance and for sure, that state aa will obtain. Holding to settlement, he sells at the ruling price his claims on state bb, which he alone knows to be worthless, since his choices barely affect prices. Trading before revaluation, which Hirshleifer calls speculation, he instead converts all his wealth into claims on aa at the old prices, expecting the news to become public, and the price of a claim on aa to rise, before decisions are final. Averaged over news naming aa or bb, each channel adds 0.6730 to his expected utility, the second on top of the first, and 0.6730 nats, or 0.971 bits, is the entropy of the state, the uncertainty about which will obtain (our calculation).6 Box 4.1 shows why, and why the two channels differ for a signal that is not sure (our derivation).

Both channels assume perfect markets, log utility, a price taker and counterparties who do not infer the news from his trades (our judgment; Section 4.4); the second also needs the news to come out before consumption decisions. The second channel is a case of Chapter 3’s judgment that others’ learning can raise the value of information held for less time than the news takes to come out; Hirshleifer’s model, with one price-taking informed person, says nothing about many holders leaving together (our judgment).

Made public to all before decisions are made, the same news moves the price at once, everyone consumes as before, and expected utility, averaged over the news, does not change. Such information is of no value to the community, which under pure exchange, with no production, gains nothing from acquiring or spreading private foreknowledge (Section 4.7).

Accuracy can also pay in the long run, as a larger share of wealth, under the premises below, though over a single period the trader with more accurate beliefs can do worse (our judgment). In Blume and Easley’s (2006) model, in a Pareto optimal allocation, one that no change can improve for someone without hurting someone else, and with equal discount factors, how much each values the future, those whose beliefs are closest to the truth end up with all the resources, whatever their attitudes toward risk, so correct beliefs are selected whenever markets are complete, with a security for every state; with incomplete markets these conclusions may not hold (working-paper version). Section 18.4 takes up this market selection, the weeding out of wrong beliefs by trading.7

4.4 The no-trade theorem

Milgrom and Stokey’s (1982) central result, the no-trade theorem (Section 1.6.3), is that, whatever the institutions, if the initial allocation is ex ante Pareto optimal, private information cannot create any incentive to trade: if it is common knowledge that a trade is feasible and acceptable to all, weakly risk-averse traders are indifferent to it, and strictly risk-averse ones make none. The premises are these.

  • An ex ante Pareto optimal allocation, one that, judged before any news, no trade could improve for someone without hurting someone else, rules out trading for insurance or liquidity.
  • Concordant beliefs, agreement on how likely each signal is in each state, leave priors free to differ; a common prior is not a premise.
  • Strict risk aversion rules out trades that weakly risk-averse traders merely find no worse.
  • Common knowledge, known to all, known by all to be known, and so on, that a trade is feasible and acceptable rules out the naive reasoning below; each knowing it is not enough.
  • Cash flows fixed from outside rule out decision makers who learn from the terms of trade and change the cash flows, which Bond and Eraslan (2010) show can produce purely informational trade, as Bond, Edmans and Goldstein (2012) report (working-paper version).

In Milgrom and Stokey’s example, two risk-neutral traders, each told only which of a few ranges the drawn signal falls in, are offered a one-dollar bet on which of two states holds. Given that draw, naively, each puts his chance of winning at 2/3 and accepts; with rational expectations, each inferring what the other’s acceptance reveals, both accept only at that value of the signal, where each is indifferent, and slightly risk-averse traders decline.

Hirshleifer’s economy of Section 4.3, with log utility and identical endowments and beliefs, meets the first three premises (our judgment). Milgrom and Stokey note that his case for speculating profitably at virtually unchanged prices assumes an individual “small” relative to the market; with rational expectations “a trader with new information is never ‘small’”. So the 0.6730 per channel is a gain against naive counterparties, who do not infer the news from his trades; the no-trade theorem brings it to zero, but only when all its premises hold (our judgment).

In real markets trade goes on because some premise fails, as when an exogenous counterparty trades for reasons other than information, such as a fund meeting withdrawals or a farmer hedging a harvest (our judgment). Without one, traders short of rational expectations still trade, as in the naive case. In Dhawan and Putniņš’s framework, when all participants are alike, rational individuals with correct beliefs do not take part in pumps, and their evidence suggests a gambling game (working-paper version; Section 4.1). Gambling motives can sustain trade without exogenous noise traders, who trade at random (our judgment).

In Milgrom’s (1981) auction of kk identical objects, each sold at the highest losing bid, a bidder whose information adds nothing to what each of at least kk rivals knows, and who values an object no more than they do, cannot expect a positive payoff however he bids: “Poorly informed speculators can only lose”. Trade continues because the sellers play a purely passive role, an exogenous counterparty (our judgment). Information is valued against what the marginal competitors who set the price know (our judgment).8

4.5 Information that costs money

In Grossman and Stiglitz’s (1980) argument (Section 1.6.6), if the price passes on what the informed know, each of them, taking the price as given, thinks he could stop paying for information and do as well; if no one is informed, the price shows nothing and, if information is cheap enough, each wants to become informed. They proved this only in an example with normal variables and a dislike of risk that does not change with wealth (Chapter 16). There the risky asset’s return has a part observable at a cost and an unobservable part; the informed see the first, the uninformed only the price, and the only noise in the price is random change in the asset’s supply per person, the supply noise. By their Theorem 5, with no supply noise, no equilibrium of price-taking traders exists if, and only if, the cost is below a bound that rises with the signal-to-noise ratio, the variance of the observable part over that of the unobservable part, and falls with risk aversion; with perfect information there is never one. The bound is what the signal is worth when the price carries no information, its information about the return divided by risk aversion, as a certainty equivalent, the sure sum worth as much (our derivation; Box 4.1).

Milgrom (1981) argues that the root cause of this non-existence, the Grossman–Stiglitz paradox, is probably the definition of price taking. In a special example built to show this, an auction of one object with uniformly distributed value and signals, the price each bidder faces reveals all his competitors’ information but not his own, and an equilibrium exists at every cost of information, which suggests that non-existence needs a trader who sees his own information in the price yet takes the price as given (our judgment). In the same auction, a setting unlike a continuously traded market, with every bidder informed, one bidder’s expected profit falls from about 0.096 times the object’s highest possible value with two bidders to about 0.0046 times with ten, against half that value for a lone informed bidder (our calculation); here the more informative the price, the smaller the rent, which stays above zero (our judgment).

In an interior equilibrium informed and uninformed traders coexist and expect equal utility. By their equation (13), being informed is worth, in certainty equivalent and before its cost, the information the signal adds to the price divided by risk aversion, so that information then equals risk aversion times the cost, in nats, for buying or not buying a signal of fixed precision (our derivation; Box 4.1). This equality involves only risk aversion and cost; the price’s informativeness in equilibrium also depends on the signal-to-noise ratio, and neither depends on the supply noise. In Verrecchia’s (1982) model, where traders choose the precision at a convex cost, the price becomes more informative as supply noise falls, unlike in Grossman and Stiglitz, where two effects exactly offset.

Admati and Pfleiderer (1990), on selling information, write that if any positive fraction of traders knows the payoff perfectly, the price is fully revealing and “information has no value and the seller’s profits are zero”, which we read as the paradox on the seller’s side (our judgment; Chapter 15).9

4.6 Price impact and the value of trading

Both definitions of Section 4.2 assume a price taker. In Kyle’s (1985) model (Section 1.6.2; Chapter 17), a single risk-neutral insider, a trader with private information, hides behind noise traders. His expected profits, which equal the noise traders’ expected losses, grow in proportion to the noise trading he can hide in, while the share of his information that reaches the price does not depend on it. The value of his information comes apart from its amount; the proportionality holds only for a single risk-neutral insider whose information is given from outside (our judgment).

In Kyle’s (1989) model, an informed speculator who recognizes his price impact, how far his own trades move the price, restricts his trading like a monopolist, making it less responsive to his private information, so that with imperfect competition “prices never reveal more than one-half the private precision of informed speculators”. Of two speculators with the same amount of information beyond what the price shows, one who knows his orders move the price gains less from it, as a certainty equivalent, than one who takes the price as given, where Kyle’s Theorem 10.1 applies (our derivation; Box 4.1); the two equilibria reveal different amounts, so the comparison holds fixed the amount beyond the price rather than the signal. When noise trading is exactly zero and traders are not risk neutral, the market shuts down, with no trade and no transaction prices, and his proofs certainly do not apply to that case in a straightforward manner. Imperfect competition therefore avoids the Grossman–Stiglitz paradox only when noise trading is positive (our judgment).10

4.7 Private and social value

Social value, the value of information to the economy as a whole, comes only from the real decisions it changes, such as production and investment. Hirshleifer (1971) writes that information is of value only if it can affect action, and that the wealth shifts due to price revaluations are purely redistributive. His results run along two lines, private or public and pure exchange or production: private information that remains private has no social value, and with production, public information on which state will obtain does. The information gradient and both definitions of Section 4.2 measure private value.

Public information can lower welfare in at least four ways.

  • Coordination. In Morris and Shin’s (2002) model people want their actions to suit the fundamentals and to match one another’s; a weight measures how much the second motive, second-guessing the others, counts relative to the first, and that motive is socially inefficient, being zero-sum; and “public” means that the signal’s realization is common knowledge (working-paper version). With independent private information, more public information has an ambiguous effect on welfare (working-paper version). When that weight exceeds 0.5, there are ranges of the parameters where more precise public information lowers welfare, and it helps only when private information is not very precise (working-paper version; Chapter 21).
  • Others’ actions. In Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer and Welch’s (1992) model, public information released before the first person decides can make some people worse off in an ex ante sense, since besides informing directly it changes earlier decisions and what they convey; their example uses very noisy public information (Chapter 14).
  • Distributive risk. Under pure exchange, if the true state is announced before any trading, while people still hold their endowments, those holding more claims on one state cannot be sure which way the news will point; Hirshleifer states that risk-averse people will then prefer that it not be released, its prospect adding distributive risk to the risk of which state will obtain.
  • Less sharing. In Duffie, Malamud and Manso’s (2009) model, public signals given to new entrants reduce the sharing of private information and, with proportional search costs, in some cases lower welfare judged at entry (Chapter 19).

Bond, Edmans and Goldstein (2012) call a price’s power to forecast a firm’s value forecasting price efficiency, and the extent to which prices reveal the information needed for efficient real decisions revelatory price efficiency; they report Dow and Gorton’s (1997) point that prices can quite possibly be efficient in the first sense and not the second (working-paper version). The information gradient, a forecasting quantity measuring private value, can move apart from revelatory price efficiency, since the two differ in what is predicted, from whose information and against what benchmark (our judgment; Chapter 16).11

4.8 A physical reading

Under conditions that Box 4.2 lists beside each, definition one matches the extra work that Maxwell’s demon, an imagined being that sorts molecules, can extract at one temperature by measuring and acting on the result, and definition two, for one demon holding every measurement, the work available relative to equilibrium, the state a system settles into when left alone, which plays the role of the price (our analogy). Section 1.4’s match of Kelly betting, staking all capital in proportion to one’s beliefs, with a Szilard engine, a one-molecule heat engine run by the demon, cannot tell the two apart, since the engine starts each cycle in equilibrium, like a bet at calibrated, margin-free odds (our analogy).

Physically, the cost of processing information lies in measuring and clearing memory (Section 1.4; Box 4.2). Clearing a bit equally likely to hold either value gives off at least kTln⁡2kT \ln 2 of heat, kTkT being the typical thermal energy at temperature TT, though Landauer’s reasoning “gives no guarantee that this minimum is in fact achievable”.

Section 1.4 found nothing on the betting side playing kTkT; in form only, risk tolerance, the reciprocal of risk aversion, does (our analogy): value is a scale times mutual information in Grossman and Stiglitz (Section 4.5) and, approximately, under log utility in a horse-race market, and at most that in physics (our derivation; Box 4.2). The match fails in at least three ways (our judgment). kTkT belongs to the surroundings at temperature TT, the same for every part and set from outside, while risk tolerance is a personal preference, under log utility one’s own wealth. Work is conserved and adds up; sums of money based on utility do not add across people. In physics the same kTkT also prices the cost of information, while the Grossman–Stiglitz cost is a sum set from outside, so the equality of Section 4.5 holds only in equilibrium under free entry, where anyone may pay to become informed, and away from equilibrium the informed can earn more.12

4.10 A trader’s checklist

None of these questions says what to buy or sell, and good answers do not make a trade safe (our judgment).

  1. Value or others’ orders? Does the edge come from the asset’s value or from others’ coming orders (Section 4.1)? Testable: in Dhawan and Putniņš’s 355 pumps on two exchanges in 2017 and 2018, prices returned to pre-pump levels (working-paper version); a move made only by others’ orders should also reverse (our inference), but thin trading and overreaction reverse moves too, so the test needs a benchmark (our judgment).
  2. Measured against what? Check: at the same prices, would you trade differently without the signal (Section 4.2)? A correct belief bet against the price on every outcome, at odds with no bookmaker’s cut, also earns on average the price’s own error, as does a bettor without the signal who knows what the price-setters have seen (our derivation; Table 4.1).
  3. Why does the other side trade? Check: name its reason other than information, such as insurance or liquidity (Section 4.4). If there is none and the no-trade theorem’s other premises hold, it declines.
  4. Who else can buy it? Check: who else can get the signal, and at what cost (Section 4.5)? In Grossman and Stiglitz’s example, when buyers and non-buyers of a signal of fixed precision do equally well, it is worth just its cost to a buyer, as a sure sum (our derivation).
  5. How much can you trade? Check: how far would your orders move the price (Section 4.6)? Where Kyle’s (1989) Theorem 10.1 applies, the same amount of information beyond the price is worth less to a trader who moves the price than to a price taker (our derivation; Box 4.1).14

4.11 Summary

  • Dhawan and Putniņš’s 355 pumps on two exchanges in 2017 and 2018 peaked in about eight minutes on average, then fell back to pre-pump levels (working-paper version); the coin’s name said nothing about its value, and early buyers knew later buyers’ orders (our judgment).
  • A signal’s worth at the same prices equals its amount of information only under the five premises of Section 4.2, and is at most that amount in a stock market whose portfolios invest all the wealth with no short positions (Box 4.1); a correct belief bet against the price on every outcome, at odds with no bookmaker’s cut, earns on average the information beyond what the price-setters have seen plus the price’s own error.
  • In Hirshleifer’s example, under the premises of Section 4.3, sure private news adds on average 0.6730 to expected utility, the entropy of the state, by holding to settlement and as much again by trading before revaluation (our calculation); with all the premises of Section 4.4 it adds nothing (our judgment).
  • Private information alone gives no reason to trade when the five premises of Section 4.4 hold, shared starting beliefs not among them; even without anyone trading for other reasons, traders who do not infer what others’ trades reveal still trade.
  • In Grossman and Stiglitz’s example, when the price passes on what the informed know, price takers find no equilibrium if information costs less than the bound of Section 4.5; where informed and uninformed traders coexist, the information the price does not pass on equals risk aversion times the cost, in nats, for a signal of fixed precision (our derivation).
  • Where Kyle’s (1989) Theorem 10.1 applies, the same amount of information beyond the price is worth less to a trader who moves the price than to a price taker (our derivation). Information has social value only through the real decisions it changes, and public information can lower welfare under the conditions of Section 4.7.
  • Physics has counterparts of both definitions under the conditions of Box 4.2, and risk tolerance plays the role of kTkT in form only (our analogy).

Box 4.2 Information value in thermodynamics

Information here is in nats, and kTkT times nats is an energy (Box 2.2). The correspondences are our analogy.16

Kelly betting and the Szilard engine. The match of Section 1.4 (Vinkler, Permuter and Merhav 2016) needs independent, identical cycles, a partition moved quasi-statically, no cost of erasure and a demon that knows how its measurement errs (Box 1.1).

Definition one. At one temperature, with feedback (Sagawa and Ueda 2008), Wext≤−ΔF+kTI,W_{\text{ext}} \le -\Delta F + kT \, I, where ΔF\Delta F is the change in equilibrium free energy from start to end and II the information obtained, the mutual information for a classical measurement. Equality holds if and only if the feedback is perfect and the final state is canonical, in equilibrium at that temperature; with no information the bound reduces to Wext≤−ΔFW_{\text{ext}} \le -\Delta F.

Definition two. Averaged over the outcomes of an ideal measurement MM of XX (Box 2.2), at most kT[I(X;M)+D(ρX∥ρeq)]kT [I(X; M) + D(\rho_X \| \rho^{\text{eq}})] can be extracted (Esposito and Van den Broeck 2011; Parrondo 2023), matching I(θ;Y∣ℱseen)+D(π∥q)I(\theta; Y \mid \mathcal{F}^{\text{seen}}) + D(\pi \| q) term by term (our derivation). This needs one temperature, equal equilibrium free energies at start and end, an end in equilibrium reached reversibly, control so rich that it may take infinitely many parameters, no cost of measuring or resetting, and one demon holding every measurement, like a hypothetical trader who pools every signal.

After the fact. For a process that is reversible for the demon’s belief pp and ends in equilibrium whether started from the truth rr or from pp, with equal equilibrium free energies at start and end, the realized value’s counterpart is the mismatch cost, the extra dissipation when rr differs from pp, exact when pp rules out no state (Kolchinsky and Wolpert 2017; Box 1.1).

Costs. Measuring can be reversible when the apparatus starts in a standard state, the irreversible step being its later restoration (Bennett 1982). In an isothermal feedback cycle, the total work done on the system in measuring, feedback and resetting the memory is never negative, which restores the second law of thermodynamics (Parrondo 2023; Box 2.2).

Scale times information. Value is at most kTIkT \, I in physics; I(u;s∣P)/aI(u; s \mid P)/a in Grossman and Stiglitz, with risk tolerance 1/a1/a (Box 4.1); and under log utility, where a gain of II nats in expected log wealth raises the certainty equivalent from wealth ww to weIw e^{I}, by about wIw I for small II, so that ww plays 1/a1/a (our derivation).
