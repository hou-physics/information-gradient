Chapter 15 The Sender’s Choices, Sharing and Disclosure

Ljungqvist and Qian (2016) study short sellers who take a short position in an overvalued company and then reveal their information to the market, after which prices fall on average (working-paper version; Section 1.6.3). The short sellers were individuals or small boutiques without the resources to correct a mispricing in the textbook way, by selling short until the price reached its true value (working-paper version). Their sample covers 17 of them, who targeted 113 US-listed companies between July 2006 and December 2011, and who argued their case in detailed reports posted publicly and for free, with many “smoking guns,” such as recorded phone conversations, video surveillance and photographs, and with prominent disclosure that they held positions that gain if the price falls (working-paper version). One day before a release, new short selling in the target briefly rose 66% above its baseline (p = 0.002), perhaps as the short sellers built or added to their positions, though the authors call the spike economically small: on average only 0.48% of outstanding shares were newly shorted that day (working-paper version). On the report day, trading by the longs, the company’s existing shareholders, was 415% above its baseline and stayed significantly elevated for the next month (working-paper version). The short-sale data count everyone’s new short sales in the stock and do not show the short sellers’ own positions (our judgment).1

The short sellers chose what to say, facts that others could often check; when to say it, after taking a position; and whom to reach (our judgment), apparently the current shareholders, the one group that short-sale constraints did not hold back (working-paper version).

The root model assumes non-strategic forwarding: people pass news on at a fixed rate, without choosing what to say, to whom or when for their own gain. Table 5.1 in Section 5.8 lists this default with the others, and this chapter drops it, asking how a sender chooses where, when and in what form news enters (Section 15.2), how people with information and a trading interest share, sell and disclose it (Section 15.3), and which of these branches prices can tell apart (Section 15.4).

15.1 Senders and their objectives

A sender is anyone who chooses to put news before others, and the choices fall into two kinds (our judgment). In the first, the sender stands outside the network, as a marketer or a firm’s managers do, and chooses whom to tell first, when, and in what form, after which the news spreads by the given rules and those who pass it on forward non-strategically. Such a choice is a design problem, one that the sender or the modeler solves without changing how the news spreads, though a release that depends on what the news says (Section 15.2.2) already relaxes non-strategic forwarding at the source (our judgment). In the second kind, the people who hold the information also have a trading interest and choose whether to speak, how precisely and to whom to sell, so the spread depends on the content and on their own positions (our judgment).

A sender’s objective function, what the sender tries to maximize, comes in two main kinds (our judgment). Under reach maximization, as in Kempe, Kleinberg and Tardos’s (2003) choice of seeds (Section 15.2.1), the sender wants as many people as possible to end up knowing or adopting. Under profit maximization the sender wants to gain from trading, as Hirshleifer’s (1971) informed person would have every incentive to publicize the information after making his speculative commitment (Section 3.4) and as the short sellers of the opening took their positions before publishing. Managers who care about the near-term stock price (Sections 15.2.2 and 15.2.3) have an objective close to profit, the price measuring it, though they need not trade (our judgment).

The two objectives have different shapes: before the sender has built the position, each further person who knows is one more rival trading the same way, so the profit objective need not rise with the number who know; afterward, spreading the news can speed the price toward the value, as the short sellers of Section 15.3.3 count on, and the objective can then rise with reach (our judgment). The order “position first, then spread” comes from this timing, and a reach objective such as Kempe, Kleinberg and Tardos’s has no term for it (our judgment).2

15.2 A sender’s design choices

In this section the sender stays outside the spread, choosing only who receives the news first, when and in what form, and in Section 15.2.2 also whether it is released; once it is out, the propagation model and the processing rules are as given (our judgment).

15.2.1 Seeds, audiences and the timing of injection

Kempe, Kleinberg and Tardos define the influence of a starting set of active people as the expected number active at the end of the process, and their influence maximization problem asks, for a number kk, for the set of kk such people, the seeds, with the largest influence, restating a question that Domingos and Richardson posed for viral marketing: which set to target to trigger a large cascade of further adoptions. Their greedy algorithm adds one seed at a time with the largest marginal gain, with a guarantee under the conditions of Sections 7.5 and 11.5.

They also let the sender choose the timing. In their non-progressive threshold model, in which people can switch from active to inactive and back, run for a fixed number of steps, the sender may make up to kk interventions, each targeting a particular person for activation at a particular step, with influence counted as the sum, over all people, of the number of steps each is active. The problem is equivalent to choosing seeds in a layered copy of the network, with one copy of each person for each step, so that where choosing seeds has approximation algorithms, these carry over to choosing people and steps together, and timing becomes a design variable while the objective is still reach (our judgment).3

Where a source sits matters differently under thresholds, in which a person acts once enough contacts have acted, and under averaging, in which people average their neighbors’ views (our judgment). Under thresholds of two or more contacts, seeds placed together among people who know one another can reach people whom as many scattered seeds cannot (our judgment; Section 11.5). Under averaging, Acemoglu, Ozdaglar and ParandehGheibi (2010) find that one network can lead to very different limits depending on where the forceful agents sit, people such as media outlets or political leaders who sway others far more than others sway them (Section 13.8). Minorities of committed individuals, people who do not change their minds, may mobilize large fractions of a population in work that Lucchini et al. (2022) cite (as reported by Lucchini et al.; Section 11.6), though this book gives no critical size for such a minority (Section 13.8).4

15.2.2 Release timing and content

A firm chooses when to announce. In DellaVigna and Pollet’s (2009) model, short-term managers with sufficiently negative news release it on high-distraction days because they prefer an attenuated immediate response, which the authors use to explain the lower earnings surprises and returns around Friday announcements (working-paper version; Section 10.2). The choice of day is a result of their model, and the Friday pattern is their explanation of a known finding (our judgment).

Release can also depend on what the news says. Hong, Lim and Stein (2000) read their results as follows: managers with good news probably have every incentive to push it out fast, while with bad news they are likely to be less forthcoming, so outside analysts matter more, and short-sale constraints may also be part of the explanation, though alone they would not seem to explain why the effect is stronger where analysts are fewer (working-paper version). The managers’ behavior is the authors’ interpretation, which they do not test directly (our judgment); Chapter 20 takes up whether bad news travels slowly.

In these readings, choosing the day and pushing good news while holding back bad share one motive, a near-term stock price, and the sender chooses the moment and the chance of injection, relaxing non-strategic forwarding at the source; holding bad news back can work only if investors cannot read the silence (our judgment; Section 15.3.6).5

15.2.3 Presentation and public actions

As a consequence of limited attention, disclosures that carry the same information can have different effects on investor perceptions, depending on how they are presented (Section 10.3). In Hirshleifer and Teoh’s (2003) model of pro forma earnings, figures disclosed outside the standard accounting rules (GAAP) that exclude certain costs, the firm states its adjustment and only attentive investors undo it (Sections 9.3 and 9.6).

Their Proposition 1 concerns when the firm adjusts. The model has a normal state, in which we read an adjustment as one that GAAP earnings do not call for (our judgment), and an exceptional state; the authors find the normal state the more interesting case. If some investors have limited attention when evaluating pro forma announcements, the probability of an adjusted disclosure rises in the normal state, and falls in the exceptional state, with three quantities: the managers’ preference for a higher current stock price, the weight of inattentive investors’ beliefs in the price, and the signal-to-noise ratio of properly adjusted earnings. With some investors inattentive, then, the more informative properly adjusted earnings are, the more likely the firm is to adjust in the normal state, which we read as a stronger incentive to manipulate (our judgment). Presentation works here only through limited attention, since attentive investors undo any stated adjustment (our judgment; Section 9.3).6

A firm can also choose public actions. Daniel, Hirshleifer and Subrahmanyam (1998) give the example of a rationally managed firm that may tend to buy back more of its stock when its managers believe the market undervalues it; the event then reflects the managers’ belief about the valuation error and predicts abnormal returns, returns beyond what is normal for the stock, positive after repurchases and negative after equity offerings. A selective event, in their definition, is one whose occurrence or size depends on the mispricing at the time, and in their Proposition 5, if investors are overconfident, selective events initiated when the stock is undervalued (overvalued) come on average with positive (negative) abnormal price changes on the announcement date and are followed on average by positive (negative) abnormal changes. A public action is then itself a message that the sender times by mispricing, so the drift after such events carries the sender’s choice; because the action is also a trade in the firm’s own shares, it sits at the edge between the two kinds of Section 15.1 (our judgment).7

15.3.1 Reciprocal sharing among competitors

Stein (2008) asks why competitors share ideas, which this book calls reciprocal sharing. In his model two players have customer bases that overlap in part, each with a monopoly over the rest of his customers and competing with the other for the overlap, whose size Stein uses as a proxy for the degree of competition, and a player who gains access to the other’s idea has a probability of coming up with a further idea that builds on and refines it (working-paper version).

Whether honest exchange can last depends on complementarity. In his Proposition 1, if a player’s probability of having an idea is fixed and does not depend on whether he has been given a truthful report of the other’s previous idea, truthful exchange can never be part of an equilibrium; a player can deviate by saying nothing or by passing along a bogus, informationally useless version of his idea (working-paper version). The key to incentive compatibility, Stein writes, is a strong complementarity: a player can only generate a useful new insight on a topic if he has access to his counterpart’s previous thoughts on it, and a player who deviates then always does best by not reporting anything (working-paper version), so with complementarity, strategic sharing in his model comes without strategic distortion (our judgment).

It is easier to continue a conversation than to open a new one with an equally well-developed idea, since continuing gives away one further signal and opening gives away all kk signals one holds (working-paper version). In his Proposition 3, for adjacent players in a linear network, with the continuing condition met so that conversations once started can be sustained, a player whose conversation ends with each side able to produce at most a threshold number of signals opens a new conversation with the next player; above the threshold, the spread along the network stops, the threshold being stated for value that decays geometrically from one signal to the next (working-paper version; Box 15.1).

This is a step rule, passing the idea on while it is still thin and stopping once it has grown rich, and so a counterexample to non-strategic forwarding, under which the chance of passing news on does not depend on its value (our judgment). The step does not need declining value: in a variant outside his model, with value in proportion to the number of signals, conversations can be sustained when the chance of building on an idea is at least the overlap, and opening is again bounded, at 9 signals with the competition and probability of his first example (our derivation; Box 15.1).

The rule ties distance to content. Stein defines cc as the number of conversations after the first and kk as the length of the first, which he reads as the quality of the idea that emerges from it. In his Proposition 4, when the continuing condition holds, the correlation between the two is negative, −0.402 in his second example (working-paper version), so, in his reading, the ideas reaching the most investors tend to have relatively little real information content (working-paper version; Section 2.6).8

15.3.2 Selling information

Information can also be sold. Admati and Pfleiderer (1990) name the externality in its valuation as an important characteristic of information as a commodity: if more agents observe a piece of information, its value is generally diminished, and, in general, as traders in the aggregate acquire more information, the price becomes more informative and the value of information to any one trader declines (Section 4.5).

In a direct sale, by their 1986 result, the seller may prefer to sell noisier versions of what he knows or to restrict the fraction of informed traders (as reported by Admati and Pfleiderer; Section 9.5). In an indirect sale, traders do not observe the seller’s information and pay for the response to it taken on their behalf, as investors in a fund do. When information is sold indirectly it is never desirable to add noise, and adjusting the fund’s unit price controls the use of the information more effectively than a direct seller can with added noise or a limit on buyers.

Which sale earns more depends on the externality. With a single price per share for the fund, indirect sale is more profitable when the externality is relatively intense, which in their model occurs when traders are relatively risk tolerant or there is little noise in supply, and direct sale when it is relatively mild; as their ratio mm of traders’ risk tolerance to the noise in supply, larger when the externality is more intense, grows without limit, the profits of a fund with a single price per share tend to twice those of the direct sale. Their Figure 3, with risk tolerance set to 1, puts the fund ahead only where both the supply noise and the noise in the seller’s own signal are relatively small.

The results rest on premises that the authors state. There are no incentive problems between seller and buyers, and buyers do not resell the information, which is reasonable if it is useful only for a short time; the single seller does not trade on his own account; and the fund trades anonymously, since if traders could costlessly observe its position, selling information this way would be impossible. With a fixed participation fee added to the price per share, a monopolist never sells directly, a result that, like the others, depends on traders being identical.

A fund sells the use of information and leaves its buyers’ information unchanged, so they have nothing to copy, and in their model controlling use earns more than controlling content when the fund sets one price per share, the externality is intense and the seller’s own signal is precise enough, and never less when the fund can add a fixed fee, both under all the premises above (our judgment). A seller who does not trade and sells to those with capital is one way information reaches capital (Chapter 19).9

15.3.3 Verifiable evidence after a position

The short-seller report of the opening is a third way. Ljungqvist and Qian write that revealing the information voluntarily, and thereby accelerating price discovery, reduces the risk of the strategy and sidesteps the short sellers’ limited resources, and that for this to work the information must be credible, or else the longs will ignore it (working-paper version). Faster convergence has value even to a short seller who would hold until the price reached the value, since the cost of borrowing shares accrues by the day (our judgment).

The authors measure a short seller’s track record as the rolling mean of the three-month cumulative abnormal returns of his earlier reports that a third party, such as the SEC, the Department of Justice or an exchange, independently confirmed. When a more credible short seller is the first to report on a target, its price falls by an average of 12.5% on the report day, net of market movements (p < 0.001), against −4.5% for less credible reports, which is not significantly different from zero (p = 0.15) (working-paper version). They write that credibility appears to be necessary but is probably not sufficient to ensure that the longs listen (working-paper version). Splitting first reports by what they say, report-day returns average −10.2% when a report reveals new information (p < 0.001), against an insignificant −1.7% when it merely reinterprets known data (p = 0.375) (working-paper version). Of the 285 reports that raise material concerns beyond a reinterpretation of known data, only 19% were later disproved by independent parties (working-paper version).

A sender with an open conflict of interest can gain belief through evidence that others can check, as in verifiable disclosure, or through a record that third parties confirmed, a reputation of the kind van Bommel’s leader needs (our judgment; Sections 15.3.4 and 15.3.6). Early sources for this order of position first, publicity after are Hirshleifer’s informed person, whose gain needs counterparties who do not infer the news from the trades (our judgment; Sections 3.4 and 15.1), and a footnote of Stein’s, in which a manager already holding a long position might wish to communicate this credibly to others, so that their buying pushes up the price of his position (working-paper version). Here the information sat with small short sellers and the capital able to push the price down sat with the shareholders, so publishing took the information to where the capital was (our judgment; Chapter 19).10

15.3.4 A coarsened rumor after a position

Van Bommel’s (2003) informed trader (Sections 1.6.7 and 9.6) takes a fourth way. His leader sees the asset’s value, uniform between −2 and 2, before the first auction and has a trading capacity very small relative to the noise demand, orders unrelated to information; rumormongers, he says, have in common a trading capacity too small to fully exploit their information. By assumption, she trades on her signal in the first auction and gives advice to her followers after each auction, and she reverses her position whenever her rumor leads the price to overshoot the true value.

With a rumor that says only buy or sell, an honest leader’s expected gross profit is 50% greater than a silent one’s; although his analysis focuses on informed traders of modest wealth, all informed traders of finite wealth can increase their profit with the rumor strategy, albeit by less than 50%, and a simulation shows that the requirements of a small leader and many auctions for the full 50% are easily met. In equilibrium she does not profit from a rumor when the value lies farther than 1 from zero, so the second trade, against the overshoot, happens only in some cases (our judgment), yet she must still send the rumor then, so that if spreading rumors carries even a small cost, this equilibrium of the one-shot game breaks down.

With unrestricted messages, informed rumormongers use messages of the form “stock goes up a lot or down a bit” and “stock goes down a lot or up a bit,” and vagueness is the key to their profits. Such a rumor is coarsened: it lumps different values together and drops detail, while what it says stays true (our judgment). For an honest leader who always sees the value, the best cut-off between a lot and a bit is 0.8165; the price that the bullish rumor leads to is then 2/3, and her expected profit is 1.544, 54.4% above spreading no rumors, a gain that we take to need, like the 50%, a small leader and many auctions, and that a small cost of spreading rumors may also undo, since a leader who sees a value beyond the cut-off gains nothing from her rumor (our judgment). By the revelation principle any set of messages can replace his: in the equilibrium he selects, any bullish rumor, such as “the stock is hot,” means that the stock can either go down a bit or up a lot. When the value lies within 0.8165 of zero, which happens with probability about 0.41 (our calculation; Box 15.2), the rumor is literally true and still pushes the price to the other side of zero, away from the value (our derivation). A follower who buys at the rumor price then loses on that trade, though her belief given the rumor is right on average; one who reads “the stock is hot” as a plain “up” bears the cost of a wrong reading (our judgment; Section 1.6.7).

Followers must believe her. In his Proposition 4, because she could cheat, telling followers the opposite of what she knows, followers will never believe any rumor in a one-shot game, and an opportunistic leader loses all rumor profits. In repeated play, keeping her from bluffing, spreading rumors when she has seen nothing, needs a high enough discount factor: with monthly rounds and a discount factor of 0.99, she must see the value with a probability of at least 8% a month, about once a year, to support the informative equilibrium. He writes that probably the most powerful deterrent is US securities law, under which it is illegal to give trading advice to other investors without disclosing one’s own interest.

Cheap talk, costless words that can be false (Section 12.6), gains credibility from the speaker’s record, as van Bommel’s leader’s does from her reputation (our judgment); Section 1.2.3 counted one such record, how often President Trump carried out his cheap tariff threats, and its price test could not tell whether prices discounted the threats by it.11

A short-seller report and a coarsened rumor are two ways for someone with much information and little capital to earn from it, with the costs and returns of Table 15.1; both take a position first, the short sellers by their own disclosure, with only indirect evidence in the short-sale data, and van Bommel’s leader by assumption (our judgment).

Some senders here distort on purpose for their own gain, and so drop unbiased transmission, the default that news passes on unchanged whatever its truth or novelty (our judgment): the direct seller who adds noise, van Bommel’s leader, who coarsens what she knows or, if she cheats, makes her story false on purpose, and, without complementarity, Stein’s player who could pass on a bogus idea. Van Bommel’s leader, who knows the value and, when it lies near zero, leads the price to its other side, comes close to the informed persuaders whom Acemoglu, Ozdaglar and ParandehGheibi leave out (our judgment; Section 9.5).12

15.3.5 Pumps and trades that draw others in

A pump, in which a group gathers people to buy a coin together at a set time, coordinates others openly: in the pumps that Dhawan and Putniņš (2023) study, the organizers openly declare which coins they will pump, and paid members receive pump information such as the coin’s name before the general broadcast, which lets them take positions ahead of the official signal (working-paper version; Sections 1.6.7 and 4.1). What they pay for is an earlier place in the order in which people learn; since none of the messages in the 70 pumps they sampled at random suggests that the coin is undervalued (working-paper version), a pump takes positions first and then openly coordinates a purchase that carries no information about value (our judgment). Its late buyers take the place of the exogenous counterparty of Chapter 4, trading for reasons other than information, though their buying can be foreseen by those who know the pump’s timing (our judgment; Section 13.5).

For the scale of capital that moving a price takes, Tsang and Yang (2026) study Polymarket’s market on Donald Trump in the 2024 US presidential election: in October, by their corrected measure, moving its forecast by five percentage points, from 0.50 to 0.55, cost about $9.1 million, while naive volume made the market look far deeper, and so harder to move, than it was. This book uses the figure, from one market in one month, only for its order of magnitude, about ten million dollars to move one large market by five points; the paper does not test whether anyone traded to sway what others believed, and neither does this book (our judgment). Chapter 18 takes up the measure and Chapter 17 the price impact of pumps.

A trader can also try to draw others in by trading alone. Avery and Zemsky (1998) add to their model of herding (Section 14.9) a manipulator who arrives at exactly the right moment to induce herding, has no more information than the market maker and tries to profit by deceiving the market maker and the other traders with a calculated action that does not reflect information; the market maker and the other traders are completely unaware of the possibility (working-paper version). Their Corollary 18 finds manipulation unprofitable for any trading rule in their first information structure, and unprofitable in their second under the conditions of their Proposition 13, which rest on what they call extreme assumptions (working-paper version); the corollary leaves other conditions open (our judgment).13

15.3.6 Verifiable disclosure and silence

Milgrom’s (1981) verifiable-disclosure game, in which the sender can prove what he says (Section 12.6), gives this book’s reading of silence: investors can read a firm’s silence at a date when it would normally report as bad news when its information can be verified, they know that it has the information, and disclosure costs little, three conditions that together suffice for the reading, though the book does not claim that each is necessary (our judgment).

Milgrom also limits what the buyer can take in. If the buyer can assimilate only kk observations, fewer than the salesman has, the modified game has a sequential equilibrium in which the salesman always reports the kk most favorable observations, a proposition that shows such an equilibrium exists without saying it is the only one (our judgment).

Silence thus means what the equilibrium makes it mean, which turns on whether the information can be verified, whether the receiver knows that the sender has it, what disclosure costs and how much the receiver can absorb (our judgment), so managers can keep bad news out of prices by holding it back (Section 15.2.2) only where at least one of the three conditions fails (our judgment). Hirshleifer’s discovery case, in which nature does not reveal even a favorable state, so that no news is bad news, is in Section 3.3.14

15.4 Branches sorted by price reversal

Whether the price move after a message reverses, moving back the way it came, is one way to tell the branches apart (our judgment). Bradley et al. (2024) test it on WallStreetBets, a forum where retail investors post trading ideas. They take single-firm due diligence reports, posts that the poster identifies, and the moderators verify, as containing some analysis and a clear buy or sell signal: 2,340 reports issued between 2018 and 2020, a cut-off that puts all their analysis before the frenzied returns of GameStop and other meme stocks (working-paper version). Buy recommendations, about 80% of the reports, earn two-day abnormal returns of roughly 1.12%, and sell recommendations earn no significant abnormal returns (working-paper version).

Their test rests on reversal: uninformed price pressure, buying or selling that pushes the price with no information behind it, would revert over longer holding periods as prices return to fundamentals, while informed trading would not (working-paper version). They fail to find any evidence of reversals, and returns continue to drift in the same direction over the next month (working-paper version). After 13 weeks the average buy report has earned an abnormal return of 6.17%, and the posters’ ability to forecast returns is not limited to piggybacking off other information events (working-paper version). Among reports released during trading hours, retail trading rises about 7% in the five half-hours after a report relative to the five before, and the authors find some modest evidence that retail net buying is greater for reports that later earn more, consistent with retail investors perhaps having some ability to discern the reports’ quality (working-paper version). In this sample, part of retail demand may be informed, at odds with treating retail traders as exogenous counterparties (our judgment; Chapter 4).

After pumps, prices return to their pre-pump levels (working-paper version; Section 4.1). After the short sellers’ reports, the targets’ prices kept falling over the next three and twelve months (working-paper version; Section 1.8), though the authors cannot say how the short sellers chose their targets. A coarsened rumor pushes the price to its rumor price, the average value given the rumor, so given the rumor the later change has mean zero: a single rumor overshoots or falls short, and an average over many rumors shows no reversal (our derivation; Box 15.2).

Reading reversal needs an average over many events, a horizon long enough for a move to reverse, other news at the same time removed, as Bradley et al. check, and prices that do not overreact, since under overreaction informative news can also partly reverse (our judgment; Section 13.4). With these premises, reversal on average separates messages that carry information about value, the first three rows of Table 15.2, from contentless coordination or uninformed demand, the last two, and it cannot tell verifiable disclosure from a coarsened rumor; telling those two apart needs the sender’s reversing trade at the rumor price, and so data on the sender’s positions (our judgment).

15.6 A trader’s checklist

None of these questions says what to buy or sell, and good answers do not make a trade safe (our judgment).

  1. Does the person telling me hold a position? Check: the short sellers in Ljungqvist and Qian’s sample disclosed their short positions prominently in their reports (working-paper version; the opening); in van Bommel’s model the rumormonger trades before she advises, by assumption, and US law, he notes, forbids trading advice that does not disclose one’s own interest (Section 15.3.4).
  2. Can the claim be checked? Check: in Ljungqvist and Qian’s sample, report-day returns of first reports that revealed new facts averaged −10.2%, against an insignificant −1.7% for reinterpretations of known data, and first reports by less credible short sellers moved prices insignificantly (working-paper version; Section 15.3.3).
  3. Is the claim vague in a convenient way? Check: in van Bommel’s model, with a small and honest informed trader who always sees the value, many auctions and a value uniform between −2 and 2, a bullish rumor meaning “up a lot or down a bit” earns her the most, is literally true, and pushes the price to the wrong side of zero (our derivation) with probability about 0.41 (our calculation; Section 15.3.4 and Box 15.2).
  4. Did the price give the move back? Check: averaged over many events, Dhawan and Putniņš’s pumped coins returned to pre-pump levels, while the prices after Bradley et al.’s buy reports of 2018–2020 drifted on (working-paper version); a move that does not reverse can come from verifiable disclosure or from a coarsened rumor, a single coarsened rumor can give back its whole move and more (our derivation), and under overreaction informative news can partly reverse too, so one reversal does not identify a pump (our judgment; Section 15.4 and Box 15.2).
  5. Why now, and in this form? Check: in DellaVigna and Pollet’s model, short-term managers with sufficiently bad news choose distracted days (working-paper version; Section 15.2.2); in Hirshleifer and Teoh’s model, with some investors inattentive, more informative properly adjusted earnings make an adjustment in the normal state more likely (Section 15.2.3); by Milgrom’s reasoning, silence at a date when the firm would normally report reads as bad news when the information is verifiable, investors know the firm has it and disclosure costs little (our judgment; Section 15.3.6).

15.7 Summary

  • A sender outside the network who picks seeds, timing or presentation, whatever the news says, solves a design problem under the given spreading rules, while one whose release depends on the content relaxes non-strategic forwarding at the source; for a holder of information with a trading interest, profit, unlike reach, need not rise with the number who know before the position is built (our judgment; Sections 15.1 and 15.2).
  • In Stein’s two-player model, honest exchange needs complementarity in producing ideas; in his linear network, with value decaying geometrically and the continuing condition met, a player opens a new conversation only while the signals he can produce do not exceed a threshold, and the number of later conversations is negatively correlated with the length of the first (working-paper version; Section 15.3.1).
  • In Admati and Pfleiderer’s model, with identical traders who cannot resell, no incentive problems, a seller who does not trade and a fund whose position others cannot see, a fund at one price per share earns more than a direct sale when the externality in the valuation of information is intense and the seller’s own signal is precise enough, and less when the externality is mild (Section 15.3.2).
  • In Ljungqvist and Qian’s sample of first reports, the report-day fall was significant for more credible short sellers and for reports revealing new facts, and insignificant for the others in each split; the authors judge that credibility appears necessary but probably not sufficient (working-paper version; Section 15.3.3).
  • In van Bommel’s model, with the value uniform, an honest informed trader of small capital who always sees the value, trades first and then spreads an optimally vague rumor earns 54.4% more than by silence; in our reading this needs many auctions as well and may fail if spreading rumors costs anything, and her honesty needs a reputation or the law, since in a one-shot game in which she could cheat, followers believe no rumor (our judgment; Section 15.3.4).
  • Averaged over many events, over a long enough horizon, with other news removed and with prices that do not overreact, price reversal separates messages that carry information about value from contentless coordination and uninformed demand, and does not separate verifiable disclosure from a coarsened rumor (our judgment; Section 15.4).
