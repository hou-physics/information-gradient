Chapter 9 Distortion and Selective Forwarding

Vosoughi, Roy and Aral (2018) followed the true and false news stories that six independent fact-checking organizations had verified as they spread on Twitter from 2006 to 2017. Falsehood spread significantly farther, faster, deeper and more broadly than the truth in every category of information, most of all in political news. In their model of the likelihood of retweeting, false stories were 70% more likely to be retweeted than true ones, after controlling for the original tweeter’s account age, activity, numbers of followers and followees, and whether the account was verified. That 70% is an odds ratio, the odds that a false story is retweeted divided by the odds for a true one, close to a ratio of probabilities only when retweets are rare (our judgment). Their sample holds only cascades, chains of retweets that start from one tweet, of stories the six organizations had checked and that drew English-language replies linking to the organizations’ websites, and the authors say that restricting the sample to stories the six organizations had checked may bring a selection bias. Their definition of size counts the users who retweeted and leaves out anyone who saw a story and did not pass it on (our judgment). Section 8.4 gives how far and how fast the cascades went.1

The authors trace the difference to what people choose to retweet (Section 9.4), which this book reads as selective forwarding, whether a story is passed on depending on whether it is true (our judgment).

The root model of Chapter 5 assumes unbiased transmission, and this chapter drops it (Section 9.1). The sources this book has read hold no market case of news distorted in retelling, and none tests whether rumors about firms show the difference above (Section 9.8), so the market side comes from models (our judgment). Besides these cascades, the evidence is the retweets behind one correlation in Section 9.4; the rest is theory, models, arguments and our own calculations (Section 9.7).

9.1 Unbiased transmission and distortion

Unbiased transmission, a default of the root model listed in Table 5.1 (Section 5.8), means that news passes from person to person unchanged, and that whether it is passed on does not depend on whether it is true or how new it is.

Distortion in transmission takes three forms, all at the step where one person passes news to the next (our judgment). Suppose the result of the drug trial of Section 2.3 passes from someone at the trial to a friend, and from the friend to a trader. The friend can add noise, random changes to the message, or coarsen it, merging different cases into one, as when she calls a modest success and a great one both “success” (Section 9.2); she can bias it in a fixed, one-to-one way that the trader does not know about, as a person who always says the opposite does (Section 9.3); and whether she passes it on at all can depend on whether it is true or how new it is, which this book calls selective forwarding (Section 9.4).

Three other kinds of error are not distortion in this sense (our judgment; Section 9.5). The trial’s own announcement can be wrong, and everyone who repeats it copies the error faithfully, a common source error. A stubborn agent, who never changes her view, such as a commentator sure that the drug will fail, can pass on her wrong view accurately, so the error lies in the view and in the weight others give it (Chapter 13). And the person at the trial can misread the result, observation noise of her own. Three further kinds belong to other chapters: distortion that a sender adds on purpose for her own gain (Chapter 15), a listener who combines what she hears wrongly, for example by counting one story twice (Chapter 13), and limited attention, news that people do not see or do not use (Chapter 10).

The kinds are kept apart because they leave different traces and call for different checks (our judgment). Information lost in retelling can only accumulate as a story passes through more hands, when each teller passes on only what she heard and on average over the retellings that could happen (Section 9.2), and going back to the source recovers it. A common source error does not shrink however often it is copied; a stubborn agent’s view does not move when new facts arrive; and the observation noise of different observers can cancel out as their number grows, provided their errors are independent. 9.2 Noise and coarsening in retelling

Take a fact θ\theta, here whether a drug succeeds, known to a first person, and let MkM_k be the message after the kk-th retelling, a retelling being one person passing on to the next what she heard. If each teller passes on only what she heard from the one before, the retellings form a Markov chain: given the last message, the next does not depend on anything earlier. Cover and Thomas’s data processing inequality states that in such a chain no processing of a message, deterministic or random, can increase the information it contains about what came before, information here meaning mutual information, the average fall in uncertainty about one quantity from knowing another (Section 2.3). Along the chain the information about the fact can therefore only fall or stay the same, on average over the retellings that could happen, I(θ;Mk+1)≤I(θ;Mk)I(\theta; M_{k+1}) \le I(\theta; M_k) (our derivation).

The inequality needs the chain. A teller with another source on the fact, a signal of her own, a call to the source or a look at the price, can pass on more information about the fact than she received; that is aggregation, the pooling of what several people know (Section 7.5 and Chapter 19).

The inequality covers deterministic processing too, including one that maps several messages to one. Suppose the trial can end in failure, with probability 0.70, a modest success, 0.20, or a great success, 0.10, and a teller who knows the result calls both kinds of success “success”. Everything she says is true, yet what her words tell about the result falls from about 1.16 bits to about 0.88 bits, a loss of about 0.28 bits, which is what she no longer says about which kind of success it was (our calculation). The inequality holds with equality only when the retelling keeps all that the previous message said about the fact (Box 9.1), as passing a message on word for word, like a retweet, does (our judgment).

The inequality holds on average over all the retellings that could happen. One particular retelling can by chance land nearer the truth than what the teller heard, just as specific new evidence can increase uncertainty while evidence decreases it on average (Section 2.4). Whether real retellings behave as such a chain can therefore be judged only from many retellings, and a single rumor can neither confirm it nor refute it (our judgment).2

Figure 9.1 puts numbers on noise in retelling, for the drug of Section 2.3 with a prior of 0.30 for success: the first person knows the outcome, and each retelling turns “success” into “failure”, or the reverse, with the same chance ε\varepsilon, independently of everything before, every teller passing on only what she heard. One retelling is then a binary symmetric channel, a link that flips each symbol with the same probability (Section 2.7). Cover and Thomas give the information that one such channel carries about its input, and a problem they set states that a chain of identical channels is equivalent to a single one; together these give the information left after kk retellings (our derivation; Box 9.1), which the figure plots (our calculation).

One retelling at ε=0.2\varepsilon = 0.2 leaves 0.236 bits, the same as the report of Section 2.3, which is right 80% of the time (our calculation). The formula is the same for both: noise that enters where an observer looks at the fact is observation noise, and noise that enters where one person passes the message to the next is distortion (our judgment; Section 9.5).

If one retelling in ten flips the message, ε=0.1\varepsilon = 0.1, one retelling keeps about 52% of the fact’s 0.881 bits, two keep about 31% and five about 7.5% (our calculation). This book chooses the rate for illustration, since it has no measured chain of retellings from which to estimate ε\varepsilon.

At ε=1/2\varepsilon = 1/2 nothing is left after one retelling: the message is unrelated to the fact, and all three lines fall to zero. Shannon made the same point with a channel whose noise is so great that the received symbols are entirely independent of the transmitted ones, so that about half of them are correct by chance alone, though no information is transmitted at all.

At ε=1\varepsilon = 1 every retelling says the opposite of what the teller heard, and no information is lost: after an odd number of retellings every message is false, after an even number every message is true (our derivation).

The lines fall as retellings are added, which is this section’s inequality in this example; for ε\varepsilon other than 0, 1/2 and 1 they fall strictly, and with ever more retellings the information tends to zero (our derivation; Box 9.1).3

9.3 Biases the receiver does not know

Shannon (1948) used the word distortion more narrowly than this book does. When a transmitted signal always produces the same received signal, he called the effect distortion, and when no two transmitted signals produce the same received one, the distortion may be corrected, at least in principle, by applying the inverse operation to what is received; a transformation that is one to one leaves the rate of transmission unchanged. This book calls such a map, applied by a teller without the listener’s knowledge, a one-to-one bias (our judgment). The person who always says the opposite is the plainest case: every one of her messages is false, yet a listener who knows her habit turns each one around and loses nothing (ε=1\varepsilon = 1 in Figure 9.1; our derivation).

A listener who does not know the map and takes her words at face value moves her belief the wrong way. The information is still in the messages, and the loss is in her reading, which Box 1.1 measures by relative entropy: reading one’s information as the wrong posterior costs the relative entropy of the right posterior from the one used (Section 1.4). Box 9.2 works out the cost for a listener who reads the person who always says the opposite as the report of Section 2.3.

How often a source is right and how much it tells about the fact are different quantities (our judgment): the teller who smooths away detail is always right and passes on less information, and the one who always says the opposite is always wrong and passes on all of it (Section 9.2). Shannon’s milder example, read as a teller who errs once in a hundred, points the same way (our judgment). With 1000 symbols a second, 0 and 1 equally likely, and 1 in 100 received wrong, one might put the rate at 990 bits per second, the symbols that arrive right, but that leaves out the recipient’s lack of knowledge of where the errors occur; Shannon counts that missing knowledge as 81 bits per second, so the rate is 1000 − 81 = 919 bits per second. He set the meaning of messages aside as irrelevant to the engineering problem (Section 2.5), and in this book the truth enters mutual information only as the value of θ\theta that the receiver wants to recover (our judgment).

Hirshleifer and Teoh (2003) give a case from the market, in a model. Pro forma earnings, also called street earnings, are measures of earnings that a firm discloses outside generally accepted accounting principles (GAAP), the standard rules of financial reporting, and that exclude certain costs; the practice grew sharply in the years before they wrote. The purported reason, in their word, is to reflect special circumstances unrelated to the firm’s long-term prospects, such as one-time charges. Pro forma and GAAP earnings often differ substantially, they write, and the size of such a gap does not say whether it can be undone, which depends on whether the firm states what it excluded (our judgment).

In their model pro forma earnings are disclosed either as GAAP earnings or with an adjustment, and the effect of excluding the item is public information, so attentive investors can invert the pro forma figure and infer GAAP earnings. Inattentive investors treat pro forma earnings as if they had been adjusted to be as informative as possible, failing to discount for the firm’s incentive to manipulate the disclosure, a form of limited attention (Chapter 10). Since GAAP earnings can be computed from the disclosure, the data processing inequality gives the disclosure at least as much information about the firm’s future cash flow as GAAP earnings, on average over the disclosures a firm could make (our derivation; Section 9.2). In their model, then, the adjustment is a one-to-one bias: the information is in the disclosure, and the inattentive investor misreads it (our judgment).

A number can be biased and informative at once. In their Proposition 2, when some investors have limited attention, pro forma earnings are on average higher than GAAP earnings and are upward biased predictors of the firm’s final cash flow; in their Proposition 6, pro forma earnings from a manager who strategically exploits limited attention can be more accurate than GAAP earnings as indicators of firm value. With accuracy in their sense of less noise in the signal about firm value, we read this as saying that in their model a biased number can carry more information than an unbiased one, and that only a receiver who knows the map removes the bias (our judgment).

These are results of a model, which Hirshleifer and Teoh set against no data of their own (our judgment). A frequent criticism of pro forma disclosures, they note, is that many companies fail to state clearly which items are excluded; part of the information is then missing from the public record, the attentive investor cannot invert the figure, and the bias is no longer one to one (our judgment). When and why a firm adjusts is the sender’s choice (Chapter 15), and Section 9.6 gives the effect on prices.4

9.4 Selective forwarding

Vosoughi, Roy and Aral looked first at the users and the network. Users who spread false news had significantly fewer followers, followed fewer people, were less active, were verified less often and had been on Twitter for less time, and falsehood still spread farther and faster than the truth. Since the characteristics of users and the structure of the network could not explain the difference, the authors conclude that people’s greater readiness to retweet falsehood than truth drives the spread of false news, although network and individual factors favor the truth. Robots, automated accounts found by two detection algorithms, sped up true and false news roughly equally, which the authors take to suggest that false news spreads farther because humans are more likely to spread it; a speedup equal for both leaves the share of false news unchanged and is not selective forwarding (our judgment).

Others propagate a rumor by retweeting it, and the authors make no claims about the intent of those who spread it; a story made false on purpose would belong to Chapter 15. A retweet passes the first tweet on as it stands (our judgment; Section 8.4), so along one chain the information about the fact is the same at every step, the case of equality (our derivation; Section 9.2). The falsity of a false story’s first tweet is an error at the source that every retweet copies faithfully, a common source error (our judgment; Section 9.5). The bias lies in which messages are passed on: in their sample false stories were more likely to be retweeted than true ones, with the controls given at the start of the chapter. This book calls that selective forwarding; the forwarders need not know which stories are true, and a difference in the chance of a retweet is enough, whatever people respond to (our judgment).

Selective forwarding changes what people see while every copy stays the same. Suppose that false stories make up some share of what is posted, that their cascades are on average larger than those of true stories, and that the number of times people see a story is proportional to the number of users who retweet it; then false stories make up a larger share of what people see than of what is posted (our derivation; Box 9.2). The last two premises are this book’s: the main text of Vosoughi, Roy and Aral prints no ratio of mean cascade sizes, their 70%, an odds ratio from a model defined in supplementary materials this book has not read, cannot stand in for one, and their data do not count how many people saw a story (our judgment).

The authors offer an explanation from information theory and Bayesian decision theory: novelty attracts attention, contributes to productive decision-making and encourages the sharing of information, and novel information is surprising and also more valuable, for decisions and for the social status of being in the know. They randomly selected about 5000 users who had spread true and false rumors and took a random sample of about 25,000 tweets these users were exposed to in the 60 days before they retweeted a rumor. Comparing the topics of each rumor tweet with those of the earlier tweets, they found false rumors significantly more novel than true ones on all three of their measures. Users may not have perceived false rumors as novel, and the authors cannot claim that novelty causes retweets or that it is the only reason false news is retweeted more often. They also report that novel information is more likely to be retweeted, with the estimate in supplementary materials this book has not read. If whether a story is passed on depends on how new it is, that too is selective forwarding (our judgment).

Their novelty measures how far the topics of a tweet lie from what the user saw lately, and the fact does not enter it (our judgment). A message can be new and carry a large surprise, in the sense of Section 2.2, and still say nothing about the fact: at ε=1/2\varepsilon = 1/2 in Figure 9.1 the message has an entropy of 1 bit, more than the fact’s own 0.881 bits, and carries no information about the fact (our calculation). Box 9.2 puts novelty and surprise in symbols.

Weng et al. (2012) and Shiller (2017) describe selective forwarding of other kinds (our judgment). On Twitter people forward selected posts from their screen to their followers, and a retweet carries a meme, a piece of content such as a hashtag, from user to user. Users are more likely to retweet memes they posted about in the past, with a Pearson correlation coefficient of 0.98, computed over the groups of their Figure 3; a correlation across groups can be far higher than one across single users, so it says little about any one user (our judgment). Their methods section gives retweets from October 2010 to January 2011, though some of their figures run longer (our judgment; Section 6.7). Shiller writes that retellings of a narrative, a popular story that people retell, which lose its essential interest through errors of transmission do not survive, and his rate of contagion counts only the successful spread of that core interest (working-paper version), a reading he does not test on data (our judgment). The three describe different things and do not conflict; in each, whether a message is passed on depends on its content (our judgment). Chapter 10 takes up how novelty draws attention and how long a meme stays on a user’s screen.5

9.5 Errors that are not distortion

A common source error is made at the source and copied faithfully afterward, with the chance that a copy is passed on the same whether the message is true or false. Once one copy of a message is known, a second adds no information (Section 2.3), so a story heard from ten accounts that all copied one leak tells no more about the fact than the leak did and carries the same error (our judgment). The false first tweet of a false story in Vosoughi, Roy and Aral’s data is of this kind; the greater readiness to retweet it is selective forwarding (our judgment; Section 9.4). The number of cascades that make up a rumor is the number of times users tweeted the story independently; cascades whose first tweets rest on one source share its error and do not confirm one another, while those resting on separate looks at the fact could, and their main text does not say which are which (our judgment). Counting cascades that share a source as separate pieces of evidence is what DeMarzo, Vayanos and Zwiebel call persuasion bias, failing to adjust for possible repetitions of information and treating all information as new (working-paper version; Section 7.5), a fault in how a listener processes what she hears, with each message passed on intact (our judgment; Chapter 13).

In this book’s reading, Andrei and Cujean (2017) put a common source error into a model of a market (our judgment). Each agent starts with a signal that adds a common noise and a noise of her own to the asset’s fundamental value, and the common noise has two properties of a rumor: it circulates from person to person, and it is unverifiable. On average, private signals reveal only the fundamental value plus the rumor, and the signals agents exchange when they meet all contain it. Pooling more of these signals brings an agent closer to the fundamental value plus the rumor, so the rumor’s part of her error does not shrink as she hears from more people (our derivation). Chapter 19 takes up the model’s prices.

In Bala and Goyal’s (1998) model people learn from the experience of their neighbors, and the royal family is a small set of agents observed by everyone, a structure that fits people who have local information and also share a common public source (working-paper version). In their example the royal family’s bad results can overwhelm any positive information gathered locally and lead everyone to switch to the action with known payoffs; no further information is then generated, and society is locked into an inferior choice, while without the royal family society chooses the optimal action in the long run (working-paper version). The royal family update their beliefs as others do, and nobody alters what others see of their results; everyone sees the same few noisy results, so bad luck in them becomes a common shock, an error shared by all with the structure of a common source error, and nothing is distorted as it passes on (our judgment; Chapter 13).

A stubborn or forceful agent passes on her own view accurately, as the commentator of Section 9.1 does, and the error lies in the view and in the weight others give it (our judgment). In Acemoglu, Ozdaglar and ParandehGheibi’s (2010) model, two regular agents who meet both update their beliefs to the average of their pre-meeting beliefs, while forceful agents influence the beliefs of some of the others they meet without changing their own opinion, which is where the authors see the possibility of misinformation. Pedersen’s (2022) “fanatic” agents, stubborn about their own view however irrational, can also have a large influence over time if others are willing to listen. Acemoglu, Ozdaglar and ParandehGheibi leave out individuals who know the underlying state and try to convince others of some systematic bias, who would belong to Chapter 15 (our judgment); Chapter 13 compares the forceful agents and fanatics.

Observation noise, the error an observer makes when she looks at the fact herself, as in the drug report of Section 2.3, enters before anyone retells, so it is not distortion, though a retelling with the same chance of error has the same formula (our judgment; Section 9.2).

Deliberate distortion and limited attention belong to later chapters (our judgment). A sender with a stake in what others believe can distort news on purpose: Admati and Pfleiderer (1990) report their earlier result that in a direct sale of information the seller may prefer to sell noisier versions of the information he has, and van Bommel’s informed investor spreads imprecise rumors (Section 9.6); Chapter 15 takes up both. News that a receiver does not see, or sees and does not use, is limited attention (Chapter 10), as with the inattentive investors of Section 9.3.6

9.6 Distortion and prices

A price passes news on to those who watch it, and under two conditions it is one more step in a chain of retellings. Suppose that those whose trades set the price trade on a rumor about the fact and know nothing else about it, and that all other orders, such as those of noise traders, people who trade for reasons unrelated to information, are independent of the fact, the rumor and the price-setters’ orders taken together. The fact, the rumor, the orders and the price then form a Markov chain, and the price holds no more information about the fact than the rumor does, on average over the prices that could arise (our derivation; Box 9.2). A single price-setter with information of her own breaks the chain, and the price can then hold more, as in learning from prices (Chapter 16). In a widely traded stock many traders have news of their own, so the bound does not apply; it fits a price moved by one rumor and little else, as in the model below (our judgment).

In van Bommel’s (2003) model (Section 1.6.7), an informed investor with limited investment capacity spreads imprecise rumors to an audience of followers, who trade on the advice and move the price; due to the imprecision of the rumor the price overshoots with positive probability, so that she can trade a second time when she knows the price to be overshooting. Until that second trade the informed orders depend on the asset’s value only through the rumor, so the price can hold no more information about the value than the rumor does, on average over the prices that could arise (our derivation). Chapter 15 takes the model up.

The bound concerns how much the price knows about the fact and says nothing about how far the price moves. Stein’s bound on the price impact of far-traveling ideas relies on investors being rational Bayesians who do not overreact to news from their conversations; if news heard face to face seems particularly salient and compelling, even low-quality rumors might have significant consequences for prices (working-paper version; Section 2.10). How far the price strays from the fact depends on how the price-setters process what they hear (our judgment; Chapter 13).

Hirshleifer and Teoh’s model gives pro forma earnings a price side. In their Proposition 2, if some investors have limited attention, stock prices are on average higher than they would be if adjusted pro forma disclosure were prohibited. In their Proposition 4, the larger the excess pro forma earnings, pro forma earnings minus GAAP earnings, the greater on average the overvaluation and the more negative the average abnormal return that follows, an abnormal return being the part of a stock’s return beyond what is normal for it. The adjustment is public in the model (Section 9.3), so the price-setters have seen the information and the price misreads it, which in Chapter 1’s terms is undigested information, seen and not absorbed correctly (our judgment; Section 1.3).7

9.7 Evidence cited in this chapter

Two sets of data stand behind this chapter.

  • Fact-checked cascades. Vosoughi, Roy and Aral’s true and false cascades on Twitter from 2006 to 2017, with the sample limits, including a possible selection bias, given at the start of the chapter. Their check on rumors that their own annotators agreed on found results nearly identical to those of the main data, and their main text does not say which way the selection would push the 70% (our judgment). The data set is available only with an agreement to use it for validating the results of their study, and this book read the journal version and not their supplementary materials, so this chapter uses only the numbers printed in their main text (our judgment).
  • Retweets behind Weng et al.’s correlation. Weng et al.’s correlation rests on the retweets whose period Section 9.4 gives.
  • Model results and arguments with no data behind them. Everything else rests on models, arguments and calculations: Hirshleifer and Teoh’s model of pro forma earnings, which they set against no data of their own (our judgment); Andrei and Cujean’s rumor; Acemoglu, Ozdaglar and ParandehGheibi’s forceful agents; Pedersen’s fanatics; van Bommel’s rumors; Admati and Pfleiderer’s sale of information; Bala and Goyal’s royal family, Stein’s conversations, DeMarzo, Vayanos and Zwiebel’s persuasion bias and Shiller’s arguments about narratives (working-paper version); the theory of Cover and Thomas and of Shannon; and Figure 9.1, the trial with three outcomes and the numbers of Box 9.2 (our calculation).8

9.8 A trader’s checklist

None of these questions says what to buy or sell, and good answers do not make a trade safe (our judgment).

  1. How many hands has the story passed through? Check: when each teller passes on only what she heard, each retelling can only lose information about the fact or keep it, on average over the retellings that could happen (our derivation; Section 9.2); one retelling can still land nearer the truth by chance. In the model of Figure 9.1, with a prior of 0.30 and one retelling in ten flipping the message, a rate chosen for illustration, five retellings keep about 7.5% of the fact’s 0.881 bits (our calculation). A teller who went back to the source, or has news of her own, breaks the chain.
  2. Does the source reverse the news or round it off? Check: if a source’s words map one to one onto the fact, as with a person who always says the opposite, a listener who knows the map loses nothing and one who does not loses in her reading (our derivation; Section 9.3). A source that merges different outcomes into one passes on less information even when every word is true, about 0.28 bits less for the teller who calls both kinds of success “success” (our calculation; Section 9.2).
  3. Can the adjusted number be undone? Check: in Hirshleifer and Teoh’s model the firm states its pro forma adjustment, so attentive investors can invert pro forma earnings and infer GAAP earnings; when a firm fails to state clearly which items it excludes, a frequent criticism they note, the number cannot be undone (our judgment; Section 9.3). In the same model, when some investors have limited attention, larger excess pro forma earnings go with greater overvaluation on average and a more negative average abnormal return afterward; these are results of a model (Section 9.6).
  4. Is it spreading because it is true? Testable: in Vosoughi, Roy and Aral’s fact-checked Twitter sample from 2006 to 2017, which the authors say may carry a selection bias, false stories were 70% more likely to be retweeted than true ones, an odds ratio from their model of retweeting with controls for the original tweeter’s account (Section 9.4). Whether rumors about firms and markets show the same difference can be tested by comparing how far rumors later confirmed and rumors later denied spread, and none of this book’s sources has done so (our judgment).
  5. Do the copies share one source? Check: trace the copies back and count their separate sources. Cascades or reports that start from one source share its error and do not confirm one another (our judgment; Section 9.5), and counting them as independent evidence is what DeMarzo, Vayanos and Zwiebel call persuasion bias (working-paper version; Chapter 13).
  6. Can the price know more than the rumor? Check: if those who set the price trade only on a rumor, and all other orders are independent of the fact, the rumor and the price-setters’ orders taken together, the price holds no more information about the fact than the rumor does, on average over the prices that could arise (our derivation; Section 9.6). A price-setter with news of her own about the fact breaks this condition, and the price can then hold more (Chapter 16). The bound says nothing about how far the price moves.

9.9 Summary

  • Dropping unbiased transmission, the default that news passes on unchanged whatever its truth or novelty, lets in three forms of distortion: noise and coarsening in retelling, one-to-one biases the receiver does not know about, and selective forwarding (our judgment; Section 9.1).
  • When each teller passes on only what she heard, the retellings form a Markov chain, and by the data processing inequality each retelling can only lose information about the fact or keep it, on average over the retellings that could happen (our derivation; Section 9.2).
  • A one-to-one bias leaves the information in a message unchanged, and a listener who does not know the map loses only in her reading, by the relative entropy of Box 1.1 (our derivation; Section 9.3).
  • In Vosoughi, Roy and Aral’s fact-checked Twitter cascades from 2006 to 2017, a sample the authors say may carry a selection bias, false stories were 70% more likely to be retweeted than true ones, an odds ratio with controls for the original tweeter’s account; this book reads that as selective forwarding (our judgment; Section 9.4).
  • Vosoughi, Roy and Aral’s novelty measures how far a tweet’s topics lie from what a user saw lately, and the fact does not enter it; novelty and surprise differ from the information a message carries about the fact (our judgment; Section 9.4).
  • A common source error, a stubborn or forceful source passing on its own view accurately, and an observer’s own observation noise are not distortion, and each leaves different traces (our judgment; Sections 9.1 and 9.5).
  • If those who set a price trade only on a rumor, and all other orders are independent of the fact, the rumor and the price-setters’ orders taken together, the price holds no more information about the fact than the rumor does, on average over the prices that could arise, a bound that says nothing about how far the price moves (our derivation; Section 9.6).

9.10 Boxes for quantitative readers

Box 9.1 The data processing inequality in symbols

Notation. θ∈{0,1}\theta \in \{0, 1\} is the fact, with θ=1\theta = 1 for success, and π=P(θ=1)\pi = P(\theta = 1) its prior, as in Box 2.1. MkM_k is the message after the kk-th retelling, with M0=θM_0 = \theta. ε\varepsilon is the chance that one retelling flips the message, which Cover and Thomas write pp, a letter this book keeps for beliefs; their H(p)H(p) is the h(ε)h(\varepsilon) below. h(x)=−xlog⁡x−(1−x)log⁡(1−x)h(x) = -x \log x - (1 - x) \log (1 - x) is the entropy of two outcomes, Shannon’s H=−(plog⁡p+qlog⁡q)H = -(p \log p + q \log q) with his q=1−pq = 1 - p (Box 2.1), and logarithms are to base 2.9

Markov chains. X→Y→ZX \to Y \to Z is a Markov chain when the conditional distribution of ZZ depends only on YY, so that p(x,y,z)=p(x)p(y∣x)p(z∣y).p(x, y, z) = p(x)\, p(y \mid x)\, p(z \mid y). Then I(X;Y)≥I(X;Z)I(X; Y) \ge I(X; Z), whatever the processing of YY, deterministic or random, and in particular I(X;Y)≥I(X;g(Y))I(X; Y) \ge I(X; g(Y)) for any function gg. Equality holds if and only if X→Z→YX \to Z \to Y is also a Markov chain, the same condition that makes ZZ a sufficient statistic for XX, one that keeps all the information in YY about XX. Like the fall in uncertainty from conditioning, the inequality holds only on average over messages (Section 9.2).

Retelling. When each teller passes on only what she heard, θ→M1→⋯→Mk\theta \to M_1 \to \cdots \to M_k is a Markov chain. Each teller may process what she heard in any way, deterministic or random, so I(θ;Mk+1)≤I(θ;Mk)I(\theta; M_{k+1}) \le I(\theta; M_k) (our derivation). Cover and Thomas’s cascade, with no encoding or decoding at the intermediate terminals, is the special case in which each teller repeats what reached her. A teller with a source of her own breaks the chain.

One retelling. A binary symmetric channel flips its input with probability ε\varepsilon, and for any distribution of the input I(X;Y)=H(Y)−h(ε).I(X; Y) = H(Y) - h(\varepsilon). One retelling therefore carries I(θ;M1)=h(π(1−ε)+(1−π)ε)−h(ε)I(\theta; M_1) = h(\pi(1 - \varepsilon) + (1 - \pi)\varepsilon) - h(\varepsilon) (our derivation).

A chain of retellings. A problem in Cover and Thomas states that a cascade of kk identical binary symmetric channels is equivalent to one with flip probability εk=12[1−(1−2ε)k]\varepsilon_k = \tfrac{1}{2}\left[1 - (1 - 2\varepsilon)^k\right] so that I(θ;Mk)=h(π(1−εk)+(1−π)εk)−h(εk)I(\theta; M_k) = h\big(\pi(1 - \varepsilon_k) + (1 - \pi)\varepsilon_k\big) - h(\varepsilon_k) (our derivation). It is the same at ε\varepsilon and 1−ε1 - \varepsilon, zero at ε=1/2\varepsilon = 1/2 and equal to h(π)h(\pi) at ε=0\varepsilon = 0 and ε=1\varepsilon = 1. For 0<π<10 < \pi < 1 and ε\varepsilon other than 0, 1/2 and 1 it falls strictly as kk grows and tends to zero (our derivation). With π=0.30\pi = 0.30, h(π)=0.881h(\pi) = 0.881 bits, and Figure 9.1 plots the formula for k=1k = 1, 2 and 5 (our calculation).

The rigorous versions of this box’s own derivations are to be given in the appendix.

Box 9.2 One-to-one biases and selective forwarding in symbols

Notation. As in Box 9.1, θ\theta is the fact, π\pi its prior, MM or MkM_k a message about it and ε\varepsilon the chance that one retelling flips the message. gg is a map from messages to messages, and p̃(θ∣m)\tilde{p}(\theta \mid m) is the posterior that a listener holds after the message mm.

One-to-one maps. If a teller passes on g(M)g(M) in place of MM and gg is one to one, the corollary I(X;Y)≥I(X;g(Y))I(X; Y) \ge I(X; g(Y)) of Box 9.1, applied once to gg and once to its inverse, gives I(θ;g(M))=I(θ;M)I(\theta; g(M)) = I(\theta; M) (our derivation). The person who always says the opposite applies g(m)=1−mg(m) = 1 - m.

The cost of misreading. A listener who does not know gg reads each message with a posterior p̃(θ∣m)\tilde{p}(\theta \mid m) that differs from the right one, P(θ∣m)P(\theta \mid m). On average she pays the further cost of Box 1.1, 𝔼M[D(P(θ∣M)∥p̃(θ∣M))],\mathbb{E}_M\big[D\big(P(\theta \mid M) \,\|\, \tilde{p}(\theta \mid M)\big)\big], which is zero for a listener who knows gg (our derivation). Take the person who always says the opposite, after one retelling (ε=1\varepsilon = 1 in Figure 9.1), and a listener who reads her as the drug report of Section 2.3, right 80% of the time. After “success”, sent with probability 0.7, the listener believes 0.632 (Table 2.1), though the drug has failed; after “failure”, sent with probability 0.3, she believes 0.097, though it has succeeded. Her cost is log⁡2(19/7)=1.44\log_2 (19/7) = 1.44 bits after “success” and log⁡2(31/3)=3.37\log_2 (31/3) = 3.37 bits after “failure”, about 2.02 bits per message on average, while the messages carry 0.881 bits about the fact, all of its entropy (our calculation). A listener who ignores her and keeps the prior of 0.30 pays, by the same measure, the 0.881 bits that the messages carry, so misreading her costs more than ignoring her (our derivation; our calculation).

Selective forwarding. Let ff be the share of false stories among those posted and sFs_F and sTs_T the mean sizes of false and true cascades, and suppose, as this book does, that the number of times people see a cascade is the same multiple of its size for false and true stories. The share of false stories among what people see is then fsFfsF+(1−f)sT,\frac{f s_F}{f s_F + (1 - f) s_T}, which exceeds ff when 0<f<10 < f < 1 and sF>sTs_F > s_T (our derivation). With f=1/2f = 1/2 and sF=2sTs_F = 2 s_T it is 2/3, numbers chosen for illustration (our calculation). The share seen rises with ff, so a reader who knows sF/sTs_F / s_T can recover ff from it (our derivation).

Novelty and surprise. Two of Vosoughi, Roy and Aral’s measures of novelty, the Kullback–Leibler divergence and the Bhattacharyya distance, measure differences between probability distributions of topics; the first is a relative entropy, and θ\theta enters none of their measures (our judgment; Section 9.4). The surprise of a message mm is log⁡2(1/P(m))\log_2 (1 / P(m)) (Section 2.2), and its average over messages is the entropy H(M)H(M), while the information about the fact is I(θ;M)=H(M)−H(M∣θ)I(\theta; M) = H(M) - H(M \mid \theta) (Box 2.1).

Prices. Let RR be a rumor, XX the orders that set the price and qq the price. If the price-setters’ orders depend on θ\theta only through RR, the demand of noise traders is independent of θ\theta, RR and the price-setters’ orders taken together, and the price is set from the orders, then θ→R→X→q\theta \to R \to X \to q is a Markov chain and I(θ;q)≤I(θ;R)I(\theta; q) \le I(\theta; R) by the data processing inequality (our derivation). A price-setter with a signal of her own about θ\theta breaks the chain.10
