Chapter 19 Word of Mouth and Rational Expectations

GameStop’s stock started 2021 at $19 per share and hit an intraday high of $483 on January 28, which Pedersen (2022) calls a 25-fold increase with little news; the price dropped to $40 in February, rose sharply again in late February and stayed above $100 for the rest of 2021. Through the end of his sample the price stayed above its 2020 level while volume and volatility came down to levels similar to those of 2020, which Pedersen calls consistent with his model. This is one stock over one year, which his paper describes without a statistical test (our judgment).

Pedersen adds his own caution. The real world, he writes, is almost always more complex than any stylized model; the trading frenzy almost surely started in social media, as in his model, but several effects played a role in the rise. A short squeeze, short sellers buying back shares as the price rose, likely played a role in the January spike but was not a major driver of the rise that started in late February, which he reads as suggesting the importance of social network effects. Section 11.6 described the forum posts behind the same episode.

Pedersen explains the shape of this path by views spreading through a social network (our judgment). This chapter, the first of the bridges of Section 1.7, asks how word of mouth, news passed from those who know to those who do not, joins prices: through information percolation (Section 19.1), two ready-made models (Section 19.2), and the routes by which information reaches the capital that sets a price (Section 19.3).1

19.1 Information percolation

19.1.1 The full state behind the root model

Think of traders who each hold a scrap of evidence and compare notes whenever two of them meet, as in Duffie and Manso’s (2007) model of information percolation (Box 1.2). Each agent’s type, the sum of the log-likelihood ratios of her signals, each ratio measuring how strongly a signal favors one outcome, is higher the higher her posterior probability that a variable of common concern is high. Agents are matched at random times, at a rate λ\lambda common to all, tell each other their types and both end with the sum. The variable has two outcomes, each agent’s signals are independent given the outcome, and different agents’ signals are independent; for almost every pair of agents, the matching procedure is independent. With a continuum of agents, assuming the density of types differentiable in time and relying formally on the law of large numbers, they derive the equation of Box 1.2. They solve it through a transform and write that technical conditions on the initial type distribution might be needed to justify the calculation, which they have confirmed in special cases by explicit calculation and Monte Carlo simulation.

In most of the book’s bridge models the spread of information is an input fixed in advance (Section 1.7); here random matching inside the model produces it, and most models of this family have no price (our judgment). Pedersen’s model, in Section 19.2.2, is the other exception.

Duffie, Giroux and Manso (2010) write the same evolution for the cross-sectional distribution of types when two agents meet, in a form that allows a positive share of agents to hold the same type (our judgment). They assume a positive mass of agents with at least one informative signal and an initial distribution whose moment generating function is finite near zero, which rules out heavy tails, and give an explicit solution in the form of a Wild summation. In this book’s reading of the solution, an agent at time tt has pooled the starting signals of nn agents, her own among them, with a probability that falls geometrically in nn (our derivation; Box 19.1).

The root model is a projection of this state. Under the conditions of Box 1.2, a share 1−I(0)1 - I(0) starting with no signal and no single type held by a positive share of the informed, the share whose type is not zero follows the root model with no external source and λ\lambda as the rate of word of mouth (our derivation); the root model keeps who has heard something and drops how much each has pooled (Section 5.2). This book derives and checks both steps of Box 1.2, this projection and the growth of the average type, in the appendix, with the points its note left open: Duffie and Manso’s equation is written for a density while agents with no signal sit at type zero, and with discrete signals two types can cancel (our derivation).

Wild (1951) treated the Maxwellian model of a gas, in which molecules are point particles interacting through a central force proportional to the inverse fifth power of their distance, and for which a spatially uniform gas obeys an integral equation. Duffie, Giroux and Manso’s equation can be written in the same form, with λ\lambda in the place of the collision frequency and the pooling of two types in the place of a collision (our derivation; Box 19.1). Reading meetings as collisions is an analogy (our analogy), and it fails on conservation: collisions conserve mass, momentum and energy, while here both agents end with the sum, so evidence is copied (our judgment). The average type grows as eλte^{\lambda t} (our derivation; Box 1.2), and the distribution of beliefs converges to that of complete information. Section 19.1.2 gives the conditions on sources that Section 1.6.4 raised.2

19.1.2 Aggregation and the speed of convergence

What spreads in information percolation is aggregation, the pooling of what many people know (our judgment). Agents start with signals of their own, some with none, and every meeting adds the evidence of both, which drops the root model’s candidate default of a single information source, one message copied (our judgment; Sections 5.2 and 5.8).

Duffie, Giroux and Manso’s model has no price, and the private auctions of Section 19.1.3 no public one, so aggregation happens inside each person; with a continuum of agents all private signals together amount to complete information, and aggregation efficiency, how much of the dispersed information has been pooled, can be measured only by the cross-sectional distribution of what individuals know (our judgment).

Duffie, Giroux and Manso show that the distribution of beliefs across agents converges to that of complete information exponentially at the rate λ\lambda, regardless of the number mm of agents at each meeting; their results suggest that varying the size of the groups does not facilitate information transmission, at least in terms of the rate of convergence of posteriors. A bound in their proof comes from the first term of the Wild summation, the agents who have met no one since the start, whose share falls as e−λte^{-\lambda t}; those agents keep beliefs from converging faster than that, and the starting signals enter the bound only as a factor (our derivation; Box 19.1 and the appendix). They have not shown that the invariance extends to groups whose size varies at random from meeting to meeting, and add that it does hold under technical conditions on the distribution of group sizes, as Semyon Malamud later told them in a private communication (as reported by Duffie, Giroux and Manso).

Besides the assumptions of Section 19.1.1, these results rest on a continuum of agents and random matching independent for almost every pair, which together make it almost sure that two who meet share no sources (our judgment). The authors assume such matching and do not show that such a process exists. On a network the same news can come back along several paths, and adding types is right only when sources do not overlap (Section 7.5).3

19.1.3 Trading as spreading

In Duffie and Manso’s private auctions, agents at each meeting learn the types of those they meet from the bids, and this information is not revealed to agents who do not take part (Sections 3.6 and 5.3). In what they call the unique symmetric Nash equilibrium of each auction, one in which no bidder gains by changing her bid alone, each agent bids her posterior probability that the variable is high, so informed agents learn each other’s types from their bids.

They set their model against a literature that gives rational expectations strategic foundations in centralized markets, while a number of important markets, among them over-the-counter and private-auction markets, are decentralized, trades being struck privately so that only the two sides see the price. In their example trading reveals information fully, in contrast to previous models. A footnote states a condition behind this: with a continuum of agents, two who meet know that they will almost surely never meet anyone who has met the other, directly or indirectly, so they are not strategic about what they reveal, while in a market with few agents this would obviously be a concern.

In Chapter 16’s centralized market, with noise, the price reveals only part of what is known (Sections 16.2 and 16.3). Here revelation comes pair by pair and accumulates over time, with no price that everyone sees (our judgment). Each trade’s price is a private observation of its two sides, so there is no single price-implied probability qq, only a cross-section of traded prices; this drops the candidate default of centralized trading with public prices (our judgment; Section 5.3).4

19.1.4 Costly search and new signals

Duffie, Malamud and Manso (2009) let individuals choose the effort with which they search for others to learn from, each being matched at an intensity proportional to her current effort (Section 6.6). With proportional search costs, their equilibria have trigger policies that cut search to a minimal level once enough information is obtained, and additional public information reduces the sharing of private information, in some cases with a net negative welfare effect, judged as agents enter the market (Sections 4.7 and 12.10).

When the contact rate moves with each agent’s precision, the collision frequency of Wild’s equation is no longer constant and the explicit Wild summation no longer applies; how fast news spreads then depends on the state, which drops the fixed contact rates of a deterministic mean field together with unlimited attention (our judgment).

Duffie and Manso also let each agent receive, at a rate ρ\rho independently across agents, a new private set of signals, which drops the default of one-time injection, news present only at the start (our judgment). The note explains why this chapter uses only the setting.5

19.2 Two routes from word of mouth to prices

In Andrei and Cujean’s model agents update by Bayes’ rule and learn from prices; in Pedersen’s, views are averaged by a fixed rule and no one learns from the price. Each changes different parts of the root model.

19.2.1 Andrei and Cujean’s route

Section 5.7 and Box 5.2 gave Andrei and Cujean’s (2017) setting: agents with exponential utility and a common coefficient of absolute risk aversion meet at random with intensity λ\lambda and share their signals, and they trade a risky asset whose noisy supply prevents prices from fully revealing the payoff, so noise traders stay (Section 16.10). From the root model this takes four changes (our judgment; Box 5.2): signals pooled in random meetings in place of one copied message, so there is no informed fraction and no S-curve; constant absolute risk aversion in place of log utility; a risk premium in place of the fact unrelated to aggregate risk; and inference from prices in place of no learning from prices and first-order knowledge. Meetings are uniformly random, so homogeneous mixing stays (our judgment).

In their single-asset model momentum, recent winners continuing to rise, means returns correlated positively across periods (our judgment). Their Theorem 3.1 states that returns exhibit momentum at a given lag if and only if precision elasticity at that lag is higher than one; precision elasticity compares the percentage growth of investors’ average precision, which counts their prior, their private signals and what they learn from prices, with the percentage growth of their average private precision (Box 19.3). Three statements of different strength go with it. The condition on elasticity is necessary and sufficient. Information flowing at an increasing rate is necessary (Section 5.7), in the sense that Box 19.3 derives: the next increment must exceed a self-weighted average of past increments, which can hold for a while after increments start to fall (our derivation); and with a linear flow momentum never obtains (Section 16.10). Learning from prices is the main channel through which momentum arises in their model, and it is necessary but not sufficient; the price-learning channel improves precision by the square of the incremental number of private signals, which in this book’s reading is why the rate of inflow matters (our judgment).

Returns are martingales, with no serial correlation, when λ=0\lambda = 0 or when λ→∞\lambda \to \infty, and the model predicts a hump-shaped relation between momentum and the meeting intensity, momentum arising only where information flows in at an increasing rate. The authors do not attempt to match the magnitude of momentum, nor the exact length of the decaying pattern.

Investors know at time tt the cross-sectional average of their precision for the next period, because it is just a function of time, although no one knows her own. Each agent starts with an initial signal and gathers the rest in random meetings, and in the baseline model no external source sends news later, so random meetings inside the model produce the path of precision, but the path is deterministic, known in advance, untouched by the price and without an S-curve (our judgment). Chapter 16 read this as switching off uncertainty about how far information has spread on average (Section 16.7).

Section 9.5 gave their rumor, a common error that every signal carries and no one can separate from the fundamental, and derived that pooling more such signals leaves the rumor’s part of the error in place (our derivation). To let agents eventually learn about it, the authors give them an additional signal at t=3t = 3, as the economy approaches the final liquidation date. In the three cases of their Figure 8, returns mostly exhibit momentum when the rumor is fairly precise, because agents’ precision rises over time; with a less precise rumor, portfolio adjustments leave momentum in the first period and induce reversal in the second as the price gradually corrects; and when the rumor’s precision is low, agents become cautious about their information and their precision does not improve enough to generate momentum.6

19.2.2 Pedersen’s route

Pedersen adds rational agents and markets to a standard DeGroot model (Section 13.6) and calls his framework fundamentally different from standard models where people learn from prices. People in his model seek to learn the facts directly through communication, and their trades inform the price.

A naive agent selectively follows a subset of people she views as most informative or most entertaining and uses the same method for updating each round: her view next round is a fixed weighted average of the views she listens to, xi(t+1)=Aix(t)x_i(t+1) = A_i x(t), with xx the views and AiA_i her row of weights. A rational agent listens to everyone in the first round and never changes her view after it, since no new information arrives. Pedersen reads a stubborn agent as a fanatic or someone who deliberately tries to shut down all alternative views. Fanatics and long-term and short-term rational agents are his hardheaded agents, so called because they keep constant views after time 1, and trading starts at time 1, after the first round of communication. Under his assumption that every naive agent listens to a hardheaded agent or to someone who listens to someone who does, each naive view tends to a convex combination, a weighted average, of the views of fanatics and rational agents (Box 19.2).

The asset’s fundamental value is v+u(t)v + u(t), u(t)u(t) being a publicly observed random walk and vv the part investors try to learn. The equilibrium price is 1−c1 - c times the wealth-weighted average view plus u(t)u(t) less a risk premium, plus cc times the expected next price, with cc set by the wealth of short-term rational traders and the speed of revelation; the risk premium depends on the supply of shares, the fundamental risk, the speed of revelation and investors’ aggregate wealth, and wealth is shorthand for risk-bearing capacity. With no short-term traders cc is zero (our derivation), and the price is the wealth-weighted average view plus u(t)u(t) less the risk premium, the same form as the root model’s price rule (our judgment). Five other things differ (our judgment): risk-bearing capacity in place of log utility with equal, fixed wealth; trade from differing views, with no noise traders; a separate risk premium, dropping the fact unrelated to aggregate risk; averaging on a network, dropping homogeneous mixing and Bayesian updating together (Table 5.2); and, with short-term traders, their forecast of the next price, dropping first-order knowledge where payoffs depend on others’ views.

The price can stay a bubble as long as fanatics remain unmoved by rational opinion and fundamentals remain unrevealed. This is the shape of a bubble. Its size is a different matter: with no short-term traders the price, net of u(t)u(t) and the risk premium, lies within the range of views, so the most extreme view, an input of the model, bounds the bubble (our derivation). If the model alone produced GameStop’s 25-fold rise, someone would have had to hold a valuation at least as extreme (our judgment). Chapter 21 takes up the size of bubbles.

Pedersen’s model has momentum of its own: before revelation his equation (33) splits the expected return into a value term and the next change of the network part of the price, weighted by the probability that the network dynamics continue, so returns are predicted by value and momentum factors. Proxying that momentum by past price changes relies on an approximation that he makes precise in particular settings; in the symmetric naive case below, his Proposition 7 gives a value-and-momentum strategy a positive expected return. This momentum comes from a network drift that persists while the network keeps echoing views, slowing in the symmetric case, with no accelerating inflow of information (our judgment).

Views change inside the model by the fixed DeGroot rule, and only the network of who listens to whom is given from outside (our judgment; Section 1.7).7 The rule has no term like the root model’s βI\beta I, so how fast a view spreads does not depend on how many already hold it, and the averaging does not speed itself up (our judgment; Sections 5.6 and 13.6.2). One case has an especially simple solution: naive agents with equal wealth who listen to each other symmetrically, with at least one naive agent and one fanatic. There the average naive view approaches its limit by a constant factor each round, and if it starts below its limit, the part of the price that the network sets rises monotonically before the fundamental is revealed while its changes fall monotonically, a result he proves only for this symmetric case. In this book’s derivation, if the hardheaded views are already in place in round 0, every change is that factor times the one before, so in this symmetric case the average view slows from the first round (our derivation; Box 19.2).

On a layered network, where a view reaches most people only after it has been retold several times, relay delay alone can make the average view rise slowly, then fast, then slowly, an S-shape (our derivation). Figure 19.1 shows both for the same people: on the symmetric network each change is 0.6 of the one before, while on the layered network the changes rise to a peak in round 4 and then fall (our calculation).

An S-shape by itself therefore cannot tell relay delay from self-acceleration, word of mouth that speeds up with the share who already know, as in the root model (our judgment; Sections 5.2 and 5.6). In this book’s reading relay delay is a source of S-curves outside the four of Section 5.6 (our judgment). What linear averaging cannot produce is acceleration that grows with the share who already hold a view (our judgment). Pedersen suggests letting the attention weights depend on the agents’ past profits, an extension that could add such acceleration (our judgment) and is not part of his model.8

19.2.4 Copying one message and pooling signals

In the root model people copy one message: the state is the informed fraction, and hearing the message again adds nothing (Sections 5.2 and 13.1). Under pooling, evidence adds, Duffie and Manso’s types when sources do not overlap and Andrei and Cujean’s precision with each new signal (Sections 19.1.1 and 19.2.1), and either structure keeps an error shared at the source, Andrei and Cujean’s rumor (Section 9.5) or Hellwig’s common noise (Section 16.3). For a price, how far news has spread is then the informed fraction under copying and the cross-sectional distribution of precision under pooling, and neither column of Table 19.1 has the root model’s informed fraction (our judgment).10

19.2.5 Stein’s bound on the price effect

Sections 2.6, 2.10 and 15.3.1 gave Stein’s (2008) bound, that the ideas that reach the most investors tend to have relatively little real information content, its critical reliance on all investors being rational Bayesians, and conditions under which it is likely to be loose (working-paper version). One of them matters here: conversations among individual investors, likely to compete less with one another, could potentially have bigger effects on prices than conversations among professional money managers, even though the latter do occur and may be quite informative when they do (working-paper version).

Andrei and Cujean’s agents are rational Bayesians whose meetings no one chooses, so Stein’s premise holds while his mechanism, the choice of what to share with a competitor, is absent (our judgment). Pedersen’s naive agents, who count repetition as news (Section 13.6.3), break his premise of rational Bayesians, and they fit his exception that news obtained face to face may be viewed as particularly salient and compelling (working-paper version; Section 2.10) (our judgment).11

19.2.6 Problems left open

The book’s open problem, joining a real spreading process to prices, takes two forms here (our judgment; Sections 1.7 and 5.7). One puts the root model’s Bass curve into Andrei and Cujean’s condition for momentum; the other lets the informed fraction enter a market maker’s quotes, as Box 17.3 began. Box 19.3 gives both as this book’s own derivation, not checked within a full model (our derivation).

Section 5.7 derived that with three unknowns and one price, the value, the informed fraction and whether there is news, revelation in the root model may be neither instant nor monotone (our derivation), to be checked against the models of Chapters 16 and 19, and Section 16.7 left the check here. None of this chapter’s models can make it (our judgment): Duffie and Manso’s models have no public price, Andrei and Cujean’s agents know the average precision in advance, and no one in Pedersen’s model learns from prices. The question stays open.

19.3 Information and capital

The root model assumes spreaders as traders, those who spread news being those who trade on it (Table 5.1 in Section 5.8); this section drops that default. Sections 1.6.3 and 7.6 asked what happens when those who know and those who hold the capital that sets a price differ.

19.3.1 Taking information to the capital

Chapter 15 gave three ways information moves from those who have more of it than capital to those who have capital (our judgment). In Ljungqvist and Qian’s (2016) cases, short sellers with small resources publish reports after taking a position (working-paper version), and publishing takes the information to the shareholders, whose capital can push the price down (our judgment; Sections 1.6.3 and 15.3.3). Van Bommel’s (2003) leader, whose trading capacity is small, spreads a coarsened rumor after trading, which takes her information to the capital of her followers (our judgment; Section 15.3.4). Admati and Pfleiderer’s (1990) information seller does not trade on his own account in the financial market and sells his signal to traders who do (Section 15.3.2).12

19.3.2 Risk sharing among those who know

Merton (1987) assumes that each investor is risk averse and chooses his portfolio by the Markowitz–Tobin mean-variance criterion, and that he puts security kk in his portfolio only if he knows about it, knowing meaning knowing the parameters of its return, on which, by the model’s assumption, all who know agree (working-paper version). If investors must pay a significant set-up cost before they can process a firm’s news, he argues, each will follow only a subset of the traded securities (working-paper version). The group who know a security is its investor base.

Merton derives an extra term in the expected return and from it a shadow cost that lowers the firm’s value below its value with complete information (working-paper version). In plain words, only those who know bear the security’s firm-specific risk, so the fewer they are, the larger each one’s share of that risk and the higher the return they require (our judgment). The effect of a wider investor base on value is greatest for lesser-known firms and for firms with large firm-specific variances, and better-known firms with larger investor bases tend to earn lower expected returns (working-paper version). In this book’s derivation, with the firm’s weight in the market held fixed, the price depends on the share of investors who know the security through the ratio of those who do not know to those who do; the dependence runs through risk sharing, with no difference of beliefs, while the root model’s price moves in a straight line with the informed fraction (our derivation; Section 5.3). Mean-variance preferences with a coefficient of aggregate risk aversion (working-paper version) stand here in place of the root model’s log utility with equal, fixed wealth (our judgment). Merton notes that, given his return structure, his expression for the shadow cost applies to all concave utility maximizers (working-paper version), so in this book’s reading risk sharing does not need mean-variance preferences (our judgment).

Merton takes the share who know as given in his model (working-paper version). A proper development, he writes, needs a dynamic model in which current shareholders have expectations about the future path of that share; if a favorable story raises those expectations, the price should rise immediately, even if the newly informed take time to buy (working-paper version). In this book’s reading, spreading of awareness that holders can foresee enters the price in advance, and the price then responds only to surprises in its path; the root model’s price moves step by step with the informed fraction because no one learns from the price and the informed do not foresee others’ later buying (our judgment).13

19.3.3 When the capital is present

Capital can be split between groups of people and over time (our judgment). Merton splits it between those who know a security and those who do not (working-paper version); in Shleifer and Vishny’s account, arbitrage is conducted by a relatively small number of highly specialized investors who take large positions using other people’s money, so that brains and resources are separated by an agency relationship, and outside investors withdraw funds after a bad record (working-paper version; Chapter 16). Call this population segmentation.

Time segmentation splits the same people by when they are present. In Duffie’s (2010) illustrative model a fraction of investors are inattentive for kk periods after each trade, while the rest trade every period, and the frequent investors could be viewed as professional intermediaries; Section 10.7 gave his values and the drop and overshoot after a supply shock. Duffie names the cost of maintaining perfect attentiveness as a primary reason for the inattentive investors’ limited participation, and counts among the fundamentals of prices the state variables that set how much capital various investors can supply now and later.

Writing ff for Duffie’s inattentive fraction, the share of investors who have had a chance to react by the jjth period, counting the period of the shock, is 1−f+fj/k1 - f + fj/k for jj up to kk, rising in a straight line like a spread with no word of mouth and delays spread evenly over kk periods (our derivation); the likeness is one of shape only (our judgment). The inattentive know the state of the market (Section 10.7), so the limit is on acting, and their delay is this book’s attention cycle, only some investors being present to decide at any moment, which drops unlimited attention together with spreaders as traders (our judgment). Sections 12.6 and 18.8 gave the pre-announced supply and the deletions that fall in the risk premium.14

19.3.4 Thought leadership and wealth

In Pedersen’s model, hardheaded agents become thought leaders due to their commitment to their views, while influencers, naive agents whom others follow, are not committed to any view, and their fluid opinions end up without thought leadership. By his equations (20) and (23), a naive agent’s influencer value is her share of wealth plus the influencer values of those who listen to her, weighted by their attention to her, and a hardheaded agent’s thought leadership is his share of wealth plus naive agents’ attention to him, weighted by their influencer values (our judgment); in the long-term price, fanatics’ mispricings are weighted by their thought leadership.

Influence can be cut loose from wealth: when investors learn through their social network, even a penniless investor can wield a large influence on prices as a thought leader or influencer, especially if the followers are wealthy or many. In the root model the weight each person’s starting information gets in the price is set by wealth (Section 5.3); here it is set by thought leadership, which can differ greatly from both the share of wealth and the precision of the information (our judgment).15

19.3.5 Overlapping holders across assets

Sections 1.6.5 and 10.8 gave Cohen and Frazzini’s (2008) test of whether customers’ news reaches suppliers’ prices faster where more funds hold both firms, and the authors’ reading of the gap as varying inattention (working-paper version). Common ownership, the same funds holding two linked firms, is a bridge between the capital that sees the customer’s news and the capital that prices the supplier, and the bridge sets how fast news crosses from one asset to the other (our judgment). Their tables cannot tell this reading from inattention or from the network of holders of Chapter 7 (Section 10.8).16

19.3.6 Three channels from awareness to price

Awareness can reach a price through three channels, which differ in mechanism and in conditions (our judgment; Table 19.2). In the root model’s channel, the average belief, the price is the wealth-weighted average belief, which needs short selling (Section 5.3), and as more people know, the price moves toward the informed belief, in whichever direction the news points.

Merton’s channel is risk sharing: more people who know a stock spread its firm-specific risk over more capital (our judgment; Section 19.3.2). In his model those who know agree on the parameters by assumption (working-paper version), so the channel needs neither differing opinions nor limits on short selling (our judgment).

Miller’s (1977) channel is the optimistic tail. In a market with restricted short selling and differing estimates of returns, demand comes from the minority who hold the most optimistic expectations, and he hypothesizes that anything that increases awareness of a stock will increase its price, a hypothesis that this book’s sources do not test (our judgment; Section 10.9). If 1% of investors must hold a security to absorb an issue, the price is set by the most optimistic 1% when all investors look at it, and by the most optimistic 10% when only one in ten does. The effect matters most where opinion diverges most, which tends to be in riskier stocks, and publicity need not be favorable to attract attention. He cautions that the fraction of investors looking at a stock is probably not independent of its price, so attention may follow the price as well as move it (our judgment).

The three channels rest on different premises and can act together, and a test has to separate an effect on the level of prices from one on their path (our judgment). Merton’s channel raises a price as more people know of the stock (working-paper version), and so does Miller’s (our judgment). Only Miller’s needs restricted short selling and differing opinions, and its size grows with their spread (our judgment). Under Miller’s hypothesis, bad news that draws attention can raise a price; in the root model it lowers the price (our judgment). This chapter attributes Merton’s reading that foreseen spreading enters the price in advance (Section 19.3.2) to risk sharing only and does not carry it to the other two channels (our judgment). 19.3.8 Information and risk-bearing capacity

Who holds information and who can bear risk are two questions (our judgment). The weight of a person’s information in the price is set by wealth in the root model (Section 5.3), by precision and the inverse of risk aversion in Hellwig’s (1980) large market (Section 16.3), by the risk-bearing capacity of those who know in Merton’s model and by thought leadership in Pedersen’s (our judgment; Sections 19.3.2 and 19.3.4). Those with the most information need not have the most capacity to bear risk, and a price reads how the two are matched (our judgment).

19.5 A trader’s checklist

None of these questions says what to buy or sell, and good answers do not make a trade safe (our judgment).

  1. Is the news one message copied, or many people’s signals pooled? Check: under copying, track the informed fraction; under pooling, track the distribution of precision across people. In Duffie, Giroux and Manso’s model, with a continuum of agents, random matching independent for almost every pair, a positive mass of informed agents and a starting distribution whose moment generating function is finite near zero, beliefs converge at the meeting rate, and the agents who have met no one keep them from converging faster (our derivation; Sections 19.1.2 and 19.2.4).
  2. Do those who set the price learn from it? Check: in Andrei and Cujean’s model, with constant absolute risk aversion and a noisy supply, they do, and momentum needs information to flow in at an increasing rate; in Pedersen’s they do not, and with no short-term traders the price lies within the range of views, so the most extreme view bounds a bubble (our derivation; Sections 19.2.1 and 19.2.2).
  3. Does a slow-then-fast path show word of mouth speeding itself up? Check: on a layered network, DeGroot averaging, which has no self-accelerating term, can give the same S-shape from relay delay alone (our derivation; Section 19.2.2 and Figure 19.1).
  4. Do those who know hold the capital, and is it present now? Check: information can sit with those who have little capital, and capital may return only after an attention cycle (our judgment; Sections 19.3.1 and 19.3.3).
  5. Through which channel does awareness reach this price? Check: the average belief needs short selling; risk sharing, in Merton’s model where by assumption those who know agree, needs neither differing opinions nor limits on short selling (working-paper version); the optimistic tail, a hypothesis that this book’s sources do not test, needs both (our judgment; Section 19.3.6 and Table 19.2).

19.6 Summary

  • In Duffie and Manso’s information percolation, with a continuum of agents, random matching independent for almost every pair, independent signals and the conditions of Box 1.2, the full state is the cross-sectional distribution of types, and the root model’s informed fraction is its projection onto who has heard something, with the derivation and its check in the appendix (our derivation; Section 19.1.1); in Duffie, Giroux and Manso’s version, with the same continuum and matching, a positive mass of informed agents and a starting distribution whose moment generating function is finite near zero, beliefs converge to complete information at the meeting rate, whatever the number of agents at each meeting, and the agents who have met no one keep them from converging faster (our derivation; Section 19.1.2).
  • In Andrei and Cujean’s model, with constant absolute risk aversion, a noisy supply and learning from prices, returns show momentum at a lag if and only if precision elasticity there exceeds one, which needs information to flow in at an increasing rate, and the average precision is a known function of time (Section 19.2.1).
  • In Pedersen’s model no one learns from prices; with no short-term traders, his cc being zero (our derivation), the price is the wealth-weighted average view plus a public part of value less a risk premium, the same form as the root model’s price rule (our judgment); in his symmetric case, with hardheaded views fixed from the start, the average view slows from the first round, while on a layered network relay delay alone can give an S-shape (our derivation), so an S-shape by itself cannot tell relay delay from self-acceleration (our judgment; Section 19.2.2 and Figure 19.1).
  • Joining the root model’s spreading curve to prices remains open: none of this chapter’s models can check revelation with three unknowns, and the two forms in Box 19.3 are unchecked derivations (our judgment; Section 19.2.6 and Box 19.3).
  • Awareness reaches a price through the average belief, which needs short selling; through risk sharing, which in Merton’s model, where by assumption those who know agree, needs neither disagreement nor limits on short selling (working-paper version); and through Miller’s optimistic tail, a hypothesis that this book’s sources do not test and that needs restricted short selling and differing opinions; people who know may not act for at least five reasons (our judgment; Sections 19.3.6 and 19.3.7).
