Chapter 14 Information Cascades and Herding

Joel Hasbrouck (1991) measured how trades follow one another in the first quarter of 1989. For his representative stock, Ames Department Stores, he related the direction of each trade, a purchase or a sale, to the directions of earlier trades and to past changes in the quotes, using all transactions in that quarter. The coefficients on the directions of the three trades before are .167, .120 and .078, and those on the fourth and fifth trades back are smaller, .021 and .034, the fourth not significantly different from zero at the usual levels (our judgment). He reads these estimates as strong positive autocorrelation in trades: purchases tend to follow purchases, and similarly for sales, a pattern at low lags that he calls highly typical. At low and moderate lags he finds a striking absence of the reversal that inventory control would produce, inventory control being a dealer’s adjustment of quotes to work off positions she does not want, and he finds the short-run predominance of positive autocorrelation more consistent with lagged adjustment to new information.1

Such runs have several possible sources (our judgment). Hasbrouck writes that inventory control induces serial dependencies in trades, as do price pressure and order fragmentation, the splitting of one large order into many small ones, and that lagged adjustment to information and exchange-mandated price smoothing, a rule that prices move without large jumps, also involve serial dependencies. People who buy because others have bought would give runs as well, and since the directions of trades alone do not separate these sources, this book does not read the runs as evidence that traders herd (our judgment).

The root model assumes no observational learning: no one sees others’ actions or the outcomes of their actions. Table 5.1 in Section 5.8 lists this default with the others, and this chapter drops it, asking when watching others makes actions agree, what then happens to information, and what changes when prices move with trades (Section 14.9).

14.1 Watching actions instead of signals

Banerjee (1992) gives an example. Two restaurants, A and B, stand next to each other, with prior probabilities of 51 percent that A is the better and 49 percent that B is. People arrive one after another, see the choices of those before them and pick one of the two, each with a private signal of the same quality. Of 100 people, 99 have signals that B is better, but the one whose signal favors A chooses first. For the second person, whose signal favors B, the two signals cancel out, so she goes by the prior and picks A; her choice then tells the third person nothing new, and the same holds for everyone after. Everyone ends up at A, though the information of all 100 together makes it practically certain that B is better.

Banerjee calls this herd behavior, everyone doing what everyone else is doing even when their private information suggests doing something quite different; this book’s word is herding. The second person’s decision to ignore her own information inflicts a negative externality on the rest of the population. This is Table 13.1’s cell for Bayesian learning from others’ actions.

Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer and Welch (1992) concentrate on the least informative case, in which people observe only the actions of those before them. In their binary example everyone has the same cost of adopting, 1/2, and the same gain from adopting, 0 or 1 with equal prior probability. Each person’s private signal is H or L; H comes with probability p>1/2p > 1/2 when the gain is 1 and with probability 1−p1 - p when it is 0, with the same precision pp for everyone. A person indifferent between adopting and rejecting does either with equal probability. If the two people before the third both adopted, even an L signal leads him to adopt, which starts an UP cascade; if both rejected, even an H signal leads him to reject, a DOWN cascade; if one adopted and the other rejected, the actions alone leave him where the first person stood, with an expected value of adopting of 1/2, so his signal decides.

In their definition, an information cascade occurs if a person’s action does not depend on his private signal. Since each later signal comes from the same distribution, the next person is in the cascade too, so a cascade once started lasts forever, even if it is wrong. The authors add that cascades can easily be broken if signals have different distributions, if public information arrives later or if the underlying value can change (Section 14.3 takes up later public information).

Everyone here updates by Bayes’ rule from a common prior, and the one default dropped is no observational learning (our judgment). Since a person’s payoff does not depend on what later people do, no one has a reason to act against the equilibrium to sway those who follow, and one can solve for the equilibrium person by person from the first (our judgment).2

14.2 Actions that agree while information stalls

In the binary example a cascade comes quickly. After two people the chances of an UP cascade, no cascade and a DOWN cascade are (1−p+p2)/2(1 - p + p^2)/2, p−p2p - p^2 and (1−p+p2)/2(1 - p + p^2)/2, and after an even number nn of people the chance of no cascade is (p−p2)n/2(p - p^2)^{n/2}, the rest split equally between the two cascades. The probability of not being in a cascade thus falls exponentially with the number of people; even for a signal barely better than a coin toss, it is below 0.1 percent after only 10 people. Cascades tend to start sooner when signals are more precise, and later the closer pp is to 1/2.

The cascade may well be the wrong one. Given that the gain is 1, the authors give the chances of the correct cascade, of no cascade and of the wrong cascade in closed form (Box 14.1), and find the probability of the wrong cascade remarkably high even for very informative signals; as people keep arriving, it tends to 0.368 when p=0.6p = 0.6 and to 0.192 when p=0.75p = 0.75 (our calculation). In their general model, with signals of finitely many values, a monotone likelihood ratio, a higher signal meaning a higher inferred gain, and no long-run ties, people who have learned enough not being indifferent between adopting and rejecting, the probability that a cascade eventually starts approaches one as the number of people grows, with high probability before many informative actions could have revealed the gain nearly perfectly, and often a wrong one that started much earlier.

Once a cascade has started, actions convey no information about private signals, so a person’s action does not improve later decisions. The public belief, the probability that a newcomer would put, from the history of actions alone, on the gain that the cascade favors, stays where it was when the cascade began, p(p+1)/[2(p2−p+1)]p(p + 1)/[2(p^2 - p + 1)], whichever pair of people started it. Had earlier people’s signals been observable instead, even a signal that its owner does not follow would join the common pool of knowledge, so a long enough series of opposing signals would eventually change behavior; following any sequence of signals, the two regimes act exactly alike until a cascade begins in the regime of observable actions, where it is then never reversed.

Two layers separate here (our judgment). The share adopting runs to one side and stays there, while what the public history says about the gain stops where the cascade began. The root model’s self-acceleration needs what people copy to carry the information (Section 5.2), and inside a cascade the copied actions carry none (our judgment). In log-odds, the logarithm of the odds, on which independent signals simply add, the public belief of a cascade is worth 1.33 private signals at p=0.6p = 0.6 and 1.31 at p=0.75p = 0.75, however many people follow (our calculation; Box 14.1), in line with the authors’ intuition that cascades aggregate the information of only a few early individuals’ actions. A Bayesian newcomer gives those who join a cascade no weight, and persuasion bias, counting each repetition as further evidence, does the opposite (our judgment; Section 13.6.3).

Figure 14.1 draws both layers for p=0.6p = 0.6 and p=0.75p = 0.75, the second the authors’ own numerical example. Counted in bits, where one bit halves the possibilities, so that the gain starts with one bit of uncertainty, the actions carry 0.051 and 0.294 bits about it in the limit, while the same people’s signals, were they public, would carry 0.554 and 0.993 bits after 30 people, against 0.029 and 0.189 in one signal (our calculation).3

14.3 Conditions for a cascade

Watching others does not by itself produce a herd. Banerjee notes that the mere fact that people observe the decisions of others does not guarantee herd behavior: when each person chooses her best guess of a mean, given her signal and earlier choices, with a loss quadratic in her error, the choices converge to the mean for almost every sequence of signals. He traces the difference to choices that in his main model are not always sufficient statistics for the information behind them, and conjectures that choices typically reveal signals when choices and signals are of comparable dimension and payoffs are continuous.

Smith and Sørensen (2000) explore how Bayes-rational people learn one after another from the discrete actions of others. A person’s private belief is the probability she gives the high state H by Bayes’ rule from her own signal. Private beliefs are bounded when their range stops short of both 0 and 1, and unbounded when it reaches both, so that in each direction some signal, however rare, can push a belief as close to certainty as one likes; a range reaching only one end is neither. With a single rational type, everyone ranking the actions alike in each state, and no noise, no one acting at random, a herd on some action almost surely arises in finite time; with unbounded private beliefs people almost surely settle on the optimal action, and with bounded ones, unless the public belief starts in a cascade on the most profitable action, a herd arises on another action with positive probability. Strictly bounded beliefs are the mainstay of Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer and Welch’s bad herds: in the binary example a signal multiplies the odds by p/(1−p)p/(1 - p) or divides them by it, and no more (our derivation).

In these models a wrong herd needs two conditions, actions that do not reveal the signals behind them and bounded private beliefs (our judgment); with unbounded beliefs herds still form, almost surely on the right action.

A herd and a cascade also differ. For Smith and Sørensen a cascade means belief convergence in finite time, the public belief entering a cascade set, the range of public beliefs at which a person’s action no longer depends on her own signal; every later rational person’s action is then dictated by history, so a cascade forces a herd, in which rational people of the same type all act alike. In their example with bounded beliefs, public beliefs that do not start in a cascade set never enter one, even though a herd always eventually starts, a result that obtains whenever the continuation functions, the rules by which each action moves the public likelihood ratio, are always increasing, since the ratio then never jumps into the closed cascade sets; that monotonicity is crucially violated in Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer and Welch’s world of discrete signals. Barring discrete signals, they sum up, cascades need not arise: no decision need ever be a foregone conclusion even during a herd. In this book’s reading, a cascade that starts in finite time also needs a single action able to carry the public belief into a cascade set, as discrete signals allow (our judgment).

The agreement is fragile. Public information less informative than one private signal can shatter a long-lasting cascade (their Result 3; Section 12.8), and very noisy public information released before the first decision can leave some people worse off in an ex ante sense (Result 2; Section 4.7). The authors call the uniformity brittle: small shocks can easily shift the behavior of many people.4

14.4 Confounded learning

Smith and Sørensen give an example. On a highway under construction, drivers going to Houston should take the high off-ramp in state H and the low one in state L, and those headed toward Dallas the opposite. If, at some public belief, a random car is as likely to go high in state H as in state L, no inference can be drawn from further decisions, and learning stops.

In their general model people may rank the actions differently, and types are unobserved, so that only statistical inferences can be drawn about any one person. Beliefs may then converge to a point where history offers no decisive lessons for anyone, and each type’s actions forever split between two actions, which they call confounded learning. Their Theorem 2 assumes at least two rational types and finds that confounding outcomes robustly exist and are invariant to noise, robustness being defined with respect to the model’s parameters, its preferences and its shares of types and noise, which this book reads as existence for some parameter values and those near them (our judgment). At any confounding outcome some pair of types has opposed preferences. Confounded learning is nongeneric if belief distributions are discrete, as in the binary example, and with more than two actions and unbounded beliefs, generically no confounding outcome exists. With two actions and opposed preferences, a confounded learning point exists generically when beliefs are bounded, there are two types and both are active over some range of public beliefs; with unbounded beliefs one exists if the opposed types have sufficiently different preferences and, their introduction adds, not too unequal shares, so the outcome exists even when an incorrect herd is impossible; the further premises are in the note.

Noise, people randomly committed to particular actions, washes out in the long run and does not affect convergence; in our reading, rational types with opposed motives produce this kind of stop (our judgment).

The results of this chapter in which unbounded beliefs give learning all assume that preferences agree (our judgment): Smith and Sørensen’s Theorems 1(b) and 3(b) assume a single rational type, and in the network model of Section 14.6 Acemoglu, Dahleh, Lobel and Ozdaglar assume that, given the state, everyone prefers the same action, listing heterogeneous preferences among the questions their framework could study (working-paper version).

In a market, traders with opposite motives on the same news, some selling on a signal to cut a risk they carry elsewhere and others buying on it, could in suitable proportions make the order flow, the net of buy and sell orders, uninformative about direction (our judgment). Buying, selling and staying out are three actions, so with unbounded beliefs Theorem 2(e) makes such a point generically absent (our judgment), while with bounded beliefs models with more than two actions still have confounding outcomes, since only two actions may well be taken over a range of public beliefs. Smith and Sørensen’s model has no prices, and Chapter 16 leaves open whether a confounding point survives a price that moves with trades.5

14.5 Three forms of stopped aggregation

Pooling can stop in three forms, which Table 14.1 sets side by side (our judgment). In uniform actions everyone of a type does the same, and information stops where a cascade began or, in Smith and Sørensen’s herds with one type and bounded beliefs, keeps growing only toward a limit short of the truth. In silence the informed stop acting: Avery and Zemsky report Lee’s (1995) result that information cascades arise if there are transaction costs, since informed traders will then almost surely stop trading at some point, after which no new information reaches the market (as reported by Avery and Zemsky; working-paper version); Chapter 21 reads Lee’s paper. In offsetting split actions, Smith and Sørensen’s confounded learning, the types keep acting differently and their actions say nothing about the state (Section 14.4). Avery and Zemsky’s herding with prices is better read as a delay, keeping information about the new value out until prices adjust while information about whether an event occurred keeps flowing (our judgment; working-paper version; Section 14.9).

When pooling stops, actions may agree, cease or split, so whether actions agree does not by itself show whether information is still flowing (our judgment).6

14.6 Learning across a network

Acemoglu, Dahleh, Lobel and Ozdaglar study a perfect Bayesian equilibrium in which each person has a signal, sees the past actions of a randomly drawn set of earlier people and chooses one of two actions, everyone preferring the same action given the state (working-paper version). A finite group is excessively influential if infinitely many people, with a probability uniformly bounded away from 0, observe only the actions of some of its members; such a group exists exactly when the network has nonexpanding observations, and their Theorem 1 finds no asymptotic learning in such networks (working-paper version). Asymptotic learning means that the probability of the right action converges to 1, which asks less than the convergence of beliefs in Smith and Sørensen (our judgment). Their Theorem 2 states that with unbounded private beliefs, meaning that the likelihood ratios that signals imply have no bound, and expanding observations, asymptotic learning occurs in every equilibrium (working-paper version), which the authors read as showing that unbounded private beliefs are sufficient for asymptotic learning for most, but not all, network topologies (working-paper version).

With bounded private beliefs there is no asymptotic learning in the network where everyone sees all past actions, nor in most common deterministic and stochastic networks (working-paper version). Their Theorem 4 gives an exception: in certain stochastic networks asymptotic learning is possible with bounded private beliefs, because some people decide on the basis of limited observations, so that enough new information keeps arriving (working-paper version).

Whether a consensus rests on a few voices turns on how concentrated influence is in models that average opinions (Chapter 13), and on unbounded beliefs and expanding observations in models that infer from actions (our judgment).7

14.7 Learning from others’ outcomes

Young (2009) notes that some models of social learning assume that the payoffs of earlier adopters are fully observable, while others assume only that the act of adoption is observable, and the latter are usually called herding models (working-paper version).

In social learning people adopt once the outcomes among earlier adopters give enough evidence (working-paper version; Section 5.6); in Ryan and Gross’s survey of hybrid corn, early adopters, in some sense, gave their neighbors a community laboratory (as reported by Young; working-paper version; Section 11.7). In Young’s model the state variable is the integral of the adoption curve, since it measures the cumulative information generated by all earlier adopters from the time they first adopted (working-paper version): use produces the information, and the evidence grows with cumulative adoption, which the root model’s informed fraction cannot represent (our judgment). When some people adopt even though no one else has, such a process strictly decelerates near the origin, in Young’s words “irrespective of the distribution generating it”, his Proposition 3 stating it formally for a density of information thresholds that is bounded away from zero near the origin and has a continuous bounded derivative, though deceleration need not occur when observations are bunched at particular points in time, as with a new crop whose outcomes farmers see at the end of each growing season (working-paper version). Young finds social learning consistent with the curve for hybrid corn, though he cannot say that it was the sole explanatory factor (working-paper version).

In Bala and Goyal’s (1998) model, the action a person chooses each period brings a random reward and also information about the true payoffs, and people use only the outcomes of their neighbors’ actions, drawing no inferences from the choices themselves (working-paper version). In their example a royal family, a small set whom everyone observes, can through bad results lead everyone to the action with known payoffs, after which no further information is generated and society is locked into an inferior choice (working-paper version; Sections 9.5 and 13.7). In a cascade, information stops because actions no longer reveal signals; in lock-in it stops because no one uses the other action, so its outcomes are no longer produced (our judgment).

With path dependence, an outcome set by early random draws, and lock-in, an outcome that later people do not leave, this chapter also drops the root model’s default of a deterministic mean field, under which spreading follows its average path (Chapter 8). In the binary example, when a cascade starts and which way it goes depend on the first few signals (our judgment; Section 14.2), and Banerjee writes that since the herd externality is of the positive feedback type, the pattern of choices will be very volatile across several plays of the same game, the first few signals, partly random, deciding where the first crowd forms.8

14.8 Four results tying learning to unbounded signals

Four results tie complete learning to unbounded signals, signals whose strength has no bound, which in Smith and Sørensen’s sequential model and Acemoglu et al.’s network model (working-paper version) make private beliefs unbounded; each holds for its own reason and with its own exceptions (our judgment; Table 14.2).

  • An auction. In Milgrom’s (1981) auction of kk objects, let WnW^n be the k+1k + 1st highest bid with nn bidders and VnV^n the most that any bidder would pay under perfect information. Some signal must then be possible that is arbitrarily strong evidence for the higher value against the lower, a condition on one side only (our judgment). Milgrom reads the theorem as indicating that prices may give a good approximation of value when nn is large.
  • Sequential learning. With one rational type and no noise, unbounded beliefs, reaching certainty in both directions, give complete learning and bounded ones a possible wrong herd (Section 14.3). With two or more rational types and two actions, confounded learning can stop learning even with unbounded beliefs when the opposed types’ preferences are sufficiently different (Section 14.4).
  • Learning across a network. Unbounded beliefs with expanding observations give asymptotic learning, and with bounded beliefs most common networks do not learn; Theorem 4 gives an exception (working-paper version; Section 14.6).
  • Learning from outcomes. In Bala and Goyal’s model, people who can generate arbitrarily large amounts of positive information with positive probability may let groups whose neighborhoods do not overlap overcome the royal family’s negative information (working-paper version). Their Proposition 4.1 gives complete learning in a connected society, royal family included, when every optimal action meets this condition and infinitely many people, with at most KK neighbors each and a prior on the true state of at least some bound, have neighborhoods that overlap only in the royal family (working-paper version). Two exceptions need no unbounded information. Their Theorem 4.1, for infinitely many locally independent people with at most KK neighbors each and priors on the truth above a bound, imposes no restriction on how informative actions are, though a royal family leaves at most one such person, so it then no longer ensures complete learning; and their condition (H), that for each state some person’s prior lies as close as one likes to certainty of it, a strong restriction in their words, makes incomplete learning less likely than any bound even with a royal family of at most KK, in an infinite connected society with at most KK neighbors each (working-paper version).

The reasons differ. In sequential learning with discrete actions an action records only which range a signal fell in, so without signals strong enough to cross a threshold the flow of information stops short of the truth (our judgment). Milgrom’s price is an order statistic that, with kk fixed, depends on the most extreme few signals (our judgment). Bala and Goyal’s lock-in comes from a shared royal family and from information that only use produces, and unbounded positive information lets a good run somewhere outweigh the royal family’s bad one (our judgment). The exceptions show that unbounded signals are not necessary for learning in general (Theorem 4 of Acemoglu et al.; Theorem 4.1 and condition (H) of Bala and Goyal; working-paper version), and not sufficient when observations do not expand or, with two actions, when preferences are opposed enough (our judgment).

14.9 Prices that move with trades

Avery and Zemsky (1998) add a price that moves with trades. Their model is a special case of Glosten and Milgrom’s (1985) in which the noise traders, who trade for reasons unrelated to information, have completely inelastic demand; a competitive market maker, a dealer who posts the prices at which she will buy and sell, sets the prices, and traders learn from the history of trades, so the model also drops no learning from prices (working-paper version; Chapters 16 and 17). This book reads their results in three layers (our judgment).

The first layer concerns cascades. In their Definition 1 an information cascade occurs when, given the trading history, the distribution of trades does not depend on the true value, so that no new information reaches the market, and their Proposition 2 states that an information cascade never occurs in market equilibrium (working-paper version). This book counts three premises behind it (our judgment): minimal useful information, so that there is always private information in the economy; limited frictions, since with transaction costs informed traders almost surely stop trading at some point (Section 14.5); and noise traders, without whom the no-trade theorem of Milgrom and Stokey applies and the market breaks down (working-paper version; Section 4.4). This layer does not need uncertainty in a single dimension (our judgment).

The second layer concerns herding under monotonic signals. In their Definition 2 a trader engages in herd behavior if he buys when his own signal puts the value below its starting expectation and the trading history puts it above, or sells in the reverse case, and strictly prefers doing so (working-paper version). A signal is monotonic if there is a value v(x)v(x) such that the expectation given the signal and any trading history always lies between v(x)v(x) and the expectation given the history alone, and a trader with a monotonic signal never engages in herd behavior, so when traders have an informational advantage on a single dimension, the new asset value, price adjustments by a competitive market maker prevent any herd behavior (working-paper version).

The third layer concerns herding under uncertainty in several dimensions. In their Definition 4 there is event uncertainty when the probability that the value equals the market maker’s initial expectation lies strictly between 0 and 1, that is, when the market does not know whether anything has happened (working-paper version). Their first information structure adds event uncertainty to the signals of Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer and Welch: traders are informed if there is an information event and then have such signals, with precision pp, and they know whether an event has occurred (working-paper version). Event uncertainty added this way makes signals non-monotonic and herd behavior possible, though it can also leave signals monotonic (working-paper version). In this structure herding occurs with positive probability when p<1p < 1, though not when p=1p = 1, and with positive probability it is misdirected (working-paper version). New information still reaches the market during herding, because the consensus among the informed traders is itself informative and the market maker is learning that there was an information event (working-paper version). Herding keeps information about the new asset value from entering the market, and rational actors, the market maker included, account for this; after enough time the market maker learns enough that prices adjust, which ends the herding, and in this structure herding cannot move the price away from 1/2 by more than the information in one trade (working-paper version).

In their general model the price is a martingale that converges to the true value, so there is no excess volatility, nor can price bubbles be both likely to occur and extreme (working-paper version). Their second added dimension is composition uncertainty, the shares of the different types of trader not being common knowledge. When the market is uncertain both whether the value has changed and whether traders are well or poorly informed on average, herding can lead to significant, short-run price movements that do not reflect the true value (working-paper version).

In this book’s reading, under the three premises, prices that move with trades rule out cascades in the sense of Definition 1; under event uncertainty herding holds back information about direction until the price adjusts, and with composition uncertainty too it can move prices away from the value for a while (our judgment). Herding under event uncertainty would give runs of purchases like Hasbrouck’s, as would lagged adjustment, split orders and smoothing (our judgment); Chapter 17 takes up how a market maker reads the order flow.10

14.10 Acting on how many have adopted

Table 13.1 has a second cell in its column of others’ actions, for rules taken as given (Section 13.10): Granovetter’s threshold, the number or proportion of others who must make a decision before a given person does so (Chapter 11), and Bass’s model, in which imitators “learn,” in some sense, from those who have already bought. Both act on the count of visible adopters, and Bass’s imitation is the simplest form of watching actions (our judgment).

In the binary example an UP or DOWN cascade begins as soon as a person observes two more adoptions than rejections, or two more rejections than adoptions (Box 14.1). That is a threshold too, derived from Bayes’ rule and coarse actions, and it counts only actions that carry information, leaving out everyone who follows the cascade, while a mechanical threshold counts everyone visible (our judgment).11

14.11 Waiting to learn

So far the order of moves has been fixed in advance, exogenous sequencing in the literature’s term, which Brunnermeier sets against endogenous sequencing, where people decide when to move. If each person can decide when to decide, everybody would want to decide last in order to profit from the positive information externalities of others’ decisions. Brunnermeier reports several models.

In Gale’s (1996) model of two people, the unique equilibrium does not fully reveal information and need not be efficient: if both signals lie between 0 and the threshold, nobody invests, though investing would be socially optimal, and if nobody invested in the first two periods, investment stops forever, an investment collapse (as reported by Brunnermeier).

In Zhang’s (1997) model the precision of each private signal is itself private information, and the equilibrium shows an initial delay until the person with the most precise signal invests; everyone then follows at once, since the second person’s choice carries no additional information, so that after a delay investment cascades start suddenly, and as the number of people increases, each person tends to wait longer, since someone else more likely has a more precise signal (as reported by Brunnermeier). Gul and Lundholm (1995) assume that time is continuous, and in their model endogenous sequencing leads to informationally efficient clustering (as reported by Brunnermeier), so letting people choose when to move does not by itself produce a delayed cascade (our judgment).

Waiting to learn is one of the reasons in Section 5.3 why people who know may not act, and Chapter 19 sets the sources of delayed action side by side.12

14.13 A trader’s checklist

None of these questions says what to buy or sell, and good answers do not make a trade safe (our judgment).

  1. How many independent decisions lie behind the crowd? Check: count the decisions that pushed the crowd one way before the others followed, leaving out pairs that cancel; in Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer and Welch’s binary example, with Bayes’ rule and only earlier actions seen, a cascade’s public belief is worth between one and 4/3 private signals in log-odds, 1.33 at a precision of 0.6 and 1.31 at 0.75, however many acted (our calculation; Section 14.2 and Box 14.1).
  2. Could a small piece of public news turn it? Check: in the same example a public signal less informative than one private signal can shatter a long-lasting cascade (Section 12.8), so agreement formed by imitation is fragile against contrary news (our judgment).
  3. Do the people I watch want the same thing? Check: in Smith and Sørensen’s model with at least two rational types, types with opposed preferences that others cannot see can make actions split forever while carrying no information about the state; with unbounded private beliefs this needs two actions, sufficiently different preferences and not too unequal shares (Section 14.4).
  4. Does the price move with the trades? Check: in Avery and Zemsky’s model, with noise traders, limited frictions and private information always present, information cascades never occur and traders with monotonic signals never herd; herding with signals made non-monotonic holds back information about the new value until prices adjust, and with composition uncertainty as well it can move prices away from the value for a while (working-paper version; Section 14.9).
  5. What does a run of buy orders say? Check: in Hasbrouck’s estimates for Ames Department Stores in the first quarter of 1989, purchases tended to follow purchases, which he found more consistent with lagged adjustment to new information than with inventory control; split orders, price smoothing and buying after others can give runs too (our judgment; the opening and Section 14.9).

14.14 Summary

  • In Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer and Welch’s binary example, with Bayes’ rule and only earlier actions seen, the chance of no cascade after 10 people is below 0.1 percent and that of a wrong cascade remarkably high; once a cascade starts, actions carry no information about signals, and the public history stays worth one to 4/3 private signals (1.33 and 1.31 at precisions 0.6 and 0.75; our calculation), however many follow (Sections 14.1 and 14.2).
  • In the sequential models of Sections 14.1 to 14.3, a wrong herd needs actions that do not reveal the signals behind them and bounded private beliefs, and a cascade in finite time also needs a single action able to carry the public belief into a cascade set, as discrete signals allow (our judgment); in Smith and Sørensen’s example with bounded beliefs a herd forms though a public belief that did not start in a cascade set never enters one (Section 14.3).
  • In Smith and Sørensen’s model with at least two rational types whom others cannot tell apart, types with opposed preferences can make actions split forever while carrying no information about the state, with unbounded private beliefs too if there are two actions, preferences differ enough and the types’ shares are not too unequal; this is nongeneric with discrete beliefs, or with more than two actions and unbounded beliefs (Section 14.4).
  • Information can stop with uniform actions, silence or offsetting split actions when people learn from actions, and with lock-in when they learn from outcomes that only use produces, as after the royal family’s bad results in Bala and Goyal’s example (working-paper version); so agreement alone does not show whether information still flows (our judgment; Sections 14.5 and 14.7).
  • Four results tie learning to unbounded signals for different reasons; unbounded signals are not necessary in general, as exceptions in Acemoglu et al. and in Bala and Goyal show (working-paper version), not sufficient when observations do not expand or, with two actions, when preferences are opposed enough, and speak only of the limit (our judgment; Section 14.8).
  • In Avery and Zemsky’s model, with noise traders, limited frictions and private information always present, prices that move with trades rule out information cascades and monotonic signals rule out herding; herding needs non-monotonic signals, as event uncertainty can make them, then holds back information about the new value until prices adjust and, with composition uncertainty as well, can move prices away from the true value for a while (working-paper version; Section 14.9).
  • When people choose when to move, each would want to move last to learn from others’ decisions; in Zhang’s model a sudden cascade follows a delay, while in Gul and Lundholm’s continuous-time model endogenous sequencing gives efficient clustering (as reported by Brunnermeier; Section 14.11).
