Chapter 18 Price-Implied Probabilities and Risk Premium

On Intrade, an exchange whose contracts paid according to whether an event happened, Page and Clemen (2013) studied markets that opened between August 2002 and February 2007. They found such markets reasonably well calibrated, prices matching how often events occurred, when time to expiration was relatively short, while prices were significantly biased for events farther in the future.

Page and Clemen also tested whether the bias could be traded. They split the markets by starting date into two halves and drew two simple strategies from the first half’s calibration curve; on the second half, strategy A earned an average return of 5.25% and strategy B 9.55%. A discount rate of 15% cancels all of A’s gains, and one of 25% all of B’s; this chapter calls these break-even discount rates. The authors cannot say whether traders have high discount rates, so that no systematic opportunity exists, or whether discounting cancels only part of the gains. They call 15–25% somewhat high, the more so since their simple strategies understate the profits available, and add that empirical studies find individual discount rates that can be quite high. The scope box in Section 18.3 covers these figures.

The same gap between price and frequency can thus be the cost of money tied up in a contract, a reward for bearing risk, or a price that is wrong (our judgment). Section 1.7 left to this chapter what kind of probability a price is: whose probability it reports, and which part of it is a risk premium. Sections 18.1 to 18.5 drop log utility, and Sections 18.6 to 18.10 drop the fact unrelated to aggregate risk, two defaults of the root model; Sections 18.2 and 18.3 also drop frictionless trading, through barred short sales, funding costs, fees and spreads, Section 18.4 the exogenous counterparty, whose wealth becomes endogenous, and Section 18.6 Bayesian updating, together with the fact unrelated to aggregate risk (Table 5.1 in Section 5.8).1

18.1 Whose belief the price reports

Sections 1.3 and 5.3 gave Wolfers and Zitzewitz’s (2006) result: for price takers whose wealth the event touches only through the contract, so that no one trades to hedge, and who have log utility, the price is the wealth-weighted average belief, because log utility makes demand linear in beliefs (working-paper version).

Manski (2006), whose model Section 5.7 described, takes risk-neutral price takers, who care only about expected gains, with differing beliefs, a continuous distribution of beliefs and budgets statistically independent of beliefs. Under these assumptions, he writes, the unique equilibrium price solves his equation (1), which in this book’s letters, qq for the price and pp for a trader’s belief, reads q=P(p>q)q = P(p > q) (working-paper version). The price equals the share of traders who think the event more likely than the price says, so it sits at the 100(1−q)100(1 - q)th percentile of beliefs, as Wolfers and Zitzewitz put it (working-paper version).

The price then pins the average belief only to an interval, from q2q^2 to 2q−q22q - q^2 (working-paper version). If budgets are correlated with beliefs, his condition holds for the population of dollars traded rather than of traders, and his interval then bounds the budget-weighted mean belief (working-paper version).

Constant absolute risk aversion (CARA) describes a trader whose aversion to a given risk does not change with wealth. With CARA utility, Wolfers and Zitzewitz’s demand for the contract is, in this book’s letters, A−1[ln⁡(p/(1−p))−ln⁡(q/(1−q))]A^{-1}[\ln(p/(1 - p)) - \ln(q/(1 - q))], AA being the coefficient of absolute risk aversion; wealth does not enter, and risk aversion changes only the slope (working-paper version). In this book’s derivation, as long as no trader’s wealth limits his position, with the contract in zero net supply and the same AA for everyone, market clearing puts the price’s log-odds at the average of the traders’ log-odds, however wealth is distributed, and with differing AA at their average weighted by risk tolerance, 1/A1/A; CARA takes wealth out of demand only, so if risk tolerance is correlated with beliefs, the price still moves (our derivation).

With risk-averse traders, Manski writes, price depends on the joint distribution of beliefs, budgets and risk preferences (working-paper version; Section 16.1). In Wolfers and Zitzewitz’s numerical examples, with belief distributions and utilities of their choosing, the gap between price and mean belief is generally very small when prices lie between $0.20 and $0.80, its sign varies with the utility function, and it grows as beliefs become more dispersed (working-paper version). For traders less risk averse than log utility, who trade particularly aggressively when they think the event close to 50–50, two forces, with beliefs distributed symmetrically, suggest prices biased toward one half, which with accurate mean beliefs implies a favorite–longshot bias, longshots winning less often than their prices suggest; for more risk-averse traders they expect prices biased toward zero or one, and a reverse favorite–longshot bias may occur (working-paper version).

Figure 18.1 puts the three cases on one set of beliefs, a Beta(3, 7) distribution, a standard family on 0 to 1, with a mean of 0.30, the price of Section 1.3’s drug contract. With wealth unrelated to beliefs, log utility gives the mean, 0.300; risk neutrality with budgets gives 0.346, closer to one half than the median of 0.286; and CARA with a common risk aversion gives 0.279 (our calculation). When each trader’s wealth is proportional to his odds p/(1−p)p/(1 - p) raised to a power from −1 to 1, tilting wealth toward pessimists below 0 and toward optimists above 0, the log-utility price runs from 0.200 to 0.400 and the risk-neutral price from 0.265 to 0.424, while the CARA price stays at 0.279 (our calculation).

The three preferences read different statistics of the same beliefs, a weighted mean, a quantile and an average of log-odds, so “the market’s probability” has a definite meaning only once one states the utility class and whether wealth is correlated with beliefs (our judgment). The root model’s price rule rests on log utility with equal, fixed wealth (Section 5.3), and this is where the book drops it.2

18.2 A band of prices

Section 3.7 defined price-implied probabilities, the chances prices give to outcomes, from the no-arbitrage law. With frictionless markets, Harrison and Kreps (1979) show that every claim is priced by arbitrage, and the model viable, if and only if there is a unique equivalent martingale measure, the set of pricing probabilities that Section 3.7 described; in the book’s reading a price-implied probability is unique only if traded securities can reproduce every event about the state of the world (our judgment; Section 3.7).

Frictions loosen the link further. If the pricing rule is not linear, the case of market frictions, Jouini and Kallal (1995b) show that there could be an infinity of underlying frictionless pricing rules, and these rules need not agree on the claims that are traded, even if markets are complete. With short sales barred, Jouini and Kallal (1995a) show that a claim that dynamic trading can duplicate is not necessarily priced by arbitrage, and the acceptable prices form an interval, the tightest bounds available without knowledge of traders’ preferences. Both papers allow only simple strategies, give everyone one information structure and, in this book’s reading, take prices as unmoved by trades (our judgment; Section 3.7). A price-implied probability is therefore unique only when the market is complete and frictionless (our judgment).

A single stock gives less (Section 1.3): its price is an expected value under the pricing probabilities, and reading a whole distribution would need a security that bets on each event separately (our judgment). The root model’s binary contract offers a bet on each of its two outcomes and so is complete by construction (Section 5.4); Section 18.4 shows what completeness does to wealth.

For a binary contract, Section 3.7 and Box 3.2 gave the band within which no one trades when every belief is the same, money tied up earns no interest and all traders discount alike, and Kalshi’s best Yes and No offers summing to more than $1. Anyone using qq must say where in the band it is taken, at the midpoint or at the price at which one side traded (our judgment). Sections 1.5 and 5.3 asked for funding costs, fees and the bid–ask spread, which the root model’s frictionless trading leaves out, to come off before a calibration gap is read as a gap between belief and price; Section 18.3 does that.

A price-implied probability can therefore differ from the probability under the correct model for three reasons: a risk premium, beliefs that differ from the correct ones, and frictions such as funding costs, fees and spreads (our judgment). Read as a friction, the opening’s break-even discount rate is compensation for money tied up, but only if traders really discount that steeply, which Page and Clemen cannot tell.3

18.3 Reading the calibration curve

Page and Clemen call a price calibrated if contracts exchanged at it yield on average a result equal to the price, in their letters p=E(y∣p)p = \mathrm{E}(y \mid p), with yy the contract’s final value, 1 or 0; this chapter writes qq for the price. The calibration curve c(q)=E(y∣q)c(q) = \mathrm{E}(y \mid q) gives the average outcome at each price. Let π\pi stand, as in Box 1.1, for the probability under the correct model given what the price-setters have seen. The price belongs to what they have seen, so c(q)c(q) is also the average of π\pi among contracts at price qq. Relative entropy, the cost of betting at the price when π\pi is the truth (Section 1.4), is convex, so the average of the second term of the information gradient, D(π∥q)D(\pi \| q), among contracts at price qq is at least D(c(q)∥q)D(c(q) \| q) (our derivation; Box 18.1).

Three limits belong with this bound (our derivation). It bounds an average over contracts at one price, and a single market’s D(π∥q)D(\pi \| q) can be smaller. A calibration curve estimated from a finite sample makes the right side too large on average, so a measured value is a lower bound only within sampling error. And a calibrated price, c(q)=qc(q) = q, is needed for the second term to vanish and does not show that it has: overpricing and underpricing at the same price can cancel. The bound uses only prices and past outcomes, so it is a reading of public information (our judgment).

In nats, a unit of information measured with natural logarithms, about 1.44 bits, and per resolution of a contract, the bound at the listed prices is 0.0074 at Intrade’s 0.20 and 0.019 at 0.80, from the frequencies of Section 1.3 (our calculation). For Kalshi, Bürgi, Deng and Whelan (2026) regress the outcome less the price on the price, in their letters y−p=α+ψpy - p = \alpha + \psi p, with outcomes and prices in cents, a winning contract paying 100, and find ψ=0.034\psi = 0.034 and α=−1.736\alpha = -1.736 (working-paper version). The units are this book’s reading, since the paper elsewhere gives the outcome as 0 or 1 (our judgment). That line gives about 0.003 nats at 5 and 95 cents and 0.0003 to 0.0004 at 20 and 80 cents, about 20 to 60 times less than Intrade’s at the same prices (our calculation); a straight line gives only an order of magnitude (our judgment).

The gaps include frictions (our judgment). Page and Clemen’s own explanation is one: traders who earn no interest on money committed to a contract stay out when their beliefs are near the price. A Kalshi trade takes an offer that a maker posted (Section 17.10), so calibration measured at traded prices takes in part of the spread (our judgment), and fees make a contract need to win more often than its price to break even (Section 1.3). How much comes off before a gap is read as undigested information depends on the estimates of interest rates and fees (our judgment).

Chapters 15 and 17 left the measure of volume to this chapter. On Polymarket, naive volume, every trade added up, overstates turnover, the value that actually changes hands, 2+κ2 + \kappa times, κ\kappa being the market’s issuance-to-turnover ratio (Section 17.9). Across the 249 election-related markets of Tsang and Yang’s (2026) Table 5, the multiple has a 10th percentile of 2.39, a median of 3.57, a 90th percentile of 8.44 and a maximum of 50.2, and it is largest in thin, young markets. Under log utility the price weighs traders’ wealth (Section 18.1), and new positions come in as net inflow, which Tsang and Yang separate from turnover; naive volume makes a market look more active and deeper than it is (our judgment).

Scope. The figures come from three samples on three platforms: Intrade, for markets that opened between August 2002 and February 2007; Kalshi, from 2021 through April 2025, using only prices from the last 10 days before close; and Polymarket in the 2024 US presidential election, its Trump market and, for the volume multiples above, 249 election-related markets. The Kalshi study is a working paper and the Polymarket study a preprint. The relative entropies are pointwise values of the calibration curves at the listed prices, per resolution, in nats, and Kalshi’s come from a straight line and give only an order of magnitude. They include funding costs and fees, so they are not all mispricing; each bounds from below the average of the second term of the information gradient among contracts at its price, a single market’s term can be smaller, and the bound holds only within sampling error; averaging over prices needs the distribution of prices, and for contracts held TT years the interest forgone, rTrT, comes off first (our derivation). How much of a gap discounting explains depends on how widely traders’ beliefs are spread, which no one observes, and at discount rates of the order of 20% a year discounting cannot explain the gap within 10 days of closing (our derivation). Volume must be counted as turnover, since naive volume overstates turnover 2+κ2 + \kappa times. Section 17.9’s scope box covers price impact on Polymarket.4

18.4 Wealth dynamics and market selection

Section 4.3 left market selection, the weeding out of wrong beliefs by trading, to this chapter, and gave Blume and Easley’s (2006) result that with equal discount factors those whose beliefs are closest to the truth end up with all the resources, so correct beliefs are selected whenever markets are complete, while with incomplete markets these conclusions may not hold (working-paper version). The authors add that the assumption that some trader has correct beliefs at the outset limits the interest of the complete-market result (working-paper version).

In their case of states drawn independently from one distribution each period, Blume and Easley define a survival index that measures a trader’s potential to survive, the log of his discount factor less the relative entropy of the truth with respect to his forecast; with identical discount factors, the traders with the largest index are those whose forecasts are closest in relative entropy to the truth (working-paper version). In this book’s derivation, for two log-utility traders in a complete market who bet their beliefs on securities that each pay in one state, the prices cancel from the change in the logarithm of their wealth ratio, whose expected change each period is the difference of their survival indices; with a common relative risk aversion, the attitude to risk sets the speed of selection and leaves its direction alone (our derivation; Box 18.2).

For two Bayesian learners who both come to learn the truth, Blume and Easley show that the more patient survives whatever the number of parameters each must learn: in the independent case, differences in the number of parameters they must learn act at order log⁡t\log t and differences in discount factors at order tt (working-paper version), tt being the number of periods. That wealth flows to the more accurate is thus a result for complete markets with equal discount factors: with unequal discount factors patience can outweigh accuracy, and with incomplete markets the flow can reverse (our judgment).

Two of the authors’ examples show the reversal. With incomplete markets, a trader who is overly optimistic about the return on some asset in some state can choose to save enough to more than overcome the poor asset allocation his incorrect expectations create; in their example of this savings effect, trader 1’s wealth and consumption converge to 0 even though he has correct beliefs and trader 2 has incorrect ones, a clear disconnect, they write, between utility maximization and survival (working-paper version). Even with identical savings behavior, portfolio effects can let incorrect beliefs survive and even prosper, because relative entropy does not match well with the asset structure (working-paper version). These are two examples the authors constructed, with particular utilities and parameters (our judgment); they believe that market incompleteness is the natural assumption in economic models that take heterogeneity seriously (working-paper version).

The root model’s counterparty, trading for reasons other than information, is given from outside; here the noise traders’ wealth is endogenous. Blume and Easley write that De Long and his coauthors, in two papers of 1990 and 1991, argue that irrationally overconfident noise traders can come to dominate an asset market in which prices are set exogenously (working-paper version). In the 1990 paper, optimistic noise traders who invest a large share of their wealth in risky assets earn, as long as risk taking is rewarded, a higher expected return than sophisticated investors, though with lower expected utility, in a two-period model that precludes treating the effects of their wealth accumulation (working-paper version). De Long et al. judge fitness by expected return and Blume and Easley by the long-run share of wealth, and whether noise traders survive when prices respond to their trades needs a model in which wealth evolves (our judgment).

Blume and Easley write that the time scale of selection is governed by the frequency of transactions, and that in financial markets, where transaction frequencies are high, it is plausible that the long run happens very quickly (working-paper version). This book reads the scale as set by how often information is realized, since cutting the same flow of information into shorter trading periods shrinks each period’s relative entropy in proportion (our judgment). In a complete market, a log-utility trader who puts 0.55 on an event with a true probability of 0.5 loses about 0.005 nats per resolution against a correct trader of equal patience, halving his relative wealth in about 138 resolutions, while discount factors of 0.96 and 0.95 differ by about 0.0105 per period (our calculation).5

18.5 Prices and the distribution of capital

Under log utility the price weights beliefs by wealth, and under risk neutrality it counts dollars of budget rather than traders (Section 18.1), so in those cases it leans toward the beliefs of whoever holds the money (our judgment). Tsang and Yang’s record of Polymarket in October 2024 shows how concentrated new money can be. The large trader they call the whale traded through four accounts and bought $26.6 million of Trump YES exposure in October, 15.1% of the $176.4 million of net inflow that funded the Trump market that month; with every account ranked by October net buying and market makers excluded, the largest account bought $17.0 million net, 9.6% of the month’s net inflow, the top five 31.1% and the top twenty 50.7%.

Capital came in on both sides. Pooling the Biden and Harris markets as the Democratic side, the authors find that the 90-day rolling correlation between its daily YES net inflows and Trump’s stayed above 0.8 from October 6 to the race call, and they read the month’s flows as “consistent with heterogeneous beliefs rather than one-sided manipulation”. This is one market in one month, an address is not a person, and the paper does not test whether anyone traded to sway what others believed (Section 15.3.5). Under log utility, or risk neutrality with budgets, the price reflects how money is distributed, which can differ from how people are (our judgment).

Concentration of holdings is a different quantity. Kuśmierz and Overko (2022) obtain a Zipf coefficient, the exponent at which balance falls with rank, the negative of the slope on logarithmic axes, by linear regression on the 30, 50 and 100 largest addresses, averaged over their sample, which runs from each chain’s first block to January 16, 2022; for Bitcoin’s top 100 it is 0.787. The authors write that their data do not represent the wealth of individual owners, only its distribution among wallets, and that one user can hold several wallets.

In this book’s reading the top 100 addresses describe custodians, such as the cold wallets of exchanges, and large holders, while in Cong, Li and Wang’s adoption model of Chapter 11 the price is set at the other end of the list, by the marginal user deciding whether to hold, who must be measured separately; counting addresses as people errs in opposite directions at the two ends, since a custodian’s address pools many users and one user’s several wallets inflate the count of small holders (our judgment). Chapter 19 takes up the mismatch of information and capital.6

18.6 The risk premium inside the gradient

From here the chapter drops the fact unrelated to aggregate risk, under which D(π∥q)D(\pi \| q), the second term of the information gradient, is pure mispricing (Box 1.1); Section 4.2 left the risk premium, the extra return demanded for bearing the risk of the market as a whole, to this chapter.

Ross (2015) writes that risk-neutral state prices, the prices of claims that pay in one state each, are the product of risk aversion, the pricing kernel, and the natural probability distribution, the natural probabilities being the market’s own forecast (working-paper version). For a representative agent, a single investor whose choices stand for the market’s, the kernel is the discount factor times the ratio of marginal utilities, the value of one more unit of consumption, in the next state and in the current one (working-paper version). With FF the market’s belief and mm the kernel scaled to average one under FF, the price-implied probability is q=mFq = mF, so a risk premium shows up as an mm that varies across states (our derivation; Box 18.3).

Box 1.1 printed the split of the second term, D(π∥q)=D(π∥F)−𝔼π[log⁡m]D(\pi \| q) = D(\pi \| F) - \mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\log m] (our derivation). The first term measures how far the market’s belief lies from the correct probability, the mispricing; the second is the risk-premium term, computed under π\pi. If the market’s belief is correct, F=πF = \pi, the first term is zero and D(π∥q)D(\pi \| q) is all risk premium and never negative; if F≠πF \ne \pi, the second term can be negative (our derivation; Box 18.3).

Milgrom and Stokey’s (1982) Theorem 3 takes strictly risk-averse agents with continuously differentiable utility, an ex ante round of trade on competitive markets for claims on each state that leaves them at a Pareto-optimal allocation, one that no change can improve for someone without hurting someone else, in which every agent holds a positive amount of every good, and the paper’s condition (3) on priors, the concordant beliefs of Section 4.4. Then any ex post equilibrium prices supporting that allocation, even if not fully revealing, change in relative terms independently of endowments, utility functions, prior beliefs and the initial allocation, and when markets are open both before and after the news is released, it is the change in relative prices that reveals information. In this book’s reading, under those premises, changes in price-implied probabilities measure news with less interference from the risk premium than levels, which carry marginal utilities (our judgment).

When Bayesian updating is also dropped, with differing priors or biased rules for processing news, the fact unrelated to aggregate risk no longer suffices for the price-implied probability to equal the probability. One sufficient condition is that the price-setters’ beliefs equal the correct probability and that their expected marginal utility be the same given each event about the state; it is only sufficient, since belief errors and differences in marginal utility can offset each other (our derivation). Chapter 16 took up learning from prices.7

18.7 What a measured excess return contains

A measured excess return, the return actually observed above the riskless rate, can contain parts with different sources and premises. Table 18.1 lists nine (our judgment). The table uses “risk premium” in a broad sense, compensation for bearing risk, sub-items unrelated to aggregate risk included, while the narrow sense, the extra return demanded for bearing the risk of the market as a whole (Section 18.6), is the standard one (our judgment).

The table says only which parts a measured excess return may contain; each part must be counted once and its size estimated separately (our judgment). In this book’s reading the risk premium line enters the gradient’s second term through its risk-premium term, −𝔼π[log⁡m]-\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\log m] of Section 18.6, which pays for bearing risk; no one can learn it away, so it is not an informational edge (our judgment). In De Long et al.’s own model, where everyone knows the fact, the underpricing sits in the resale value instead, outside the gradient about the fact (our judgment; Section 18.8).

Adverse-selection compensation has its own line. In Glosten and Milgrom’s (1985) Proposition 6, the discount rate represents the expected holding-period return plus the return that the uninformed anticipate losing to the informed. In this book’s reading this part belongs to the required return even when the price-implied probability equals the probability, and it differs both from the risk premium and from the information rent that the informed collect (our judgment).

Prices can also change what they price. In Goldstein and Guembel’s (2008) model, a speculator realizes that if she drives the stock price down, even without information, the manager might cancel an investment, reducing firm value and letting her profit from a short position (as reported by Bond, Edmans and Goldstein; working-paper version; Section 16.8). In this book’s reading, once a price feeds back into real decisions, the information rent is no longer given from outside (our judgment); Section 4.7 gave the two kinds of efficiency in Bond, Edmans and Goldstein’s (2012) survey.

Overvaluation enters with a minus sign. Write a price as the value that weights all signals correctly plus a stationary overvaluation Δ\Delta. If the correct value earns zero objective expected excess return, as when the outcome is unrelated to aggregate risk, a holder’s objective expected excess return, in money per unit held and per period, averages −r𝔼[Δ]-r\mathbb{E}[\Delta] at interest rate rr, since the overvalued part has no cash flows behind it; otherwise this part comes on top of the correct value’s risk premium (our derivation; Box 18.3). It is negative exactly when 𝔼[Δ]>0\mathbb{E}[\Delta] > 0, and no source used here gives the distribution of Δ\Delta (our judgment). In this book’s reading it arises where differences between people cannot be learned and short sales are restricted, the mechanisms of Chapter 20 (our judgment). In De Long et al.’s model, if noise traders earn higher expected returns than sophisticated investors, the asset’s average price is guaranteed to be below its fundamental value (working-paper version); this book counts that underpricing once, as compensation for noise trader risk (our judgment).

Frictions enter in two ways, as compensation within the required return, the reading of the opening’s break-even discount rates, or as a bound on how much of the other parts can be captured (our judgment). Bernard and Thomas (1989) found post-earnings-announcement drift bounded at approximately 5%, 4.3% and 3% for small, medium and large firms, consistent with Stoll and Whaley’s evidence that transaction costs vary inversely with firm size (as reported by Bernard and Thomas). Read as a bound, a friction is not added to the other parts, and the part of a spread that pays for adverse selection belongs to its own line (our judgment).

Cong, Li and Wang (2021) set the volatility of platform productivity at 200%, which gives much of its growth under the physical measure, the probabilities of actual outcomes: in their letters, with AA for platform productivity, the drift there is μ̂A=μA+ηρσA\hat\mu^A = \mu^A + \eta\rho\sigma^A, and with ηρ=1\eta\rho = 1 it is 202% (working-paper version), of which the risk adjustment ηρσA\eta\rho\sigma^A makes up 200 points (our calculation). They grant that a correlation ρ\rho of 0.67 seems at odds with studies that find low correlations between the returns of cryptocurrencies and those of traditional assets and macroeconomic factors, and argue that their shock captures the underlying technology or platform quality rather than direct return shocks (working-paper version). Biais et al. (2023) calibrate their model under risk neutrality, likely innocuous, they write, because bitcoin’s capitalization was only a small fraction of aggregate wealth from 2010 to 2018; in their equation (52) the return must compensate investors for crashes, transactions costs and hacks, minus transactional benefits, and the wedge between realized and expected returns could reflect a rare disaster such as a crash, the peso problem (working-paper version).8

In this book’s reading the two papers assign a high return to different parts of Table 18.1, one to a risk premium and the other to crash compensation under risk neutrality, which in a sample of survivors shows up as a selection effect; a risk premium needs returns that move with consumption or market factors, while crash compensation needs average returns that fall as an asset goes longer without an accident (our judgment). Chapter 11 gave Cong, Li and Wang’s adoption mechanism.9

18.8 Excess return without information

An excess return does not show that anyone has information (our judgment). In De Long et al.’s model, noise traders with faulty and stochastic expectations create the possibility of capital gains and losses on rational agents’ holdings of an asset, which carries no fundamental risk and thus becomes risky (working-paper version). Sophisticated investors will not hold it unless compensated for the risk that noise traders become bearish and its price falls, and noise traders can affect prices even though there is no uncertainty about fundamentals (working-paper version). The model’s agents live two periods, and with longer lives the equilibrium comes closer to the fundamental one; with fundamental dividend risk, the authors add, its qualitative properties hold even with a known terminal date (working-paper version).

In this book’s reading the excess return here pays for the risk in the resale price that others’ future opinions create, and has nothing to do with how the outcome moves with aggregate risk; it is one of the risk premium’s sub-items in Table 18.1, and the mispricing behind it sits in the resale value, which lies outside the information gradient (our judgment; Section 1.3).

Index deletions are this book’s example of a pure supply shock. Duffie’s (2010) Figure 1 covers 61 deletions from the S&P 500 index from December 1990 to July 2002, with about 7.5 days on average from announcement to effective date. Chen, Noronha and Singhal (2004) found for a similar data set that deleted stocks lost approximately 8% on the announcement date and a further 6% by the effective date, and that the negative effect disappears completely 60 days after the effective date (as reported by Duffie).

Box 1.1 left the reading of such a shock to this chapter. A deletion carries no news about cash flows, so the first term of the information gradient is zero; once the fact unrelated to aggregate risk is dropped, the price change falls in the risk premium, as compensation for absorbing a supply shock when too little capital is at hand (our judgment). It differs from an information rent in reversing: slow information gives a lasting drift in the direction of the outcome, and slow capital gives a reversal (our judgment). A reversal also follows an overreaction to news, as in the EntreMed case of Section 1.1, so it rules out a lasting information rent without proving a supply shock (our judgment). Section 12.6 gave Duffie’s pre-announced supply.10

18.9 The recovery theorem and its conditions

Ross’s (2015) recovery theorem separates option-implied state prices into the pricing kernel and the natural probabilities, so as to determine the market’s forecast of returns and its risk aversion from state prices alone (working-paper version). Its premises, as Ross states them in his working paper, are these (working-paper version).

  • A representative agent exists, a maintained assumption of his analysis; with complete markets and state-independent utility that is additively separable over time it is unique, and its kernel depends only on the marginal rate of substitution between future and current consumption, the discounted ratio of marginal utilities in the next and the current state.
  • The state follows a time-homogeneous process over finitely many states.
  • The pricing matrix is positive or irreducible, every state reachable from every other; then there exists a unique positive solution for the natural probabilities, the discount rate and the kernel.
  • The riskless rate is allowed to depend on the state; fixed across states, the unique natural density is the risk-neutral one itself, so recovery says something new only with a state-dependent rate.
  • The full matrix of transition prices must be known, although there is no rich forward market for options and the transitions are not observed directly.
  • The state is a full description of everything pertinent to the future evolution of the market, while his illustration identifies states by the price level alone, which he defends for relatively short periods.

The uniqueness is no test of these premises. Ross notes in his proof that existence follows from the assumption that the prices were generated in this way, and that his solution shows existence even if they were not (working-paper version). The Perron–Frobenius theorem gives any irreducible non-negative matrix a unique eigenvector with all entries positive, so a unique solution says nothing about whether the premises hold (our judgment).

What the theorem recovers is the market’s belief. Ross calls it the market’s subjective assessment of the distribution of returns (working-paper version), so it is FF in the notation of Section 18.6; π\pi stays unidentified, and the mispricing term D(π∥F)D(\pi \| F) still needs an independent estimate of π\pi (our derivation; Box 18.3). Ross himself calls for a careful look at the boundaries of recovery, particularly the robustness of the assumption that a state-independent kernel exists (working-paper version).

Differing beliefs and awareness strain the premises. In a complete market whose log-utility traders share a discount factor and differ in beliefs, state prices are those of a representative agent who holds the wealth-weighted average belief (our derivation). Wealth shares move with outcomes (Section 18.4), so that agent’s belief drifts with the state, and the kernel may no longer take the separable form of one state-independent utility (our judgment). The root model’s state includes the informed fraction, the share of people who have heard the news, and a state defined by the index level alone projects it away (our judgment).

Ross’s illustration uses S&P 500 option prices from one day in April 2011 (working-paper version). A historical bootstrap puts the probability of a six-month drop of more than 32% at .0008, 4 in 5000 bootstraps, and the recovered density puts it at 1.2% (working-paper version). Ross writes that this is only a crude first pass, “intended to be indicative rather than conclusive” (working-paper version). In this book’s reading the gap could be a risk premium left in because a premise fails, an error in the estimated state prices, of which Ross sees signs (working-paper version), a mispricing, or a disaster that did not occur in the historical sample, a selection effect, and the recovery theorem cannot tell these apart (our judgment). This book computes nothing of its own from the illustration.11

18.10 Selection, risk premium and tests of risk

Blume and Easley write that in dynamically complete markets in which all traders have a common discount factor, if one trader has correct beliefs, then in the long run all assets will be priced using this trader’s preferences and his correct beliefs (working-paper version). In this book’s reading, under those three premises, dynamically complete markets, a common discount factor and one trader with correct beliefs, selection removes the mispricing and leaves the risk premium: the belief error in the price tends to zero while the price keeps the survivor’s attitude to risk, so D(π∥q)D(\pi \| q) tends to the risk-premium term, in general above zero; without a correct trader the belief error shrinks only to that of the most accurate survivor (our judgment).

Bernard and Thomas, whose sample and drift Chapter 12 described, tested risk explanations of post-earnings-announcement drift one by one. For beta, a stock’s sensitivity to the market, to explain the drift, the difference in beta between the firms with the most and the least favorable earnings surprises would have to be 2.6, against an actual 0.21. They found no evidence that the strategy’s returns are significantly correlated with any of five macroeconomic risk factors. They could reconcile the evidence with a failure to control fully for risk only if at least one of three conditions held: that the rarity of losses in 1974–86 was extremely unusual relative to a longer period, that the premium compensated for infrequent but catastrophic losses none of which occurred in the 13 years, or that the losses fell in periods when a $1 decline in wealth was 28 times more important than the average $1 increase. They find the first and third conditions implausible and note that there is no evidence to support the second.

In this book’s reading the second condition is the peso problem, which a 13-year sample cannot rule out; trading costs enter as the bound on the drift of Section 18.7; and what remains of the information part belongs to the processing of public information, a processing rent, with no place here for a private information rent (our judgment). This fits the authors’ reading, given in Chapter 12, that their results are consistent with a market that fails to recognize the full implications of current earnings for future earnings.12

18.12 A trader’s checklist

None of these questions says what to buy or sell, and good answers do not make a trade safe (our judgment).

  1. Whose money and what preferences set this price? Check: for price takers with no reason to hedge, the price is the wealth-weighted average belief under log utility and a quantile of beliefs under risk neutrality with budgets independent of beliefs, and under CARA wealth drops out of demand (working-paper version); for the beliefs of Figure 18.1, with wealth unrelated to beliefs, the three run from 0.279 to 0.346 (our calculation; Section 18.1).
  2. Where in the band is the price taken? Check: with funding costs, fees and spreads a price-implied probability can be pinned only to an interval, even in a complete market, as in Jouini and Kallal’s models with simple strategies, one information structure and prices unmoved by trades, so say whether qq is a midpoint or a traded price (our judgment; Section 18.2).
  3. Has the calibration gap been cleaned of frictions? Check: a calibration curve bounds the average of the information gradient’s second term at each price only from below and within sampling error, a single market’s term can be smaller, its gap includes funding costs and fees, and a calibrated price is needed for that term to vanish without showing that it has (our derivation; Section 18.3).
  4. Could this excess return be a risk premium, a selection effect or a friction? Check: go through Table 18.1 part by part, counting each once; in a short sample the peso problem cannot be ruled out (our judgment; Sections 18.7 and 18.10).
  5. Does the price change reverse? Check: in this book’s reading a pure supply shock moves the price through the risk premium and reverses, while slow information drifts toward the outcome; an overreaction to news reverses too (our judgment; Section 18.8).

18.13 Summary

  • For price takers with no hedging motive, the price is the wealth-weighted average belief under log utility, a quantile of beliefs under risk neutrality with budgets independent of beliefs, and under CARA wealth drops out of demand (working-paper version); for one set of beliefs the three prices differ (our calculation), and under log utility or with budgets the price follows the distribution of money, which can differ from that of people (our judgment; Sections 18.1 and 18.5).
  • A price-implied probability is unique only in a complete, frictionless market; in Jouini and Kallal’s models, with simple strategies, one information structure and prices unmoved by trades, it need not be unique with trading costs, even when the market is complete, and with short sales barred it is pinned only to an interval (our judgment; Section 18.2).
  • Within sampling error, a calibration curve bounds from below the average of the second term of the information gradient among contracts at each price, a single market’s term can be smaller, the bound includes funding costs and fees, and a calibrated price is needed for that term to vanish without showing that it has (our derivation; Section 18.3).
  • In Blume and Easley’s model, wealth flows to those whose beliefs are closest to the truth when markets are complete and discount factors equal, and in their incomplete-market examples it flows to wrong beliefs (working-paper version); where markets are dynamically complete, discount factors common and one trader’s beliefs correct, selection removes the mispricing and the risk premium stays in the price (our judgment; Sections 18.4 and 18.10).
  • Once the fact unrelated to aggregate risk is dropped, the second term splits as D(π∥q)=D(π∥F)−𝔼π[log⁡m]D(\pi \| q) = D(\pi \| F) - \mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\log m], mispricing plus a risk-premium term that is never negative when the market’s belief is correct (our derivation; Section 18.6).
  • In this book’s reading a measured excess return can contain nine parts, each counted once, adverse-selection compensation among them on its own line and overvaluation as a negative part when its average is positive; an excess return alone does not show information, a pure supply shock’s price change reverses, and testing risk explanations one by one, as Bernard and Thomas did, leaves the peso problem open in a short sample (our judgment; Sections 18.7, 18.8 and 18.10).
  • Ross’s recovery theorem separates the pricing kernel from the market’s belief under premises that include a representative agent with time-separable, state-independent utility, a time-homogeneous process over finitely many states and, for an answer other than risk neutrality, a state-dependent riskless rate; what it recovers is the market’s belief, and his one-day estimate is an illustration (working-paper version; Section 18.9).

Box 18.1 The calibration bound in symbols

The rest is our derivation. In a complete market, a log-utility trader ii consumes the share 1−βi1 - \beta_i of wealth each period and spends the rest on securities that pay in one state each, in proportion to his belief pip_i. With riskless gross return RfR_f and state prices scaled to probabilities qq, his wealth after state ss is Wi′=βiWiRfpi(s)/q(s)W_i' = \beta_i W_i R_f\, p_i(s)/q(s). Let qq be the price-implied probability, FF the market’s belief and m=q/Fm = q/F, so that 𝔼F[m]=1\mathbb{E}_F[m] = 1. Overvaluation. If a price is the correct value VV plus a stationary Δ\Delta, which pays no cash flow and has no expected change, and VV earns zero objective expected excess return, a holder’s expected excess return per unit and per period is −r𝔼[Δ]-r\mathbb{E}[\Delta] at interest rate rr.
