Chapter 16 Learning from Prices

Bond, Edmans and Goldstein (2012), surveying how financial markets affect what firms do, give the case of a manager who reads his own stock price: when a manager announces an acquisition and the market responds negatively, he may learn from this response and cancel the deal, and the authors credit Luo (2005) with presenting evidence on this channel (working-paper version). In the cases where learning is most likely, when the deal is irreversible and the market most plausibly has information that the manager does not, Luo finds the probability of cancellation much higher after a low announcement return, while in an earlier paper Jennings and Mazzeo (1991) do not find evidence consistent with this hypothesis (as reported by Bond, Edmans and Goldstein; working-paper version). The survey reports no figures from either study, and this book has read neither.

The survey argues that the information in prices can help a manager who is no less informed than the traders, provided only that he does not have perfect information about every factor relevant to the decision, so that outsiders may hold some incremental information useful to him (working-paper version).1

This chapter opens the canopy of Section 1.7, on how information enters prices. The root model assumes no learning from prices: no one reads the price for what others know. Table 5.1 in Section 5.8 lists this default with the others, and this chapter drops it. It also drops three that Table 5.1 lists for it: the exogenous counterparty, by removing the noise traders (Section 16.5); unlimited attention, together with no learning from prices, when information costs money (Section 16.4); and the single exogenous fact, when decision makers who read the price change the cash flows (Section 16.8). Manski’s traders, who average differing beliefs toward the price (Section 16.1), drop Bayesian updating together with no learning from prices (Table 5.2).

16.1 Three ways to learn from a price

Hayek (1945) argued that where the knowledge of the relevant facts is dispersed among many people, prices can coordinate their separate actions. In his example tin has become scarcer, and its users need not know whether a new use or a lost source of supply did it: all they need to know is that some of the tin they used to consume is now more profitably employed elsewhere, and that in consequence they must economize tin. He saw the price system as a mechanism for communicating information, one that works less perfectly as prices grow more rigid, whose most significant fact is the economy of knowledge with which it operates, how little each participant needs to know to take the right action. The paper argues in words, with no model or data.

Traders who read a price for what others know can do it in at least three ways, which this book sets side by side (our judgment; Table 16.1).

  • Rational expectations. A trader infers from the price what others know, which needs the structure of the model; this is an inference-based higher-order belief in the terms of Section 21.1.3, which Table 5.1 counts in this chapter under no learning from prices (our judgment). Manski (2006) lists what demonstrations that markets can be efficient, such as Theorem 1 of Grossman (1976), have assumed: traders start from common priors, observe different private signals, use Bayes’ rule to combine data with their beliefs, trade on their posterior beliefs and revise them again after seeing the price at which trades take place, with all of this structure common knowledge (working-paper version). This route needs the noise traders, who trade for reasons unrelated to information, kept in the model (our judgment; Section 5.7), and Sections 16.2 to 16.5 follow it.
  • Forecasting rules on past prices. Hong and Stein’s (1999) newswatchers base their forecasts only on news that they privately observe about future fundamentals and do not condition on current or past prices; their momentum traders condition only on some simple measures of past price changes and do not observe news about fundamentals (working-paper version). Momentum traders thus learn from prices by a rule, with no model of what others know (our judgment), and restricted to rules of one variable they must inevitably cause overreaction at long horizons (working-paper version; Sections 10.7 and 13.4; Chapter 20).
  • Averaging toward the price. In Manski’s prediction market, risk-neutral price takers with differing beliefs, whose budgets are independent of their revised beliefs and who revise on seeing the price but stay on the same side of it, leave the equilibrium price where their prior beliefs put it (working-paper version; Section 5.7). Whether and how traders in prediction markets use price data to revise their expectations is, Manski writes, an open empirical question that his paper cannot address, and with risk aversion the price depends on the joint distribution of beliefs, budgets and risk preferences (working-paper version).

Whether traders who read the price move it further thus depends on how they read it: Manski’s averaging traders leave the price where it was and Hong and Stein’s momentum traders push it past its value at long horizons (working-paper version), while rational-expectations learning can make it reveal what the informed know, fully only under the premises of Sections 16.2 and 16.5 (our judgment).

16.2 Full revelation and its premises

Grossman’s (1976) result is that when traders learn the rational-expectations way and there is no noise, the equilibrium price summarizes all the information in the market, under the eight premises of Section 5.7 and Box 5.2, among them constant absolute risk aversion, a dislike of risk that does not change with wealth, with normal distributions, a known total supply and price taking. Grossman calls this perfect aggregation not robust once noise is added (Section 1.6.2), and Chapter 5 gives three pathologies of the noiseless case, among them a communication process that Hellwig (1980) finds not well defined at zero noise.

Hellwig gives the reason. In his account an auctioneer looks for the clearing price, and with no noise, no matter where the auctioneer begins the auction and what signals traders have received, the market will clear, so there is no mechanism by which the auctioneer can find the price that Grossman’s formula prescribes. He adds that in Grossman’s model the covariance between the noise in each trader’s information and the noise in the price is nonzero, because the number of traders is finite and each exerts a nonnegligible influence on the price, yet the traders are price takers, who do not attempt to manipulate the price or its information content; he calls them “slightly schizophrenic”.

Full revelation can take two forms (our judgment). In Grossman’s formal model every trader has a signal of the same precision, with errors independent across traders (Box 5.2), and the price aggregates them, pooling many partial signals into one summary; in the root model the collapse would be a relay, the price passing the informed traders’ common belief pp to the uninformed (our derivation; Section 5.7). Grossman’s model has one trading period and no order of events in time, so this book does not call its revelation instant (our judgment).3

16.3 Aggregation in a large market

In Hellwig’s model each trader has constant absolute risk aversion, and the value, the supply and the errors in the traders’ signals are jointly normal and independent of one another. To remove the tension of Section 16.2 between reading the price and taking it as given, he looks at the aggregation of information in a competitive sequence of economies in which the number of traders grows without bound, the supply per trader stays the same normal random variable, and traders’ risk aversion and signal variances stay within fixed bounds, with their spread across traders settling to a limit. As the number of traders becomes large, the weight of each one’s signal in the price goes to zero, and individual traders can no longer affect the price, so in the limit price taking and reading the price fit together, as they do not in the finite market (our judgment).

The weight of trader ii’s signal relative to trader jj’s becomes approximately ρjsj2/ρisi2\rho_j s_j^2/\rho_i s_i^2, where ρ\rho is a trader’s risk aversion and s2s^2 the variance of her signal’s error; in Hellwig’s reading, the relative importance of a trader’s information becomes inversely proportional to her risk aversion, so that aggregation in a large market depends on traders’ preferences as well as on the precision of their signals. In this book’s reading the price weights each piece of information by its precision and by its holder’s risk-bearing capacity, the counterpart under rational expectations of the root model’s weighting by wealth (our judgment; Chapter 23 takes up risk-bearing capacity).

When the element common to all signals carries a noise δ\delta of its own, each signal being the value plus δ\delta plus the trader’s own error, the equilibrium price depends on δ\delta as well as on the value, while the individual errors still cancel out. Hellwig sums up that his results suggest communication through the market relies on a large number of independent sources of information; because preferences differ, the equilibrium price is not in general an “efficient” aggregator, but this inefficiency is irrelevant if the market draws on many independent sources, so that individual errors cancel out. In this book’s reading a price can thus hold more information about the fact than any one rumor when those who set it have news of their own, as one such price-setter already does in Section 9.6, while an error that every signal shares from its source, Hellwig’s δ\delta or the rumor in every signal of Andrei and Cujean’s (2017) model of Section 9.5, stays in the price (our judgment; Chapter 19).4

16.4 Costly information and the paradox

Section 4.5 gave Grossman and Stiglitz’s (1980) cost condition: in their example with constant absolute risk aversion and normal variables, price-taking traders without supply noise have no equilibrium if, and only if, information is cheap enough (their Theorem 5). This section adds what the example says about the price.

In the example the risky return is u=θ+εu = \theta + \varepsilon, with θ\theta observable at a cost and ε\varepsilon unobservable, and the only noise is the supply of the risky asset per person, xx. With θ\theta, ε\varepsilon and xx jointly normal, nondegenerate, so that every variance is positive and the supply carries noise, and mutually independent, and with a fraction λ\lambda of traders informed, their Theorem 1 gives an equilibrium price that is a linear function of a variable wλw_\lambda, just θ\theta plus noise, the noise being inversely proportional to the proportion of informed traders and proportional to the variance of ε\varepsilon (Box 16.1). The uninformed do not see xx, and they cannot distinguish variations in the price due to the informed traders’ information from variations due to changes in aggregate supply. The authors measure the informativeness of the price system by ρθ2\rho_\theta^2, the squared correlation between the price and θ\theta, which is also the share of the price’s variance that comes from θ\theta, since the noise in wλw_\lambda is independent of θ\theta (our derivation; Box 16.1). Their nn, the variance of θ\theta over that of ε\varepsilon, measures the quality of the informed traders’ information, since n/(1+n)n/(1 + n) is the squared correlation between θ\theta and the return.

Their comparative statics, how the equilibrium moves when one parameter changes, show what more noise does: at any given λ\lambda, an increase in noise reduces the informativeness of the price, but it raises the returns to information and leads more traders to become informed, and the two effects exactly offset each other, so that the equilibrium informativeness is unchanged. This holds at an interior equilibrium, which needs supply noise: their equation (19) holds for γ(0)<1<γ(1)\gamma(0) < 1 < \gamma(1), conditions that ensure that the equilibrium λ\lambda lies between zero and one, and then the equilibrium informativeness is determined completely by the cost of information cc, its quality nn and the degree of risk aversion aa, with γ\gamma the ratio of the informed traders’ expected utility to the uninformed’s (Box 16.1). Outside these conditions their definition of an overall equilibrium has no one informed or everyone informed, and with supply noise present their Theorem 3 gives a unique overall equilibrium among prices that are monotone functions of wλw_\lambda.

As the noise vanishes, the informed vanish with it: from their equations (16a) and (19a), as the supply noise goes to zero, the percentage of informed traders goes to zero, and the informativeness of the price is unchanged.

Figure 16.1 draws the example for two signal qualities, n=1n = 1 and n=4n = 4, with a=2a = 2 and c=0.1c = 0.1, values this book chose since the paper gives no numerical example (our calculation).

With the informed fraction fixed, dashed, the price becomes fully revealing as the noise vanishes, the noiseless limit of Section 16.2 in its relay form, since all the informed see the same θ\theta. With the fraction set by the cost, solid, the informativeness stays where the cost, the quality and risk aversion put it, at 0.508 and 0.877 until everyone is informed (our calculation), and the informed vanish with the noise until, at zero noise, no equilibrium is left (our judgment). At an interior equilibrium, then, what the informed know beyond the price does not shrink as the noise falls: seeing θ\theta adds exactly aa times cc nats about the return to what the price shows (our derivation; Box 4.1), a nat being a unit of information measured with natural logarithms, about 1.44 bits. That is 0.2 nats at the figure’s values for both qualities, while the price itself carries 0.355 and 1.048 nats about θ\theta on the flat parts (our calculation). This is one form, within a model, of the gradient that does not vanish in Table 1.1, in nats that put a price and a signal on the one scale Section 3.5 asked for (our judgment).

In Verrecchia’s (1982) contrast (Section 4.5), traders choose their precision at a convex cost and the price becomes more informative as the noise VV, the variance of the supply per person, falls, yet as VV approaches zero only the most risk-tolerant traders keep acquiring information, until at some point the private incentives are eliminated and no competitive equilibrium exists, so the price’s informativeness stays bounded even as noise goes to zero.5

16.5 Removing the noise traders

Three results say what removing the noise does when traders learn the rational-expectations way. With signals given free, under the premises of Box 5.2, Grossman’s price summarizes all the market’s information when there is no noise (Section 16.2); Hellwig shows that this describes the limit as supply noise vanishes, and that at zero noise the communication process is not well defined (Sections 5.7 and 16.2); and in Grossman and Stiglitz’s example, where information costs money and is cheap enough, price-taking traders have no equilibrium without noise, while as the noise shrinks the informativeness stays where the cost puts it (Section 16.4). In these models, then, full revelation from removing the noise is a limit, reached under these premises with information given free (our judgment).

Hong and Stein make the same point about slow news. Even granting that private information diffuses gradually among the newswatchers, the gradual adjustment of prices in their model hinges critically on the further assumption that newswatchers do not condition on prices; if they did, and as long as the aggregate supply QQ were common knowledge, the logic of Grossman (1976) would imply a fully revealing equilibrium, and gradual diffusion of information would not by itself generate gradual adjustment of prices, with no serial correlation of returns at any horizon as long as QQ is nonstochastic (working-paper version).

Prices that reveal a lot leave little trade. Grossman and Stiglitz note that no trade takes place as the informed fraction goes to 1, and read the incompatibility of competitive equilibrium with informationally efficient markets as meaning that speculative markets where prices reveal a lot of information will be very thin, being made up of traders with very similar beliefs. Section 4.4 gave the no-trade theorem, whose premises include cash flows fixed from outside, and Bond and Eraslan’s (2010) purely informational trade when decision makers use the terms of trade for real decisions that change the cash flows (as reported by Bond, Edmans and Goldstein; working-paper version; Section 16.8).6

16.6 Speed and limits of learning from prices

Section 14.8 gave Milgrom’s Theorem 4.5: in his auction of kk objects with kk fixed, the price comes as close as one likes to the most that any bidder would pay under perfect information, with probability approaching one as the number of bidders grows, if and only if his condition (4.9), the infimum condition printed in Section 14.8, holds, which this book reads as saying that some signal can be arbitrarily strong evidence for the higher value (our judgment; Table 14.2).

In his auction the kk identical objects are offered to nn bidders, with n>kn > k; sealed bids are tendered, and the kk highest bidders each receive one object for a price equal to the k+1k + 1st highest bid. For a single bidder, the kkth highest bid among the others can be thought of as the price he faces, and with this interpretation each bidder is a price taker who cannot individually affect his price, which in this book’s reading holds exactly for any number of bidders, with no need for Hellwig’s large market (our judgment). The price the auction forms is not, in general, fully revealing, and Milgrom lists two shortcomings relative to rational-expectations models: the market is one-sided, with the sellers playing a purely passive role, and each buyer can acquire only one of the objects.

Milgrom’s price is an order statistic, a value picked by its rank among the bids, that with kk fixed depends on the most extreme few signals (our judgment; Section 14.8). In the models of Grossman, Hellwig, and Grossman and Stiglitz, signals are normal and unbounded in strength and enter the price linearly; in Grossman’s and Hellwig’s models what limits revelation is the noise, and in Grossman and Stiglitz’s example, at an interior equilibrium, the cost of information sets the price’s informativeness, the noise deciding only how many buy information (our judgment; Section 16.4).

Brunnermeier (2001) reports how fast prices learn in Vives’s (1993) dynamic setting, where revelation through current and past prices can become a victim of its own success: as the price process becomes more informative, less information is incorporated in the current price (as reported by Brunnermeier). With information dispersed among traders, the speed of convergence to the fully revealing outcome is only 1/t1/31/\sqrt{t^{1/3}}, much slower than the standard rate of 1/t1/\sqrt{t} in rational-expectations learning, a rate that also emerges when a mass of traders know the true value (as reported by Brunnermeier). At the slower rate, halving the price’s error takes 64 times as many trading periods, against 4 times at the standard rate (our calculation). Brunnermeier adds that a slower speed of learning is not necessarily welfare reducing.

One further limit is possible: once the informed know, newly informed traders may stop entering, and the price may stop short of reflecting what they know. Box 16.2 derives this in the root model for traders who must pay a fixed cost to enter from outside, while traders already in the market, when switching is worth the cost to them, keep switching as the price nears the truth (our derivation); this book has not checked either reading against any model with trading costs and does not present them as results, and Chapter 21 takes up a model in which trading costs hold information back from prices.

Whether Smith and Sørensen’s (2000) confounding outcome, order flow left uninformative by traders with opposite motives whom others cannot tell apart (Section 14.4), survives a price that moves with trades is a question this book has not checked and leaves open (our judgment); Chapter 17 takes up market makers who price the order flow.7

16.7 Time scales and the unknown state of spreading

A trader who receives a piece of information and wants to exploit it, Brunnermeier writes, has to figure out, before he determines his optimal trade, whether the information is already reflected in the current price. In Treynor and Ferguson’s (1985) setting, one trader’s trades alone do not move the market price, which jumps only when all other traders hear the news and act on it, and they assume that once an event occurs, it becomes public very fast; in their numerical example the trader infers from the past price process that, with a probability of 70 percent, all the other traders have not yet received the same information, which shows, Brunnermeier writes, that technical analysis, inferring information from past prices, helps in the evaluation of new private information (as reported by Brunnermeier). The 70 percent comes from one numerical example in one model, and this book has not read the paper.

In the root model’s terms, a newly informed trader knows pp and does not know I(t)I(t), the informed fraction, so she is uncertain how far the news has spread, one of the unknowns that Section 5.7 counted for the uninformed (our judgment). Hong and Stein’s momentum traders face the same problem: a price increase sometimes comes only from previous rounds of momentum trade, and because they cannot condition directly on whether news has recently arrived, they do not know whether they are early or late in the cycle (working-paper version).

Vives’s rate sets no time scale without the length of a trading period (our judgment; Section 16.6). In Andrei and Cujean’s model, traders know one period ahead the cross-sectional average of their precision, because it is just a function of time, although no trader knows her own future precision, so uncertainty about how far information has spread on average is switched off there (our judgment; Chapter 19).

Section 5.7 derived that with three unknowns and one price, revelation in the root model may be neither instant nor monotone (our derivation), and named Chapters 16 and 19 for the check; this chapter gives related results (as reported by Brunnermeier) and leaves the check to Chapter 19, where none of the models can make it and the question stays open (our judgment; Section 19.2.6).8

16.8 Real decision makers learning from prices

Bond, Edmans and Goldstein describe two ways in which a price feeds back into what a firm does, which this book calls information feedback and incentive feedback (our judgment). In information feedback, decision makers learn from the price and change their decisions, the cash flows change with them, and prices both affect and reflect cash flows (working-paper version). In incentive feedback, decision makers care about prices even without learning from them, because they are party to contracts that depend on prices, most of all managers whose pay is tied to the share price; if the stock price is driven by noise and only loosely tied to firm value, the manager has weak incentives to exert costly effort to improve the firm’s fundamental value, and the level of investment is “increasing in the informativeness, rather than level, of stock prices” (working-paper version). A price that changes only what traders know falls outside both kinds, so the root model’s single exogenous fact, a true value that trading does not touch, fails here only through the feedback (our judgment; Chapter 22).

Section 4.7 gave the survey’s two notions of efficiency: forecasting price efficiency, a price’s power to forecast firm value, and revelatory price efficiency, the extent to which prices reveal the information needed for efficient real decisions. Revelatory price inefficiency immediately generates real inefficiency, while forecasting price inefficiency affects real inefficiency only to the extent that it is related to revelatory price inefficiency, and revelatory efficiency is necessary for real efficiency but not sufficient (working-paper version).

The survey counts at least three ways in which revelatory efficiency can fail when decision makers do not observe the state variables (working-paper version): commonly if expected firm value under the efficient decision is non-monotonic in the state variable (working-paper version), so that one price can stand for several states (our judgment); as in Dow and Gorton (1997), because a firm’s response to the information in prices may destroy speculators’ incentives to collect information in the first place (as reported by Bond, Edmans and Goldstein; working-paper version); and, even with information at no cost, if prices do not efficiently aggregate speculators’ diverse pieces of information (working-paper version). Decision makers who use the information in a price might also harm the price’s informativeness about the variable they wish to learn (working-paper version).

The information gradient, a forecasting quantity measuring private value, can move apart from revelatory price efficiency (our judgment; Section 4.7): in a market where no one collects information, the price can be efficient in the forecasting sense, with no private signals and a zero information gradient, while it reveals nothing that a decision maker lacks (our judgment).

In Goldstein and Guembel’s (2008) model, which the survey reports, the manager learns from the stock price about the profitability of an investment project, and because of this feedback, manipulation arises as an equilibrium phenomenon: a speculator who drives the stock price down, even with no information, may lead the manager to cancel the investment, since he thinks the fall may have been due to negative information, so that the cancellation reduces firm value and lets the speculator profit from her short position, while buying without information causes overinvestment, also bad for firm value, so the speculator loses on her long position (as reported by Bond, Edmans and Goldstein; working-paper version). In Edmans, Goldstein and Jiang’s (2011) model the limit to arbitrage that feedback creates is asymmetric too: it deters informed selling and encourages informed buying (as reported by Bond, Edmans and Goldstein; working-paper version). In this book’s reading these are further possible reasons for bad news to enter prices more slowly than good news (our judgment; Chapter 20).

In Shleifer and Vishny’s (1997) model, which this book reads as one of financiers learning from prices (our judgment), poorly informed outside investors rationally use the past performance of arbitrageurs to gauge their ability and to increase or reduce the funds they give them to manage, a good track record bringing in more funds and a bad one causing a withdrawal of funds (working-paper version). This performance-based arbitrage makes arbitrage ineffective in extreme circumstances, precisely when returns to arbitrage are highest, circumstances, they add, in which many of the arbitrageurs in a market are already fully invested, experience substantial losses and face fund withdrawals and liquidations, and to which the model is most applicable (working-paper version). The performance the investors see is the price’s move, so a move against the arbitrageurs reads to them as a sign of low ability (our judgment; Chapter 23).

Even fully rational learning from the price, Bond, Edmans and Goldstein note, can lead to inefficient decisions, since price changes sometimes arise from non-fundamental shocks, such as price pressure caused by fire sales, sales forced on a holder at depressed prices, which real decision makers may misinterpret as fundamental (working-paper version). Section 16.9 takes up moves in demand unconnected with the fact.9

16.9 Sentiment and information in a price

Hayek’s users of tin need not know why tin became scarcer (Section 16.1), but a trader who reads the price for what others know has to ask why it moved, since a move tied to the fact and a move in demand unconnected with it call for different trades (our judgment).

In Grossman and Stiglitz’s example the uninformed cannot tell the informed traders’ information from changes in aggregate supply (Section 16.4), and 1−ρθ21 - \rho_\theta^2 is the share of the price’s variance that comes from the supply noise (our derivation; Figure 16.1). Hellwig’s traders likewise cannot tell whether a high price comes from a high payoff or a low supply (Section 5.7).

A premise lies behind this: Grossman and Stiglitz assumed that a trader’s endowment is independent of the market’s endowment per person, primarily so that a trader’s own endowment would hold no useful information about the total market endowment, so no trader’s own holdings help forecast the noise before the price forms (our judgment). In Tetlock’s (2007) data, by contrast, a measure that may stand for sentiment or for risk aversion followed the market’s past returns, and its effect on the Dow was fully reversed within a week (working-paper version; Section 13.11), and a trader able to forecast such demand would gain information about future prices and none about the fact, the distinction of Section 2.5 (our judgment).

Whether a move lasts or reverses is the test of Sections 13.11 and 15.4, and a reversal shows only that the move was temporary (our judgment).10

16.10 Three consequences of learning from prices

In Hong and Stein’s model with newswatchers alone, new information works its way gradually into the price over zz periods, a baseline level of underreaction that, the authors write, is essentially built into the model by the assumption that newswatchers do not extract information from prices and that to some extent they have “hardwired” in through their assumptions of gradual diffusion and no conditioning on prices (working-paper version).

Andrei and Cujean keep the noise and let traders learn from prices the rational-expectations way: besides their necessary condition for momentum, information flowing at an increasing rate (Section 5.7), they show that momentum never obtains when the flow of private information is linear, a customary assumption in the literature (Chapter 19).

In these two models the same slowly spreading news moves prices differently under different ways of learning, each statement holding within its own model, so whether slow news produces momentum depends both on how fast the news spreads and on how traders learn from prices (our judgment; Table 16.2); in Manski’s market no news arrives or spreads, and learning from the price leaves it where the prior beliefs put it (working-paper version; Section 16.1).

16.12 A trader’s checklist

None of these questions says what to buy or sell, and good answers do not make a trade safe (our judgment).

  1. How do the people who set this price learn from it? Check: in Manski’s prediction market, risk-neutral price takers with differing beliefs leave the price unchanged when their budgets are independent of their revised beliefs and they stay on the same side of the price as they revise; in Hong and Stein’s model, momentum traders who use past prices through rules of one variable cause overreaction at long horizons (working-paper version; Section 16.1 and Table 16.1).
  2. How much of a move in the price is information? Check: in Grossman and Stiglitz’s example, with constant absolute risk aversion, normal variables and price-taking traders, the price’s informativeness at an interior equilibrium is set by the cost of information, its quality and risk aversion, and it does not change with the supply noise, because more noise draws in more informed traders (Section 16.4 and Figure 16.1).
  3. Could someone hold decisive evidence? Check: in Milgrom’s auction of a fixed number of objects, the price comes close to the most that any bidder would pay under perfect information, with probability approaching one as bidders multiply, if and only if, in this book’s reading of his condition, some signal can be arbitrarily strong evidence for a higher value (our judgment; Section 16.6 and Table 14.2).
  4. Do I know whether the market already knows? Check: in Treynor and Ferguson’s numerical example, in which one trader’s trades do not move the price and news becomes public very fast, the trader infers from past prices a 70 percent chance that the others have not yet received the news; it is one example from one model (as reported by Brunnermeier; Section 16.7).
  5. Does anyone make real decisions from this price? Check: in the models Bond, Edmans and Goldstein survey, prices that guide decisions both affect and reflect cash flows (working-paper version); in Goldstein and Guembel’s model an uninformed speculator can profit by driving the price down, while buying without information loses, and in Edmans, Goldstein and Jiang’s model the feedback deters informed selling and encourages informed buying (as reported by Bond, Edmans and Goldstein; working-paper version; Section 16.8).

16.13 Summary

  • Traders can learn from a price in at least three ways, by inferring others’ information the rational-expectations way, by forecasting rules on past prices or by averaging their beliefs toward the price, and in the models this chapter compares each way gives its own outcome for the price (our judgment; Sections 16.1 and 16.10).
  • With rational-expectations learning, signals given free and no noise, under the premises of Box 5.2, among them constant absolute risk aversion with normal variables, a known total supply and price taking, Grossman’s price, in its linear equilibrium, summarizes all the market’s information, and this is only a limit: at zero noise Hellwig finds the communication process not well defined, and in Grossman and Stiglitz’s example, where information costs money and is cheap enough, price-taking traders have no equilibrium at zero noise (Sections 16.2, 16.4 and 16.5).
  • In Hellwig’s large market, with constant absolute risk aversion, normal variables and errors independent across traders, where the number of traders grows without bound while the supply per trader stays the same normal random variable, no one affects the price, each signal counts in proportion to its precision over its holder’s risk aversion, and individual errors cancel while an error common to all signals stays in the price (Section 16.3).
  • In Grossman and Stiglitz’s example with constant absolute risk aversion, normal variables and price-taking traders, the price’s informativeness at an interior equilibrium is set by the cost of information, its quality and risk aversion, whatever the supply noise, so the informed fraction goes to zero as the noise does (Section 16.4).
  • Learning from prices can stop or slow. In Milgrom’s auction of a fixed number of objects the price converges in probability, as bidders multiply, to the most any bidder would pay under perfect information if and only if, in this book’s reading of his condition, some signal can be arbitrarily strong evidence for a higher value (our judgment); in Vives’s market, with information dispersed, prices converge to full revelation much more slowly than at the standard rate (as reported by Brunnermeier; Section 16.6).
  • In the models Bond, Edmans and Goldstein survey, decision makers who act on prices make prices both affect and reflect cash flows, and the survey separates revelatory price efficiency, whose failure immediately generates real inefficiency, from forecasting price efficiency (working-paper version; Section 16.8).
  • In Grossman and Stiglitz’s model, with each trader’s endowment independent of the market’s, no trader’s own holdings help forecast the noise in the price, while a trader able to forecast sentiment, such as a measure that in Tetlock’s data followed past returns (working-paper version), would learn about future prices without learning more about the fact (our judgment; Section 16.9).

Box 16.2 An entry cost in the root model

This optional box is this book’s own derivation throughout, which this book has not checked against any model with trading costs (our derivation). In the root model traders have log utility and so bet as Kelly would (Sections 1.4 and 5.3); a trader who believes pp and bets at the price qq gains D(p∥q)D(p \| q) in expected log wealth, by her own belief (Section 1.4).

Entering from outside. A newly informed trader with wealth ww who is outside the market must pay a fixed cost kk to enter. Entering gives ln⁡(w−k)+D(p∥q)\ln(w - k) + D(p \| q) in expected log wealth, against ln⁡w\ln w outside, so she enters only if D(p∥q)>−ln⁡(1−k/w).D(p \| q) > -\ln(1 - k/w). As the price moves toward pp, D(p∥q)D(p \| q) falls to zero, so entry stops at a price q*q^* with D(p∥q*)=−ln⁡(1−k/w)D(p \| q^*) = -\ln(1 - k/w), short of pp, and until public disclosure a gap stays between how many know and how much the price reflects.

Switching a position. A newly informed trader who already bets at the price as her old belief q0q_0 would gains, by her new belief, D(p∥q)−D(p∥q0)D(p \| q) - D(p \| q_0) from that position; paying kk to bet on pp instead gives D(p∥q)D(p \| q), so switching adds D(p∥q0)D(p \| q_0) whatever the price, and she switches only if D(p∥q0)>−ln⁡(1−k/w)D(p \| q_0) > -\ln(1 - k/w). Under this reading the switching does not stop as the price nears pp.

What it shows. In this derivation, whether the price stops short of full reflection depends on whether the newly informed were in the market before (Section 16.6).13
