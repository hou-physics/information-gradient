Chapter 6 Forgetting and Attention Decay

Shiller (2017) notes that the susceptible–infected–recovered (SIR) model of disease epidemics, in which the infected pass a disease on for a while and then recover, can also describe the word-of-mouth transmission of an idea (working-paper version). His subject is narratives, popular stories that spread by being retold. In the model, starting from a few spreaders, the number infected and contagious rises at first and then falls (working-paper version). To follow narratives he counts newspaper articles that contain a word or phrase, as a percent of all articles each year, in ProQuest’s news and newspapers from 1900 to 2016 (working-paper version). The phrase “stock market crash” had two fast epidemics in newspapers, each peaking within a year, and the narrative of the 1929 crash persists today, though more in books than in newspapers (working-paper version). Shiller writes that the 1987 epidemic, which looks far stronger than the 1929 one, draws its strength from memories of 1929 and may not in fact have been stronger, given the limitations of word counts as measures of narratives (working-paper version).

In his Figure 6 the newspaper share of the phrase “stock market crash” had faded long before 1987, while the memory of 1929 had not (our judgment). A word count follows the people talking about a story now, or the people newly hearing it, and neither is the number who have heard it (our judgment). Shiller notes that during an epidemic public attention tends to focus on the number of infectives, and the infectives of his model are the people actively spreading (working-paper version).1 This chapter opens trunk one of Section 1.7, on how news spreads. In the root model of Chapter 5, the book’s simplest information market, those who know never stop telling and never forget, an assumption the book calls permanent spreading; this chapter lets spreaders stop. The network results of Section 6.3 also drop homogeneous mixing, and the window of Section 6.6 unlimited attention, each only together with permanent spreading, which is why Table 5.1 lists this chapter for them in parentheses. They can stop in two ways (Sections 6.2 and 6.3), which, when anyone may meet anyone, give opposite results on whether a story needs a minimum strength to take off and on its chance of dying out early (Section 6.4). Apart from Shiller’s word counts and one sample of Twitter data, the chapter rests on models with no data behind them (Section 6.7).

6.1 Stopping and forgetting

Permanent spreading is one of the root model’s default assumptions, listed in Table 5.1 in Section 5.8. Section 1.6.4 noted that any threshold presupposes spreaders who stop and that where it lies depends on how they stop (our judgment). Dropping permanent spreading raises two questions, whether a spreader stops telling or forgets, and what makes a spreader stop.

Bartholomew (1982), as reported by Shiller, argued that when variants of the epidemic model are applied to the spread of ideas, in Shiller’s words, “we should not assume that ceasing to infect others and forgetting are the same thing” (working-paper version). The same passage notes that an old idea people still remember, though they rarely discuss it, might influence behavior (working-paper version). Shiller himself says that the rate at which the SIR model removes spreaders might be described as the rate of forgetting (working-paper version), so both readings appear in his paper.

The book keeps the two apart, because they act differently on the informed fraction, the share of people who have heard, and on the price (our judgment). Stopping spreading closes only the state of telling others. Those who have stopped still know and keep their belief, so the informed fraction stays where it was, and under the price rule of Section 5.3 so does the price. Forgetting shrinks what a person knows at a given moment, her information set; the informed fraction falls, and the price moves back toward the belief of those who never heard. Under the book’s observation law, people learn only by observing and can forget (Section 3.3), and telling others leaves the teller’s knowledge intact (Table 1.1). By these rules, in a closed population, which no one joins or leaves, the informed fraction can fall only through forgetting (our judgment).

Spreaders can stop for two kinds of reason. Under spontaneous stopping, spreaders fall silent after a while of their own accord, whoever they meet; the epidemic models of Section 6.2 work this way, and so do forgetting and attention decay, a message drawing less attention as time passes. Under contact-triggered stopping, a spreader stops on meeting someone who already knows, having learned that the news has lost its news value; the rumor model of Section 6.3 works this way. Castellano, Fortunato and Loreto (2009) describe the same contrast, writing that in an epidemic the infected recover spontaneously at some rate, while people typically stop propagating a rumor when they realize that those they want to inform already know.

Daley and Kendall (1964) set the two side by side, with the same three classes of people, and found that stopping on contact makes the system behave strikingly differently from an epidemic, in ways that familiarity with the epidemic could not have predicted. Their stated aim was to stress “the danger of transferring formulae designed for epidemiological use without a thorough re-examination of the hypotheses”.2

6.2 Spreaders who stop on their own

Shiller writes the three-equation SIR model as dSdt=−cSI,dIdt=cSI−rI,dRdt=rI\frac{dS}{dt} = -cSI, \qquad \frac{dI}{dt} = cSI - rI, \qquad \frac{dR}{dt} = rI (working-paper version). Here SS is the number of susceptibles, who have not caught the idea; II the number of infectives, who are actively spreading it; and RR the number of recovereds, who have stopped and cannot catch it again. The total N=S+I+RN = S + I + R is constant. In a thoroughly mixing population, people newly catch the idea at a constant contagion rate cc times SS times II, and spreaders stop at a constant recovery rate rr times II (working-paper version). Shiller assumes, for simplicity, that recovery comes as an exponential decay (working-paper version), so every spreader has the same chance of stopping at each moment, however long it has been spreading (our derivation). Shiller adds that constant rates might be plausible if they are intrinsic to the narrative (working-paper version). Box 6.1 gives the model in symbols.

For a narrative, the contagion rate is the fraction of encounters in which a spreader convinces the other person enough to spread the story further, and many encounters may be needed before a person is infected (working-paper version). Infection in this sense asks more than having heard, so the number ever infected can be smaller than the number who have heard (our judgment). Where this chapter sets SIR beside the rumor model, in Sections 6.4 and 6.5, it reads catching the idea as hearing it, so that the share who have heard is 1−S/N1 - S/N; without that reading, S/NS/N only bounds the share who never hear from above (our judgment).

The basic reproduction number, R0R_0, is the average number of people that one spreader infects directly in a population where no one has the idea yet; in Shiller’s symbols it is cN/rcN/r (our derivation). Under homogeneous mixing, where anyone may meet anyone at random and two who meet draw on different sources, the model has an epidemic threshold, a value that R0R_0 must exceed for spreading to take off. Only if R0>1R_0 > 1 can a single spreader start an outbreak that reaches a finite share of the population. The deterministic epidemic model as Daley and Kendall describe it, which follows average numbers and leaves chance out, has the same kind of threshold, an initial trace of infection generating an appreciable epidemic if and only if the initial number of susceptibles exceeds a threshold. On networks the threshold moves (Chapter 7).

In the model, the number who have ever been infected approaches a limit strictly below NN, because spreaders become fewer and fewer as they recover (working-paper version). A higher c/rc/r means a larger epidemic, regardless of the level of cc or of rr, while a higher cc at the same c/rc/r makes it faster (working-paper version). With a first spreader too small to count, Shiller’s relation between the numbers not yet infected and recovered fixes the share who never catch the idea by R0R_0 alone, and it is 1 when R0≤1R_0 \le 1 (our derivation; Box 6.1; Section 6.4). Spreaders are most numerous when the share not yet infected has fallen to 1/R01/R_0, so a count that tracks spreaders peaks while that share has still not caught the idea (our derivation). At R0=2.5R_0 = 2.5, the value used in Section 6.5, that share is 40% (our calculation). These relations hold inside the model, under its premises.

In the susceptible–infected–susceptible (SIS) model the passage out of the infected state is spontaneous, in SIR and SIS alike, and those who stop can be infected again, a cycle that under some conditions can be sustained forever; only above the threshold can it settle at a steady share of spreaders, the endemic state, while in SIR the threshold marks an outbreak of a finite share. Shiller describes a variant, the susceptible–infected–recovered–susceptible (SIRS) model, in which those who recovered gradually become susceptible again; there the number of spreaders may, depending on parameters, approach a level above zero, so that spreaders never effectively disappear (working-paper version).

Shiller also suggests that for ideas the contagion rate should decline with time, as the idea becomes gradually less exciting (working-paper version). This is attention decay. It belongs to spontaneous stopping, because the fall does not depend on whom a spreader meets (our judgment). The threshold and the final-state relations above hold for constant rates, the forgetting rate among them. With a contagion rate that falls over time and no removal, the final share is set by the contagion rate added up over time (our derivation), and those relations do not carry over.

Spreading is random for each person, and a cascade, the chain of tellings that starts from one seed, can end early. Under spontaneous stopping and homogeneous mixing, R0>1R_0 > 1 is only a necessary condition for an outbreak in a random model, and even above it chance can end the spread while spreaders are few. With spontaneous stopping at constant rates, the chance of such an early end does not shrink toward zero as the population grows, because early on each spreader stops at its own rate whatever the others know, so the start is a branching process, each spreader giving rise to a random number of new ones, whose chance of dying out does not depend on NN (our derivation). How large that chance is depends on how the number each spreader infects is distributed (Chapter 8).

Kempe, Kleinberg and Tardos’s (2003) independent cascade model runs on a network. When a node first becomes active, it gets a single chance to activate each neighbor not yet active, and succeeds with a probability set for that pair, independently of the history so far; whether or not it succeeds, it makes no further attempts on that neighbor. They focus on the progressive case, in which active nodes never switch back, so a node stops spreading without forgetting (our judgment). Pastor-Satorras et al. (2015) call the model essentially an inhomogeneous SIR, inhomogeneous because the chance of passing the idea on differs from link to link (our judgment). We read it as an SIR model in which each spreader spreads for a single step (our judgment); Box 6.2 sets out this reading and its limits. Kempe, Kleinberg and Tardos’s own question is which starting nodes make a cascade largest (Chapter 15).3

6.3 Spreaders who stop when the news is stale

Daley and Kendall (1965) take a closed, homogeneously mixing population in three classes: XX people who have not heard the rumor, NN at the start; YY spreaders, one at the start; and ZZ people who have heard it but stopped spreading, none at the start. A spreader who meets someone who has not heard tells them. When two spreaders meet, both stop; when a spreader meets someone who has stopped, the spreader stops. The three kinds of meeting happen at rates proportional to XYXY, 12Y(Y−1)\tfrac{1}{2}Y(Y - 1) and YZYZ, with one constant for all three, since it only measures how often a given pair meets. Stopping at the first meeting with someone who knows is, in their words, “an extreme assumption”, chosen because it is the simplest possible and introduces no parameters. In an epidemic, by contrast, people leave the spreading class at a rate proportional to YY alone. So a spreader’s chance of stopping rises with the number who already know in the rumor model and stays the same in an epidemic (our derivation; Box 6.1).

Under these rules there is no threshold. The fraction who ultimately hear the rumor is approximately independent of the population size; in the 1964 note it is always about 0.80, almost irrespective of the initial number who have not heard. Early on almost no one knows, so a spreader meets many people who have not heard before meeting one who has, and the number told before stopping grows with the population (our derivation).

The rumor can still die almost at once. Under homogeneous mixing, the chance that only one person besides the first spreader ever hears is about 0.5/N0.5/N; with N=1000N = 1000 it is about 1/2000 per run.

The share who never hear rests on five premises: a closed population, homogeneous mixing, no outside source of the news, a single first spreader, and a spreader who stops at the first meeting with someone who already knows. Under them, in the deterministic approximation for a large population, the share xx who never hear solves xe2(1−x)=1,x\,e^{2(1 - x)} = 1, whose smaller root is about 0.203, so about 80% hear in the end. In their random model the share who hear averages about 0.80, with a variance of about 0.31/N0.31/N in the main hump of a two-humped distribution, a hump that holds all the probability in the limit of a large population; the small hump is the early end above (Chapter 8). These are mathematical results, and they have not been compared with any real message’s coverage. Spontaneous forgetting, a sparse network or a continuing outside source would each give a different coverage, so the result cannot be read as word of mouth always missing a fifth of the people.4

Daley and Kendall also let a spreader stop only after being involved in an “unsuccessful telling” kk times, which here means after kk meetings with people who already know. In the deterministic version for a large population, for k=1k = 1, 2 and 3 the share who never hear is about 0.203, 0.060 and 0.020. In another version a spreader tells the rumor only with some probability, and stops only with some probability on being told it or on learning that the listener already knows. In the deterministic version for a large population, whatever the two probabilities, the share who never hear then has an upper bound, so at least about 71.5% hear (our calculation; Box 6.1). Like the share above, these values have not been compared with any real message’s coverage.

Two later results, which we know only as reviews report them, show that the absence of a threshold rests on homogeneous mixing and on spreaders who stop only on contact (our judgment). When spreaders can also forget at a rate of their own, a threshold returns and, on networks, behaves as SIR’s does (Chapter 7), because forgetting is linear in the density of spreaders and dominates while spreaders are few (Nekovee et al., as reported by Pastor-Satorras et al. and by Castellano, Fortunato and Loreto, neither of whom names the method). On a small-world network, one in which most links are local and a share of them is rewired at random, a rumor spread by Daley and Kendall’s rule stays near its origin when that share is below a critical value, and the critical value stays finite as the network grows (Zanette, as reported by Castellano, Fortunato and Loreto and by Pastor-Satorras et al.). The results on networks in this section, and Chapter 7, which takes up networks, drop homogeneous mixing together with permanent spreading (Table 5.2).5

Shiller treats a contagion rate that falls over time and Daley and Kendall’s rule as two ways of modeling approximately the same notion (working-paper version). The first does not depend on whom a spreader meets and is spontaneous stopping; the second is triggered by contact (our judgment). The share who never hear and the absence of a threshold hold for contact-triggered stopping only.

6.4 Two ways of stopping compared

Figure 6.1 sets the two final states side by side (our calculation); its left panel reads catching the idea as hearing it (Section 6.2).6

Up to R0=1R_0 = 1 the left panel stays at 1, since no outbreak takes off; that is the threshold. Past it, the larger R0R_0, the fewer never hear, about 0.42 at R0=1.5R_0 = 1.5, 0.11 at 2.5 and 0.007 at 5, model values under the premises of Section 6.2 (our calculation).

Under homogeneous mixing, spontaneous stopping at constant rates puts the threshold at R0=1R_0 = 1, and a cascade from one seed can die out early with a chance that does not shrink as the population grows (our derivation). Under the same mixing, contact-triggered stopping has no threshold, an early end with a chance of order 1/N1/N, and a share who never hear that is set by the stopping rule alone (Sections 6.2 and 6.3). With catching the idea read as hearing it, the final shares in a large population can match, SIR at R0=2R_0 = 2, 3 and 4 leaving the same share unreached as the rumor model with k=1k = 1, 2 and 3 (our derivation; Box 6.1), so the final share alone does not show how spreaders stop (our judgment).

6.5 The price after spreading stops

Take the drug report of Sections 2.3 and 2.4 again. A contract pays $1 if a new drug passes its final trials. The prior, the chance before any news, is q0=0.30q_0 = 0.30, and whoever hears that a report right 80% of the time says success puts the chance at p≈0.632p \approx 0.632, as in Chapter 5. The example rests on three sets of premises. The price follows the rule of Section 5.3, under its premises, among them equal wealth, log utility and no learning from prices, with the noise traders’ demand set aside. Those who hear stop telling at some point but do not forget, so they keep their belief. And the news spreads by word of mouth alone, under Daley and Kendall’s five premises (Section 6.3). When the rumor has died out, a share xx of about 0.203 has never heard, a model value not compared with any real message’s coverage (Section 6.3), and the price rule with an informed fraction of 1−x1 - x gives q=(1−x)p+xq0,q = (1 - x)\,p + x\,q_0, about 0.56 (our calculation).7 With no outside source it stays there, since word of mouth alone does not take it to 0.632. The plateau also rests on no learning from prices. Traders who read others’ news from the price could move it further toward 0.632 without more word of mouth, though Section 5.7 found that such learning need not make the price reveal more (our judgment); Chapter 16 takes up learning from prices. A public disclosure, putting the news out in public, moves it further only through those who see it (our judgment), and at settlement, when the contract pays out, it jumps to 1 or 0 (Section 5.4). If spreaders stop only after two or three meetings with people who already know, the price stops at about 0.61 or 0.63, and in the deterministic version with probabilities of telling and stopping, for a large population, it stops no lower than about 0.54 (our calculation).

Forgetting pulls the price the other way. If those who have heard forget and return to the prior, the price falls back toward 0.30 (our judgment). SIR gives a worked contrast, under the premises of Section 6.2 and the price rule of Section 5.3, with R0=2.5R_0 = 2.5, a first spreader too small to count, catching the idea read as hearing it, equal wealth, and people who forget returning to the prior and, as in SIR, never catching the news again. If removal is forgetting, as Shiller suggests it might be described, only the current spreaders hold the belief of 0.632; they peak at about 23% of the population, the price peaks at about 0.38, and it ends back at 0.30. If removal only stops spreading, as in Bartholomew’s distinction, the price ends near 0.60 (our calculation).

Those who never hear learn the news only from a public disclosure, so the price reaction left for an announcement depends on the stopping rule; under the rumor model, once the rumor has died out, it depends little on how early word of mouth began (our derivation). This is untested. A test needs the volume of discussion on social media and prices around announcements, which this book has not assembled (Chapter 12).

Since newspaper and search counts follow newcomers or the people talking now (our judgment), a count that has fallen back shows neither that the price has caught up nor that those who heard have forgotten.

6.6 Stopping as a choice

Duffie, Malamud and Manso (2009) let people choose how hard to look for others to swap information with. They work out how information is shared over time, in equilibrium, in a large population, where each agent starts with signals about a random variable that matters to all. The uncertainty is Gaussian, following a bell curve, unlike the book’s yes-or-no fact; agents enter and leave, and the equilibria studied are stationary, steady over time for the population as a whole. Given what the others do, an agent meets someone at a rate proportional to the effort it currently chooses, and two agents who meet share what they know.

If the cost of search rises with effort, each extra unit costing more than the last, then, taking the information and efforts of others as given, an agent’s best effort falls as its own information grows more precise, because more information is worth less to an agent who already has more. With search costs proportional to effort, equilibria exist in which agents search with the most effort allowed until their information reaches a sufficient precision and with the least allowed after that, a rule called a trigger policy. The better informed reach that precision first, so they are the first to cut their effort (our judgment). The least effort allowed can be above zero; where it is, an agent who has cut to it still meets others, less often, and shares information at each meeting (our judgment).

In SIR every spreader has the same chance of stopping at each moment, set by a fixed recovery rate (Section 6.2); here the moment to cut effort is chosen, and it depends on how much the agent already knows (our judgment). Chapter 19 takes up the model of costly search.8

Weng, Flammini, Vespignani and Menczer (2012) model how memes, pieces of content such as a hashtag that users pass on, compete for attention on Twitter. In their basic model the network does not change; it is a directed sample of 10510^5 users from Twitter’s follower network. They model limited attention, people’s inability to attend to everything, by letting a post stay in a user’s list or memory for a limited time only, and a meme is forgotten once the last post carrying it has gone from the user’s list or memory. They measure the capacity of screen and memory as the time a post stays there, one time unit in their standard runs, a unit of the model’s clock equal to as many steps as there are users. This book calls that time the dwell window.

One window thus sets both how long a post stays visible and how long it is remembered. It is a form of limited attention, the subject of Chapter 10, and forgetting of the spontaneous kind, since a post leaves after a fixed time whoever else has seen it; the one parameter drops two of the root model’s defaults, permanent spreading and unlimited attention, under which everyone sees and uses all the information there is (our judgment). Their data cannot separate how long a post stays visible from how long it is remembered, and under the price rule of Section 5.3 a post that leaves the screen moves a price only if it also leaves memory (our judgment). With a window of 0.1 time units, which they call stronger competition, the model falls short of the many long-lived memes in the data; with 5 units it produces neither extremely popular memes nor extremely active users.

The authors write that their findings demonstrate that the structure of the social network together with competition for limited attention is “a sufficient condition” for wide differences in how popular memes become, how long they last and how active users are, with no need to assume that some ideas are intrinsically better; their results, they add, “do not constitute a proof” that intrinsic value plays no role. Chapter 10 compares a window like this one with a cap on how much a person can take in.9

6.8 A trader’s checklist

None of these questions says what to buy or sell, and good answers do not make a trade safe (our judgment).

  1. Stopped or forgotten? Check: when talk of a story dies down, ask whether people stopped telling it, lost sight of it or forgot it (Sections 6.1, 6.5 and 6.6). Under the price rule of Section 5.3 only forgetting pulls the price back toward the prior, so, with no other news, a price that holds while talk falls fits stopping and one that drifts back fits forgetting; a post that leaves the screen pulls the price back only if it also leaves memory, and Weng et al.’s data cannot tell the two apart (our judgment).
  2. How do the spreaders stop? Check: do they fall silent after a while, whoever they meet, or when they find that listeners already know (Sections 6.2 and 6.3)? Under homogeneous mixing the two give opposite results for the threshold and for an early end (Section 6.4), and in the models only under contact-triggered stopping does a spreader’s chance of stopping rise with the number who already know (our derivation; Section 6.3).
  3. What does the count measure? Check: before reading a series of mentions or searches as the number who know, mark whether it counts people newly hearing the story or people talking about it now (our judgment). In SIR the number spreading peaks while a share 1/R01/R_0 has still not caught the idea (our derivation; Section 6.2).
  4. Who has not heard yet? Testable: the price reaction left for an announcement depends on the stopping rule, and under the rumor model, once the rumor has died out, little on how early word of mouth began (our derivation; Section 6.5). The book has not yet tested it (Chapter 12).
  5. Do those who know most keep looking? Check: in Duffie, Malamud and Manso’s trigger equilibria, with search effort chosen and proportional search costs, the better informed cut their search to the minimum first (our judgment; Section 6.6). An agent who searches hard also raises the rate at which others are matched, so where that holds, a constant stopping rate fitted to the early spread may overstate how fast the news reaches the rest (our inference).

6.9 Summary

  • This chapter lets the root model’s spreaders stop. Stopping spreading and forgetting are two things (Bartholomew, as reported by Shiller); under the price rule of Section 5.3, stopping leaves the informed fraction and the price where they were, while forgetting moves both (our judgment).
  • Under spontaneous stopping at constant rates, as in SIR, SIS and forgetting, and under homogeneous mixing, an outbreak from one spreader needs R0>1R_0 > 1, and a cascade from one seed can end early with a chance that does not shrink as the population grows (our derivation). These model relations do not carry over to attention decay, a contagion rate that falls with time.
  • Under contact-triggered stopping, as in Daley and Kendall’s rumor model, there is no threshold under homogeneous mixing, and an almost immediate end has a chance of about 0.5/N0.5/N. With a closed population, homogeneous mixing, no outside source, one first spreader and stopping at the first meeting with someone who knows, about 20% never hear; this mathematical result has not been compared with any real message’s coverage.
  • Under the price rule of Section 5.3 and Daley and Kendall’s premises, with spreaders who stop but do not forget, the drug report’s price stays near 0.56 until public disclosure or settlement (our calculation); forgetting would pull it back toward 0.30 (our judgment).
  • In Duffie, Malamud and Manso’s model, with search effort chosen and proportional search costs, equilibria exist in which agents search hard until their information is precise enough and then cut to the minimum, so the better informed cut first (our judgment), while SIR’s spreaders stop at a fixed rate.
  • Weng et al.’s dwell window is both an attention window and forgetting, and their data cannot separate the two (our judgment).

Box 6.2 Independent cascades and bond percolation

Live edges. In Kempe, Kleinberg and Tardos’s analysis of the independent cascade of Section 6.2, each link’s coin is flipped in advance. A link (v,w)(v, w) whose coin comes up successful, which happens with probability pv,wp_{v,w}, independently of every other link, is a live edge in their word, and a node ends up active if and only if a path of live edges joins it to the starting set. We read this as bond percolation on a directed network, in which each link is open with its own probability pv,wp_{v,w} (our judgment); pv,wp_{v,w} is unrelated to the posterior pp.

One step of SIR. For an SIR model in which every infected node is removed a fixed time τ\tau after infection, Pastor-Satorras et al. let TT be the probability that the infection passes to a connected susceptible neighbor before recovery; the removed nodes from one seed then form the cluster of bond percolation with occupation probability TT that contains the seed, and the correspondence is exact for all late-time static properties. A node of the independent cascade tries once and stops, whoever it meets, so we read the model as an SIR whose infectious period, the time a node keeps spreading, is one step; its coins are independent, so one node’s transmissions are uncorrelated, as with a fixed infectious period (our judgment).

A random infectious period. Kenah and Robins (2007) take the stochastic SIR model studied by Newman, on a random undirected network with an arbitrary degree distribution, where an infected node ii infects neighbor jj with probability Tij=1−exp⁡(−βijτi)T_{ij} = 1 - \exp(-\beta_{ij}\tau_i), βij\beta_{ij} being the rate of infectious contact and τi\tau_i the time that ii remains infectious. Given τi\tau_i, the transmissions along ii’s links are independent, but their probabilities all rise with τi\tau_i, so they are dependent unless every infectious period is the same; bond percolation with occupation probability equal to the marginal probability of transmission treats them as independent whatever the distribution of τi\tau_i. It gives the right epidemic threshold, mean outbreak size below it and final size of an epidemic, the share infected in a large outbreak. When the infectious period varies, it fails to predict the distribution of outbreak sizes and the probability of an epidemic, which it overestimates. This book calls that probability, the chance that a single seed starts a large outbreak, the takeoff probability; in a large population it is one minus the chance of the early end in Section 6.2 (our judgment). The takeoff probability is never above the final size, and equals it only when epidemics have probability zero or the infectious period is constant. The TT and βij\beta_{ij} of this box are unrelated to Chapter 5’s deadline TT and word-of-mouth rate β\beta.

Scope. In this network SIR the final size and the threshold depend on the infectious period only through the mean probability of transmission, while the takeoff probability and the distribution of outbreak sizes also depend on how one spreader’s transmissions are correlated; Chapter 8 gives numbers. The mapping requires the joint distributions of recovery periods and infectious contact intervals to be defined beforehand. In the rumor model a spreader’s stopping time depends on whether others already know, so these results belong to spontaneous stopping and are not carried over to contact-triggered stopping (our judgment).12
