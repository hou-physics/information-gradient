Chapter 7 Networks and Epidemic Thresholds

Cohen and Frazzini (2008) pair suppliers with their main customers through SFAS No. 131, an accounting rule that requires firms to name any customer that makes up more than 10% of their sales; their customer data run from 1980 to 2004 (working-paper version). When a customer’s stock has a large return in some month, the supplier’s price makes about 60% of its eventual move that month and the remaining 40% over the next six months (working-paper version). Each month they count the mutual funds that hold both firms, and they expect news about related firms to reach prices faster where that count is high (working-paper version). In their Table IX, trading suppliers on their customers’ returns earned more where fewer funds held both firms, and the gap between the groups is significant at the 5% level in value-weighted portfolios and not in equal-weighted ones (working-paper version).

Cohen and Frazzini use the count as a proxy to pick out firms where attention constraints are more likely to bind, and they read the result as varying investor inattention (working-paper version). We read the same count as a network of holders, each fund that holds both stocks being a link along which the customer’s news can reach the supplier’s price, and their table cannot tell the two readings apart (our judgment). Section 1.6.5 read the count as the overlap between those who see the customer’s news and the capital that prices the supplier (our judgment), a reading Chapter 19 takes up.1

The root model of Chapter 5 lets anyone meet anyone; this chapter puts people on a network, a pattern of links along which news can pass. Apart from Cohen and Frazzini’s data and the network data of Section 7.7, it rests on models with no data behind them.

7.1 The two parts of homogeneous mixing

The root model’s word-of-mouth term, βI\beta I, rests on homogeneous mixing, one of the default assumptions listed in Table 5.1 in Section 5.8. Section 1.6.4 split it into two parts. In the part about meetings, anyone may meet anyone, and the states of two who meet are unrelated. In the part about sources, two who meet in a large population have not drawn on the same sources. Together the two are what gas theory calls molecular chaos (our analogy; Section 1.6.4). Karrer and Newman (2010) note that equations for flows between compartments, groups such as those who have heard and those who have not, assume the same chance of a contact that passes the disease on with every member of a compartment, far from true of real contacts.

They take a contact network as given and let disease pass only between people directly joined by one of its links; for news, it records who is in touch with whom. A person’s number of links is her degree, and the shares of people with each number of links make up the degree distribution. One person can keep up only a limited number of contacts, which caps how many links anyone can have; we count this as a further premise of the capacity law, the upper limit on the information a person can absorb per unit of time in Section 3.5 (our judgment), and Section 7.2 shows what such a cap does to the SIS threshold in degree-based mean field.

A network alone creates no threshold. Under permanent spreading, where those who know never stop telling or forget, spreading follows the susceptible–infected (SI) model, the limit of the susceptible–infected–recovered (SIR) model in which no one recovers, and in SI the whole population ends up infected; on a network this needs everyone to be reachable along links (our judgment). Any threshold presupposes spreaders who stop (Section 1.6.4), so every threshold in this chapter comes with one of the ways of stopping of Section 6.1.2

7.2 Networks with uneven numbers of links

On many networks the number of links is very uneven. In a power law, the share of people with kk links falls as kk to the power −γ-\gamma, where γ\gamma is the exponent. A scale-free network is one whose degree distribution has such a long tail, and the few people with very many links are its hubs. Pastor-Satorras and Vespignani (2001) cite studies of the Internet and the World Wide Web that found this form, with an exponent between 2 and 3, and Weng et al. (2012) write that Twitter’s follower graph has scale-free degree distributions, as expected in a social network.

The usual method for such networks is degree-based mean field, which treats all people with the same number of links as statistically alike. Of the network it keeps only each person’s number of links and the correlations between the numbers of links of two linked people, and it discards who is linked to whom. Pastor-Satorras et al. (2015) call it a strong approximation on a static network and a suitable one when contacts change much faster than the spreading. In this method people with more links meet others more often, which drops the claim that anyone may meet anyone; within a group with the same number of links it counts meetings as random and the states of those who meet as unrelated, and since it treats links as redrawn all the time, no fixed loop brings the same source back, so the part about sources holds (our judgment). Its equations, one for each number of links (Box 7.1), therefore stay closed, the quantities they follow being enough to say how those quantities change, and uneven links change only their form (our judgment).

In the susceptible–infected–susceptible (SIS) model, spreaders stop on their own and can catch the news again. Let the spreading rate λ\lambda be the rate at which a spreader passes the infection along one link divided by the rate at which it recovers. In degree-based mean field, on an uncorrelated network, one in which the numbers of links of two linked people are unrelated, the infection settles at a lasting level above zero, the endemic state, only if λ\lambda exceeds the epidemic threshold λc=⟨k⟩⟨k2⟩,\lambda_c = \frac{\langle k\rangle}{\langle k^2\rangle}, where ⟨k⟩\langle k\rangle is the average number of links and ⟨k2⟩\langle k^2\rangle the average of its square. A person with many links is more likely to be infected, along any of her links, and can then pass the infection along all of them, so each person counts by the square of her number of links and a few hubs make the threshold small (our judgment). If everyone has the same number of links, the threshold is 1/⟨k⟩1/\langle k\rangle. In a group of ten in which one person has 9 links, two have 2 and seven have 1, the average number of links is 2 and the average of its square is 9.6, of which the one hub supplies 8.1; the formula would give about 0.21, against 0.5 if all ten had 2 links (our calculation). Ten people are far from the large uncorrelated network the formula assumes, so these numbers only show how one hub enters it.

Under SIS and degree-based mean field, on a network of infinite size whose degree distribution is a power law with an exponent above 2 and at most 3, ⟨k2⟩\langle k^2\rangle grows without limit and the threshold tends to zero (Table 7.1). Pastor-Satorras and Vespignani’s simulations of SIS on networks with exponent 3 found no threshold in sufficiently large networks (Section 1.6.4), and they say their mean-field framework extends to exponents above 2 and up to 3.

In a finite network with a power-law degree distribution, the largest number of links, the natural cutoff, is often found to grow with the size NN as N1/(γ−1)N^{1/(\gamma-1)}. The cutoff keeps ⟨k2⟩\langle k^2\rangle finite, so this threshold stays above zero (our derivation; Table 7.1), and Pastor-Satorras et al. find the same when biological, social and cognitive constraints cap the number of links. They add that the vanishing threshold was soon seen as unrealistic for real networks, and they stress that a finite threshold is no reason to neglect uneven links, since for SIS simple homogeneous approaches can overestimate the threshold in heterogeneous networks by one or more orders of magnitude. Figure 7.1 shows the threshold on finite networks (our calculation).3

Where everyone has 6 links the threshold is 1/6 at every size; for a power law with exponent 3 it falls slowly with size, and with exponent 2.5 much faster (our calculation; Box 7.1). These results rest on SIS, degree-based mean field and uncorrelated networks read as networks whose links are redrawn much faster than the spreading (our judgment), on at least 3 links per person, and on the natural cutoff with a constant we chose (our derivation).4

Other methods give other answers for SIS. Individual-based mean field, which keeps the whole table of who is linked to whom, predicts a threshold that vanishes as the network grows on every network whose largest number of links grows with its size (Box 7.1). A rigorous result by Mountford et al., as reported by Pastor-Satorras et al., finds that on large power-law graphs the time until the infection dies out grows exponentially with the size, so the infection persists at any spreading rate.

In SIR, spreaders recover and then stay out for good. Degree-based mean field gives the same threshold, ⟨k⟩/⟨k2⟩\langle k\rangle/\langle k^2\rangle. On static, uncorrelated, locally tree-like networks, in which loops are so long that the network around any one person looks like a tree, SIR in which every spreader stops a fixed time after being infected maps onto bond percolation, each link being open with the probability that the infection passes along it (Box 6.2). With this mapping the threshold on a network of infinite size vanishes for exponents below 3, where ⟨k2⟩\langle k^2\rangle diverges, and stays finite above 3.

Under contact-triggered stopping, as in the rumor model of Section 6.3, which has no threshold under homogeneous mixing, a spreader stops on meeting people who already know. In simulations of the Maki–Thompson model on scale-free networks, uneven links lower the share who end up hearing, still without a threshold (as reported by Pastor-Satorras et al.), which the review explains by hubs that hear the rumor early and then soon stop spreading it. On a small-world network a rumor spread by Daley and Kendall’s rule can stay near its origin when the share of links rewired at random is below a critical value (Zanette, as reported by Castellano, Fortunato and Loreto and by Pastor-Satorras et al.; Section 6.3).

When spreaders can also forget at a rate of their own, Nekovee et al., as reported by Pastor-Satorras et al., find that the rumor model behaves exactly as SIR, with a threshold inversely proportional to ⟨k2⟩\langle k^2\rangle that vanishes on scale-free networks as they grow without limit. Results for spontaneous stopping in this chapter are not carried over to contact-triggered stopping (our judgment).5

7.3 Loops and clustering

Two of a person’s acquaintances who know each other may have heard the news from the same source, so hearing it from both is not hearing it from two sources (our judgment). Clustering is the degree to which a person’s acquaintances also know one another, and a loop is a path that leads from a person through others and back to her. On a fixed network with loops, meetings follow fixed links, which drops the part of homogeneous mixing about meetings, and shared sources drop the part about sources as well; the states of a person’s neighbors are then related, and equations written in each person’s own chance close only if the neighbors are treated as independent, as an approximation (our judgment).

Karrer and Newman (2010) study SIR with any distributions of the times to pass the disease on and to recover, on a given contact network. At the start each person is infected with some probability, independently of the others, so a single seed lies outside their results (our judgment). Along each link they pass a message, the probability that the neighbor at one end has not yet passed the disease to the person at the other end by a given time. This message passing is exact on trees and on locally tree-like networks such as large random graphs. In the configuration model, a random network built by giving each person a number of links and joining the links at random, the shortest loops grow longer as the network grows, so the messages a person receives along different links become independent in a large network, which, they write, in essence assumes that correlations around loops of growing length do not matter; there fixed links drop the part of homogeneous mixing about meetings, and independent messages keep the part about sources (our judgment). On a network with loops, the calculation can count the same person twice, once through each of two neighbors, and Karrer and Newman show that it then gives a rigorous upper bound on the number of people infected. In their setting, a count that treats a person’s neighbors as independent sources can thus only overstate how far an SIR spread reaches where sources overlap (our judgment); this book has no measure of how close the bound comes to the true number. Box 7.2 gives the calculation in symbols.

Pastor-Satorras et al. say the same of the mapping of SIR onto bond percolation, exact only for a tree and valid on locally tree-like random networks, while loops and multiple paths may invalidate it and short loops lead to a different conclusion.

Two results on clustering point in opposite directions. For SIR in a simple mean-field approximation, valid in principle only for networks without uneven links, Keeling found that outbreaks shrink as clustering grows (as reported by Pastor-Satorras et al.). For SIS in individual-based mean field, Pastor-Satorras et al. note that bounds in terms of the number of triangles in the network indicate a threshold that falls as clustering rises. The two differ in how spreaders stop, in the method and in what they measure, so this chapter draws from them no general rule on which way clustering pushes spreading (our judgment).

The upper bound belongs to SIR. Karrer and Newman have not found a similar message-passing solution for SIS, so this chapter does not carry the bound over to SIS (our judgment).

Kenah and Robins (2007) take the SIR studied by Newman and show that bond percolation with the mean probability of transmission gets right the threshold and the final size, the share infected in a large outbreak, and, when the infectious period varies, misses the takeoff probability, the chance that one seed starts a large outbreak (Box 6.2; Chapter 8).6

7.4 Direction, weight and groups

On Twitter, users can follow interesting people, and following forms a directed social network, one whose links run one way. In Weng et al.’s model of memes, pieces of content such as a hashtag that users pass on, a link from a user to a follower means that the user’s memes can be read on the follower’s screen. Direction drops the part of homogeneous mixing about meetings, since news passes along a link one way only, and on a locally tree-like network it keeps the part about sources (our judgment).

Weng et al. ran their model on a directed sample of 10510^5 users from Twitter’s follower network and, to study the role of the network, on a random network with the same numbers of nodes and edges, about 3×1063 \times 10^6 edges in each. On the random network the unevenness of how popular memes become, how long they last and how active users are is in general greatly reduced. For popularity, they point to the follower network’s broad degree distribution, with a consistent number of hub users who have many followers and whose memes are likely to achieve greater popularity, against the random network’s narrow one. The two networks differ in more than how uneven their links are (our judgment), and Section 6.6 gives the authors’ own limits on what their model shows.

When contacts differ, for example in how often they happen, a weighted network gives each link a weight. In individual-based mean field for SIS the threshold has the same form as without weights, each link’s weight taking the place of its entry in the table of who is linked to whom (Box 7.1). Weights drop the part about meetings, since some pairs meet more often than others; they add no loops, so the part about sources holds or fails as on the same network without weights (our judgment).

Community structure divides a network into groups with many links inside each and fewer between them. In SI, community structure makes the infection grow more slowly (Huang and Li, as reported by Pastor-Satorras et al.), which the review reads as communities tending to confine an outbreak around its first seed. Groups drop both parts of homogeneous mixing, since meetings gather inside a group and its dense links make short loops through which two people’s sources overlap (our judgment).7

7.5 Hubs in three kinds of spreading

Hubs do different things in different kinds of spreading (our judgment). In contagion, Pastor-Satorras and Vespignani’s result that hubs remove the threshold holds only within the limits of its row in Table 7.1. Pastor-Satorras et al. report, from simulations by Barthélemy et al. in their account of SI, that on scale-free networks the infection first reaches the hubs and from them quickly invades the rest of the network through classes with fewer and fewer links, while for the Maki–Thompson rumor model on such networks the review explains the result of Section 7.2 by hubs that hear early and soon stop spreading.

Watts’s threshold model, in which a person adopts once at least a threshold fraction of her neighbors have adopted, is a form of complex contagion, and Chapter 11 takes up what networks do to it.

In DeGroot learning, people repeatedly take weighted averages of their neighbors’ opinions; in Golub and Jackson’s version, with an independent noisy signal for each person whose mean is the true value and whose variance is bounded away from zero, a society is wise, everyone’s belief converging to the truth as it grows, if and only if the influence of the most influential person vanishes, and a bounded number of prominent people, who receive a nonvanishing amount of possibly indirect attention from everyone, make learning fail (working-paper version; Section 13.7).

In Acemoglu et al.’s Bayesian observational learning, each person gets a signal about the state, sees the past actions of a randomly drawn set of earlier people and chooses one of two actions, everyone preferring the same action given the state, in a perfect Bayesian equilibrium; asymptotic learning, the probability of the right action converging to 1, fails where a finite group is excessively influential, all that infinitely many people observe with a probability uniformly bounded away from zero, and holds where no such group exists and private beliefs are unbounded, signals implying likelihood ratios with no bound (working-paper version; Section 14.6).

Contagion in SIS under degree-based mean field turns on how large ⟨k2⟩\langle k^2\rangle is next to ⟨k⟩\langle k\rangle, DeGroot learning on whether influence gathers on a few people, and Bayesian observational learning with unbounded private beliefs on whether an excessively influential group exists (our judgment).

Kempe, Kleinberg and Tardos compared ways of choosing seeds in a computational experiment, running models on a real network with influence parameters they set: the coauthorship graph of the high-energy physics theory section of arXiv, with 10748 nodes and links between about 53000 pairs. They did not infer actual influence parameters from network observations; they used simple settings and estimated the reach of each set of seeds from 10000 simulations. In their linear threshold model, where each person adopts once a weighted share of her neighbors reaches a threshold drawn at random between 0 and 1, the greedy algorithm, which adds seeds one at a time, each with the largest gain, outperformed choosing the people with the most links by about 18%; they could not compute the best possible choice. The authors note that many of the best-connected nodes may be clustered, so that targeting all of them is unnecessary, and that their curves suggest that the network influence of many nodes is not accurately reflected by their degree or centrality. These results describe influence in their models on that one network (our judgment); Chapter 15 takes up the choice of seeds.

Networks also break the rule by which two people who meet add up what they know. In Duffie and Manso’s information percolation, two people who meet and tell each other what they know both end with the sum of the two (Sections 1.6.1 and 1.6.4; Chapter 19), which is right only when their sources do not overlap (our judgment), and on a network with loops the same news can come back along several paths. DeMarzo, Vayanos and Zwiebel call the failure to account for possible repetition in the information one receives persuasion bias; in their model, where people report their beliefs truthfully, it makes well-connected people influential because their information is repeated many times (working-paper version; Section 13.2). The repetition comes from the failure of the part of homogeneous mixing about sources (our judgment), and Chapter 13 develops persuasion bias.8

7.6 Networks and the price

Under the price rule of Section 5.3, with its premises, among them equal wealth, log utility and no learning from prices, and with the noise traders’ demand set aside, the price moves with the informed fraction, the share of people who know, which on a network grows along links. Under permanent spreading everyone hears in the end where everyone can be reached along links (Section 7.1), so the price ends where the root model’s does, and the network changes the path (our judgment).

In degree-based mean field on uncorrelated networks, Pastor-Satorras et al. read from the equations for SI and SIR that the time scale of an outbreak’s early growth is inversely proportional to ⟨k2⟩\langle k^2\rangle, so that where ⟨k2⟩\langle k^2\rangle diverges, as on scale-free networks, the time until the infection is established becomes very small, vanishing in the limit of infinite size. The price rule counts each person once, whatever her number of links, so news that has reached the hubs first (Section 7.5) moves the price only as far as the share of people who know (our judgment); whether those people hold the capital that sets the price is a question for Chapter 19.

When spreaders stop on their own, as in SIR, the spread ends at its final size (Section 7.3), reaching only a few below the threshold of its rows in Table 7.1, and under the price rule the price stays where that informed fraction puts it until a public disclosure or settlement, as in Section 6.5 (our judgment).

Cohen and Frazzini sort stocks each month into three groups by the number of funds that hold both firms, and within each group form long-short portfolios that trade suppliers on their customers’ returns, using quarterly fund holdings assumed unchanged between reports (working-paper version). In value-weighted portfolios, with customer data from 1980 to 2004, these long-short portfolios earned 3.02% a month where few funds held both firms and 0.55% where many did; the gap has a t-statistic of −1.98, significant at the 5% level, and in equal-weighted portfolios −1.44, which is not (working-paper version). In the network reading the count, the number of links between the two firms, acts as the weight of one link between them, in the sense of Section 7.4, and the models of this chapter, none of them fitted to fund holdings, would have heavier links carry the customer’s news to the supplier’s price sooner (our judgment).9

7.8 A trader’s checklist

None of these questions says what to buy or sell, and good answers do not make a trade safe (our judgment).

  1. How do the spreaders stop? Check: before reading any threshold, write down how its spreaders stop and the method that found it, as Table 7.1 does. On networks the rumor model, SIS and SIR need not agree, and for SIS different methods disagree; Pastor-Satorras and Vespignani’s threshold vanishes only within the limits of its row in Table 7.1.
  2. Do the sources overlap? Check: when several people in a tight circle say the same thing, ask whether they heard it from one source (Section 7.3; our judgment). For SIR with a share of people infected at the start, Karrer and Newman’s calculation on a network with loops, which can count the same person twice, gives an upper bound on how many are infected; failing to account for repeated news is what DeMarzo, Vayanos and Zwiebel call persuasion bias (working-paper version; Chapter 13).
  3. Is the best-connected account the best seed? Check: in Kempe, Kleinberg and Tardos’s computational experiment on one coauthorship network, with influence parameters they set, greedy seeds outperformed the best-connected by about 18% in their linear threshold model, and many of the best-connected nodes may be clustered (Section 7.5). Ask whether a second large account reaches people the first has not reached (our judgment).
  4. Does the consensus rest on a few voices? Check: in Golub and Jackson’s DeGroot model, with independent signals whose mean is the truth and whose variance is bounded away from zero, a bounded number of prominent people make learning fail; in Acemoglu et al.’s Bayesian model, with two actions, preferences that agree given the state, randomly drawn neighborhoods and unbounded private beliefs, only an excessively influential group stops asymptotic learning (working-paper version; Section 7.5). Which of the two applies depends on whether people average opinions or infer from actions (our judgment; Chapters 13 and 14).
  5. What links the two prices? Testable: Cohen and Frazzini expect news about related firms to reach prices faster where more funds hold both firms, and in their data from 1980 to 2004 the gap between the groups is significant at the 5% level only in value-weighted portfolios (working-paper version; Section 7.6). Whether the gap holds after 2004, in equal-weighted portfolios and after trading costs can be tested, and we read the count as a network of holders (our judgment).

7.9 Summary

  • Homogeneous mixing has a part about meetings and a part about sources. In degree-based mean field, which treats links as redrawn all the time, uneven links drop only the claim that anyone may meet anyone, so the equations change only in form; on a fixed network with loops both parts fail, and equations written in each person’s own chance no longer close (our judgment).
  • For SIS in degree-based mean field on uncorrelated networks the threshold is ⟨k⟩/⟨k2⟩\langle k\rangle/\langle k^2\rangle, which a few hubs make small; Pastor-Satorras and Vespignani’s result that it tends to zero holds only on a network of infinite size with a degree exponent above 2 and at most 3; the natural cutoff of a finite network keeps it above zero (our derivation; Figure 7.1); and for SIS other methods give other answers (Table 7.1).
  • Under contact-triggered stopping, simulations of the Maki–Thompson rumor model on scale-free networks find that uneven links lower the share who hear, still with no threshold (as reported by Pastor-Satorras et al.).
  • For SIR with a share of people infected at the start, Karrer and Newman’s message passing is exact on trees and on large locally tree-like networks and gives an upper bound on the number infected on networks with loops; failing to account for repeated information is persuasion bias, which in DeMarzo, Vayanos and Zwiebel’s model, with truthful reports, makes well-connected people influential (working-paper version; Chapter 13).
  • With independent signals whose mean is the truth and whose variance is bounded away from zero, a bounded number of prominent people make Golub and Jackson’s DeGroot learning fail (working-paper version; Chapter 13); with one signal each, a view of the past actions of a randomly drawn set of earlier people, two actions and preferences that agree given the state, an excessively influential group stops asymptotic learning in Acemoglu et al.’s Bayesian model (working-paper version; Chapter 14).
  • In customer data from 1980 to 2004, Cohen and Frazzini find that supplier prices take in news about their main customers over months and that trading on it paid more where fewer funds held both firms, a gap significant at the 5% level only in value-weighted portfolios (working-paper version); they read the count as a proxy for attention, we read it as a network of holders, and their table cannot tell the two apart (our judgment).
  • Apart from Cohen and Frazzini’s data and the network data of Section 7.7, the chapter rests on models with no data behind them.

SIS in degree-based mean field. With ρk\rho_k the probability that a node with kk links is infected and the recovery rate set to one, on an uncorrelated network dρkdt=−ρk+λk(1−ρk)Θ,Θ=∑k′k′P(k′)⟨k⟩ρk′,\frac{d\rho_k}{dt} = -\rho_k + \lambda k (1 - \rho_k)\,\Theta, \qquad \Theta = \sum_{k'} \frac{k' P(k')}{\langle k\rangle}\,\rho_{k'}, where Θ\Theta is the probability that a randomly chosen link leads to an infected node. A steady state with Θ>0\Theta > 0 exists only if λ>⟨k⟩/⟨k2⟩\lambda > \langle k\rangle/\langle k^2\rangle. The threshold tends to zero under five conditions together, SIS, degree-based mean field, an uncorrelated network, a network of infinite size and a power law with 2<γ≤32 < \gamma \le 3.

Finite networks. In the same method, with at least 3 links and at most KK, the sums over kk in ⟨k⟩/⟨k2⟩\langle k\rangle/\langle k^2\rangle give, for large KK, λc≈0.3949/(ln⁡K−0.9228)\lambda_c \approx 0.3949/(\ln K - 0.9228) at γ=3\gamma = 3 and λc≈1.2588/(2K−3.1675)\lambda_c \approx 1.2588/(2\sqrt{K} - 3.1675) at γ=2.5\gamma = 2.5 (our derivation). With K=⌊3N1/(γ−1)⌋K = \lfloor 3N^{1/(\gamma-1)} \rfloor, the cutoff of Figure 7.1, the first falls like 1/ln⁡N1/\ln N and the second like N−1/3N^{-1/3} (our derivation). For NN = 100, 1 million and 1 billion the threshold of Figure 7.1 is about 0.145, 0.0557 and 0.0375 at γ=3\gamma = 3 and about 0.0783, 0.00363 and 0.000363 at γ=2.5\gamma = 2.5 (our calculation).

Individual-based mean field for SIS. This method gives λc=1/Λ1\lambda_c = 1/\Lambda_1, where Λ1\Lambda_1 is the largest eigenvalue of the adjacency matrix, the table whose entry is 1 where two people are linked and 0 elsewhere (Section 7.2).

SIR. Degree-based mean field gives the same λc=⟨k⟩/⟨k2⟩\lambda_c = \langle k\rangle/\langle k^2\rangle on uncorrelated networks, exact when the links are redrawn very fast with P(k)P(k) unchanged. On static tree-like networks in which the numbers of links of linked people are unrelated, when every spreader is removed a fixed time after infection, let TT be the probability that it passes the infection to a linked susceptible before then. The mapping onto bond percolation gives the critical value Tc=⟨k⟩⟨k2⟩−⟨k⟩.T_c = \frac{\langle k\rangle}{\langle k^2\rangle - \langle k\rangle}. With a constant recovery rate the time to recovery varies from spreader to spreader, and replacing each link’s probability by its mean, λ/(1+λ)\lambda/(1 + \lambda), gives λc=⟨k⟩⟨k2⟩−2⟨k⟩,\lambda_c = \frac{\langle k\rangle}{\langle k^2\rangle - 2\langle k\rangle}, and this approximation gives the exact threshold, the mean outbreak size below it and the final size above it, though it fails in other respects, such as the takeoff probability (Section 7.3).

Compared with equal links. The variance of the number of links, ⟨k2⟩−⟨k⟩2\langle k^2\rangle - \langle k\rangle^2, is never negative, so ⟨k⟩/⟨k2⟩≤1/⟨k⟩\langle k\rangle/\langle k^2\rangle \le 1/\langle k\rangle and, for ⟨k⟩>1\langle k\rangle > 1, ⟨k⟩/(⟨k2⟩−⟨k⟩)≤1/(⟨k⟩−1)\langle k\rangle/(\langle k^2\rangle - \langle k\rangle) \le 1/(\langle k\rangle - 1), the values on a network where everyone has ⟨k⟩\langle k\rangle links, with equality only there. Taking every entry of a vector equal to one in the Rayleigh quotient of the adjacency matrix gives Λ1≥⟨k⟩\Lambda_1 \ge \langle k\rangle, so 1/Λ1≤1/⟨k⟩1/\Lambda_1 \le 1/\langle k\rangle (our derivation).

Box 7.2 Message passing and loops

Setting. Karrer and Newman’s SIR allows any distributions of the times to transmit and to recover, on a given contact network. At the start each person is susceptible with independent probability zz and infected otherwise. The message Hi←j(t)H^{i\leftarrow j}(t) is the probability that neighbor jj has not passed the disease to ii by time tt. The symbols zz, HH, FF and pp are theirs, and pp is unrelated to the posterior pp of earlier chapters.

Late times. With pp the total probability that the disease passes between two linked people, the late-time messages Fi←jF^{i\leftarrow j} satisfy Fi←j=1−p+pz∏l∈𝒩(j)\iFj←l,F^{i\leftarrow j} = 1 - p + p z \prod_{l \in \mathcal{N}(j)\setminus i} F^{j\leftarrow l}, where 𝒩(j)\i\mathcal{N}(j)\setminus i is the set of jj’s neighbors other than ii. The times to transmit and to recover thus enter only through pp, and Kenah and Robins (2007) find the same for the SIR studied by Newman (Section 7.3). Karrer and Newman define FF by the equation that the message HH obeys on a tree, which is perfectly well defined on any network, tree or otherwise. At late times the chance that person ii is still susceptible satisfies P(Si)≥z∏j∈𝒩(i)Fi←jP(S_i) \ge z \prod_{j \in \mathcal{N}(i)} F^{i\leftarrow j}, which gives an upper bound on the chance that she ever catches the disease and, summed over everyone, on the size of the outbreak. The late-time limit is related to a correlated bond percolation, correlated because a person who recovers quickly has a small chance of infecting each neighbor and one who recovers slowly a larger chance.

Loops. Let nin_i be the number of people within distance tt of ii, distance being the sum along a path of the delays before each transmission, and nijn_{ij} the number of those whose path to ii arrives through neighbor jj. Then ni≤∑jnijn_i \le \sum_j n_{ij}, with equality on a tree. On a network with a loop the sum counts some person twice, once along each of two paths, and from this inequality Karrer and Newman derive the rigorous upper bound.

The configuration model. Averaged over configuration-model networks (Section 7.3), Karrer and Newman’s late-time equations become precisely the standard equations for bond percolation on the configuration model.

Scope. The calculation is for SIR. Karrer and Newman have not found a similar solution for SIS, and they write that one could in principle extend it to random graphs with degree correlations or with clustering. When the infectious period varies, the takeoff probability depends on more than the mean probability of transmission (Box 6.2; Chapter 8). The rumor model’s contact-triggered stopping lies outside this setting and outside Kenah and Robins’s, since a spreader’s stopping depends on whether others already know (our judgment).12
