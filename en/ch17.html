Chapter 17 Insiders and Market Makers

When an institution moves its holdings from one set of managers or assets to another, a specialist firm can execute the orders; such a move is a portfolio transition. Kyle and Obizhaeva (2016) study 2,552 portfolio transitions executed by one such firm for US clients from 2001 to 2005, which after exclusions leave 439,765 orders. The orders are unlikely to rest on short-lived private information, because decisions to undertake transitions, and their timing, likely result from regularly scheduled meetings of investment committees and boards.

They measure a stock’s trading activity as the product of dollar volume and returns volatility, and it differs by a factor of 150 between the stocks in their lowest and highest groups. The hypothesis they call market microstructure invariance predicts powers of trading activity: an exponent of −2/3 for order size means that eight times the activity makes the median order a quarter as large a share of daily volume (our calculation). From the lowest group to the highest, the median order falls from 3.33% to 0.14% of average daily volume, by a factor of 23.79, against a predicted factor of about 28.23; the median bid–ask spread, the gap between the prices at which a dealer sells and buys, falls by a factor of 8.48, against about 5.31 predicted; and the average trading cost per order falls from 44.95 to 6.16 basis points, a basis point being 0.01%, somewhat more than the predicted factor of 5.31.

Scope. The test is a cross-section of US stocks traded in portfolio transitions from 2001 to 2005, in orders unlikely to rest on short-lived private information. What matches is the power of trading activity, economically close, across groups whose activity differs about 150-fold; at the two ends the declines in spreads, order sizes and average trading costs are 1.6, 0.84 and 1.4 times the predictions, within a factor of two (our calculation). The authors fitted the constants to the same orders, whose data are not public. The order-size exponent of −0.62 is statistically rejected against −2/3, and the spread-cost exponent of −0.49 lies three standard errors below −1/3; only the impact-cost exponent of −0.32 is consistent with −1/3.

Orders unlikely to carry news still paid spreads and other trading costs; in this book’s reading, the dealers who took them could not tell them from orders that do (our judgment). Chapter 4’s definitions of value and Chapter 5’s root model keep price taking, no trader’s orders moving the price, as a premise; this chapter drops it (Table 5.1 in Section 5.8). It also drops log utility, since Kyle’s insider and market makers are risk neutral; no observational learning and no learning from prices, together, since the market makers price others’ orders (Section 17.1); and, with trades that arrive at random, the deterministic mean field (Section 17.5). In place of the root model’s price, the average belief weighted by wealth, a market maker, a dealer who stands ready to buy and sell, prices the order flow, the net of buy and sell orders. The chapter asks how prices form from orders and how fast they learn (Sections 17.1 to 17.6), what shape and size measured impact has (Sections 17.7 to 17.9), and who quotes and who is informed (Section 17.10).1

17.1 Pricing the order flow

Section 1.6.2 gave Kyle’s (1985) model: a single risk-neutral insider, a trader with private information, the word implying nothing about legality; noise traders, who trade at random for reasons unrelated to information; and competitive risk-neutral market makers, who price at the expected value given what they see. They “do not observe the individual quantities traded by the insider or noise traders separately, nor do they have any other kind of special information”; they see the order flow.

From the root model to Kyle’s, four things change at once (our judgment). Price formation becomes the market makers’ conditional expectation. The order flow, the sum of others’ actions, sets the price, so learning from prices and from others’ actions become one, where the root model has neither; in the terms of Section 21.1.3 the market makers’ reading of the order flow is an inference-based higher-order belief, a belief about what others believe (our judgment), which Table 5.1 counts under no observational learning and no learning from prices and does not count as dropping first-order knowledge. The insider and the market makers are risk neutral. And spreading from person to person stops: the insider tells no one, and noise trading provides the camouflage that conceals his trading from market makers. The noise traders stay, as his cover, and his expected profits equal their expected losses (Section 4.6).

Table 17.1 sets the chapter’s four models of price formation side by side; Section 14.9 gave Avery and Zemsky’s (1998) special case of Glosten and Milgrom’s (1985) model (working-paper version).

17.2 Kyle’s insider

Kyle’s results below rest on the following premises. A single risk-neutral insider trades against noise traders and competitive risk-neutral market makers. In each auction the insider and the noise traders first choose the quantities they will trade, in effect placing market orders, orders filled at whatever price the market sets. The insider may not condition his quantity on the price; insiders who choose demand functions, or limit orders, orders to trade only at stated prices, appear in another of Kyle’s papers. Market makers see only the total order flow. The random variables are normally distributed, which gives the model a tractable linear structure, and in the continuous version the noise traders’ quantity follows a Brownian motion, a random walk in continuous time.

In a single auction the price rises with the order flow at a slope λ\lambda, whose inverse is the depth of the market, the order flow needed to move the price by one dollar (Section 1.9). Market depth is proportional to the amount of noise trading and inversely proportional to the amount of private information not yet incorporated into prices. His Theorem 1 gives a unique equilibrium among those in which the insider’s trade and the price are linear functions; for his sequential auctions he suspects, but has not been able to prove, that equilibria with nonlinear rules do not exist. Box 17.1 gives the equilibrium and notes a misprint in the paper’s statement of it. The insider’s expected profits are proportional to the standard deviations of both the value and the noise trades. Since more noise trading brings forth more informed trading, Kyle notes, it does not destabilize prices, a result which would disappear if the insider were risk averse.

Kyle discusses the continuous auction, in his word, “unrigorously”, taking for granted that a unique linear equilibrium exists with a structure like that of his sequential auctions; his Theorem 4 shows that as the intervals between sequential auctions shrink to zero, the sequential equilibria converge to the continuous one, although in a sequential auction equilibrium depth is not constant over time. The continuous results are therefore a limit, in which prices follow Brownian motion, the depth of the market is constant over time, and all private information is in the price by the end of trading. The private information not yet in the price, the variance Σ(t)\Sigma(t) of the value given the price, falls in a straight line, Σ(t)=(1−t)Σ0\Sigma(t) = (1 - t)\Sigma_0, with trading time tt running from 0 to the close at 1 and Σ0\Sigma_0 the prior variance. Kyle reads the constant volatility of prices as information entering them at a constant rate. The insider knows that the price will converge to the value, but to market makers, who do not observe the value, price fluctuations appear to have no drift.

Kyle explains why depth must stay constant. If depth ever increases, the insider wants to destabilize prices before the increase to generate unbounded profits; if it ever decreases, he wants to put all of his private information into the price immediately. With λ\lambda constant, any path of the remaining information that reaches zero at the close, and that some trading intensity can attain, satisfies the insider’s condition for maximum profit, so the equilibrium path comes from the market efficiency condition, the market makers’ pricing at expected value. In this book’s reading the pace at which private information leaks into the price is set by the deadline together with market efficiency; the insider has no preference among the paths, and the amount of noise trading leaves the path unchanged (our judgment).

An ex ante doubling of the noise traders’ quantities induces the insider and market makers to double theirs, has no effect on prices, and doubles the insider’s profits. This holds for a single insider who is risk neutral and whose information is given from outside (Section 4.6). Section 17.4 gives Kyle’s 1989 cases in which it does not.3

17.3 Kyle against the root model

Beside the root model of Chapter 5, Kyle’s model differs in three ways (our judgment). Information reaches the price only through the insider’s trades, with no word of mouth. The deadline and market efficiency set the pace (Section 17.2), so the remaining variance falls in a straight line, where in the root model contact rates and outside sources set it and the informed fraction rises along an S-curve when word of mouth outweighs the external source (Section 5.2). And to Kyle’s market makers the price is a Brownian motion without drift, while the root model’s price moves with the informed fraction, so someone who knows how far spreading has gone can foresee its direction (Section 5.3).

In units of information the picture changes (our derivation; Box 17.1). The price at time tt carries −12ln⁡(1−t)-\frac{1}{2}\ln(1 - t) nats about the value, a nat being a unit of information measured with natural logarithms, about 1.44 bits, at a rate of 1/[2(1−t)]1/[2(1 - t)] nats per unit of trading time that grows without bound as the close nears, so the constant rate that Kyle describes holds in units of variance. A single auction leaves half the variance, exactly half a bit whatever the parameters (our derivation).

In the price channel of Section 3.5, the price as a carrier of information, the order flow’s signal-to-noise ratio per unit of time, 1/(1−t)1/(1 - t), also grows without bound (our derivation; Box 17.1), so Kyle’s market makers are observers of unlimited capacity (our inference); this book has not checked whether constant depth and full revelation by the close survive the capacity law of Section 3.5 (our judgment). Figure 17.1, in Section 17.5, draws Kyle’s straight line.4

17.4 Trading on the price and bidding

Kyle’s (1989) model has three kinds of traders, noise traders, informed speculators and uninformed speculators. Speculators are imperfect competitors who maximize their expected utility of profits; after their private observations are realized, each chooses a demand function, taking as given the strategies other speculators use to choose theirs. A demand function, or demand schedule, says how much to buy at each price, so the trade is conditioned on the price, as a limit order is. An auctioneer aggregates the schedules, calculates a market clearing price and allocates quantities, so no market maker prices an order flow here. Section 4.6 gave his result that with imperfect competition the price never reveals more than half of the informed speculators’ private precision, in a single auction with normal variables and exponential utility.

Here more noise can make the price reveal more or less. By his Theorem 8.2, an increase in the number of informed speculators, or a decrease in noise trading per unit of risk-bearing, ρI2σz2\rho_I^2\sigma_z^2, raises the informational efficiency parameter φI\varphi_I, the share of the other informed traders’ precision that the price reveals to one of them. The theorem takes ρI2σz2/(N−1)\rho_I^2\sigma_z^2/(N - 1) to be positive, so it speaks of risk-averse informed speculators, for whom more noise lowers φI\varphi_I (our judgment). By his Theorem 10.5, in his model of monopolistic competition with an infinitely elastic supply of potential informed traders at a constant cost, an increase in noise trading increases the informativeness of prices.

Bidding is another way in which a trader’s own action sets his price. Milgrom’s (1981) paper on good and bad news applies its results to a sealed-bid tender for mineral rights. With the bidders’ estimates independent given the value and drawn from families with the monotone likelihood ratio property, under which a higher estimate is always better news about the value, one can sensibly assume nondecreasing bidding functions, and a bidder who won with a bid bb would find, on average, the value expected given his own estimate and given that every other bid was below bb, short of his estimate on his own information alone, “precisely the winner’s curse”, which higher bids alleviate. The winner pays his bid, so he is no price taker (our judgment), unlike the bidders of Section 16.6, from Milgrom’s other 1981 paper, whose price is the highest losing bid.5

17.5 Sequential trade

Glosten and Milgrom’s (1985) model has a specialist, the designated dealer of an exchange, who is risk neutral and competitive and faces no transactions costs, so that his expected profit from each transaction is zero. He quotes a bid and an ask for one unit, and each arriving investor is free to buy one unit at the ask, sell one at the bid, or leave; all orders are in effect market orders. Insiders, and liquidity traders who trade for reasons of their own, arrive at random, one at a time, which drops the root model’s spreading along its average path (our judgment), and the specialist sets the ask using the information that a purchase would reveal.

With no costs and zero expected profit, the spread in this model compensates the specialist only for adverse selection, the risk that the trader on the other side knows more (our judgment); the authors do not claim that adverse selection is the sole source of the bid–ask spread. Transaction prices form a martingale, a process whose best forecast is its current value, relative to both the specialist’s and the public information. By their Proposition 4, when trade is reasonably balanced, the probability of a purchase staying away from zero and one, the expectations of the specialist and the traders converge as the number of trades increases.

Their section 3 works an example. The stock is worth 1 or 11, with prior probability π\pi of the higher value, and insiders know which. Liquidity traders buy or sell with probability one half each, the case of perfectly inelastic supply and demand, and no information arrives from outside during trading. Each arriving trader is an insider with probability α\alpha, whatever the value. The bid and ask follow from the specialist’s breaking even (Box 17.2). For small α\alpha the expected number of trades before the insiders’ information is nearly all in the price is roughly proportional to 1/α21/\alpha^2, and the spread roughly proportional to α\alpha, so doubling α\alpha from 0.1 to 0.2, the authors conclude, roughly doubles the spread at each level of π\pi and divides by four the time until nearly all insider information is in the price; at a prior of one half the spread goes from exactly 1 to 2 (our calculation). Box 17.2 notes a misprint in the paper’s equation for that number of trades.

Each trade carries a bounded amount of information (our derivation; Box 17.2). In the example a trade’s mutual information with the value is largest at a prior of one half, about α2/2\alpha^2/2 nats (our derivation), 0.0050 for α=0.1\alpha = 0.1 and 0.0201 for α=0.2\alpha = 0.2 (our calculation), and the price learns the insiders’ information no faster than the arrival rate of trades times this amount (our derivation), while Kyle’s order flow has a signal-to-noise ratio that grows without bound (Section 17.3).

Kyle’s deadline pins the remaining information to zero at the close; in Glosten and Milgrom’s example, with no deadline, it falls with the number of trades at a pace set by the insider share and never quite reaches zero (our judgment; Figure 17.1).

The market can also close. If the insiders are too numerous or their information too good relative to the elasticity of liquidity traders’ supplies and demands, no bid and ask let the specialist break even, and no trade occurs. A closed market stays closed until the insiders go away or their information is at least partly disseminated from some other source; in the example, with stationarity and no outside flow of information assumed, it remains closed indefinitely. If uninformed demand and supply are inelastic, adverse selection can never close the market. In this book’s reading a closed market fits Avery and Zemsky’s definition of a cascade (Section 14.9), since trades then say nothing about the value, so their no-cascade result needs, besides the three premises of Section 14.9, noise demand inelastic enough to keep the market open, as their completely inelastic noise traders ensure (our judgment).

How the root model’s informed fraction would enter such quotes is left to the optional Box 17.3 (our derivation).6

17.6 Learning from trades and from no trades

Sections 3.3 and 5.7 gave Easley and O’Hara’s (1992) result that traders learn from trades and from the lack of trades, and their event uncertainty, the difficulty uninformed traders face in knowing whether new information exists. In the standard sequential trade framework used by Glosten and Milgrom, and in Kyle’s model, they write, event uncertainty does not arise, because an information event is assumed to have occurred. Their informed traders are risk neutral and take prices as given, an assumption that rules out strategic behavior by informed traders. If an information event occurs, the market maker expects a fraction μ\mu of trades to come from the informed, and an uninformed trader who checks the quote trades with a fixed probability.

Time and volume then matter: the numbers of buys, sales and no trades determine prices, so the total amount of trade affects price behavior, and the spread should decrease the longer the time between transactions.

Quotes, and thus transaction prices, converge to their strong-form efficient values, those that reflect the private information too, at exponential rates in clock time. If a given signal occurs, the rate is the smallest relative entropy, the cost of betting on one distribution when the other is true (Section 1.4), between the distribution of trade outcomes under that signal and under any other (Box 17.2). The rates rise with the fraction of trades from the informed and, when the uninformed are equally likely to buy or sell, fall with normal volume, because with more uninformed or liquidity trading the trades of the informed can be hidden more effectively.

They contrast this with Kyle’s model, in which orders are batched and altering normal volume has no effect on prices, because the single informed trader strategically scales his trade size, so that normal volume changes his profits while the adjustment of prices stays the same. Whether their result holds when the informed can adjust would need, they add, either endogenous entry of informed traders or an explicit strategic link between μ\mu and volume, an exercise to which their partial equilibrium competitive model is not amenable. In this book’s reading the two results differ in whether the intensity of informed trading responds to the noise (our judgment).7

17.7 Measured impact and the no-manipulation condition

Sections 3.1 and 3.6 gave Hasbrouck’s (1991) estimates for 80 NYSE and AMEX stocks in the first quarter of 1989: a trade’s full impact arrives only with a protracted lag and rises with trade size less than in proportion, a concavity, he writes, quite typical of all the stocks in the sample. Per share, then, the permanent impact, the part of a price move that stays, is smaller for large trades than for small ones (our judgment). Across firms, the price impact, and by implication the extent of the information asymmetry, appear more significant for firms with smaller market values. The note to this section compares his impacts with Kyle’s linear impact at the scale of invariance (Box 17.4), for a turnover that this book assumes.

A concave measured impact has more than one reading (our judgment). Hasbrouck’s own is that the information problem may differ for large block trades, negotiated between participants in a loose ongoing business relationship, and for small trades, which are relatively anonymous. Huberman and Stanzl (2004) note that empirical papers, Hasbrouck’s among them, assume time independence and equate the immediate reaction of the price with its permanent effect, and that read in the light of their work these results imply the feasibility of price manipulation; alternatively, they write, their work calls into question either assumption (working-paper version). And when trades reverse in the long run, vector autoregressions of low order, systems that forecast several series from the past of all, are likely to overstate the long-run impact (Section 3.6).

Section 3.7 and Box 3.2 gave Huberman and Stanzl’s conditions for a trader with no better information than others, any trade size being allowed. With impact the same in every period and others’ orders zero or normally distributed, ruling out price manipulation when trades come close enough together requires a permanent impact linear in volume (their Proposition 1); when linear impact changes over time and has no temporary part, it requires, for large enough initial prices, that liquidity not increase over time by too high a rate (their Theorem 2), or the trader could buy repeatedly in the early illiquid periods, pushing the price up, until the market becomes more liquid, and lock in expected profits (working-paper version). Kyle’s argument for constant depth runs on the same mechanism, applied to the insider (our judgment; Section 17.2). Huberman and Stanzl write that Kyle’s slopes are almost constant, so that price manipulation is infeasible and Kyle’s equilibrium is strongly viable, the trader’s optimal demand existing, unique and zero (working-paper version; Box 3.2). In the continuous-auction limit a constant λ\lambda is thus both a result of Kyle’s equilibrium and enough, by their Theorem 2 and for large enough initial prices, to rule out manipulation by such a trader (our judgment; working-paper version), one case of their condition that impact rising over time also meets (our derivation; Box 3.2). In Allen and Gale’s (1992) equilibria limited trade sizes let uninformed traders manipulate profitably (as reported by Huberman and Stanzl; working-paper version; Box 3.2). This book uses these conditions only to check models of impact (Section 3.7).

How an impact fades depends on which part is measured, the permanent or the temporary (Section 3.6); Hasbrouck notes that trades reversed, in his earlier work, only in large pooled samples of relatively low market-value stocks, and even there very weakly, over lags of up to 200 transactions.8

17.8 Invariance and the scale of price impact

Kyle and Obizhaeva define market microstructure invariance as the hypotheses that the distributions of risk transfers, which they call bets, and of transaction costs are constant across assets when measured per unit of business time, time that passes at the rate at which bets arrive in the market. Bets result from new ideas, which can be shared, so the buy orders of several customers who follow one analyst’s recommendation make up a single bet.

The spread-cost exponent lies three standard errors below its prediction (the opening’s scope box), a result that may have something to do with the minimum tick of one cent binding for some stocks. They sum up that their statistical tests usually reject invariance while the results are economically close to it. Invariance itself does not fix the shape of the cost function, and both a linear and a square-root model explain costs well. Because of market frictions they do not expect invariance to hold exactly across all assets and times; it may hold most closely when tick size is small, market makers are competitive and fees and taxes are minimal.

Applying invariance to Kyle’s model, they obtain a linear version of invariant transaction costs with no bid–ask spread term. This book uses their formula to put an order of magnitude on Kyle’s λ\lambda, a step that awaits checking (our derivation; Box 17.4): for their benchmark stock, with daily volatility of 2%, a price of $40 and a million shares traded a day, an order of 1% of daily volume executed at once moves the price by about 5.2 basis points (our derivation). The formula treats every bet as Kyle’s noise, so its λ\lambda is a lower bound (our derivation), a treatment that suits transition orders unlikely to carry short-lived private information (our judgment). Their own linear impact coefficient for such an order, calibrated from the orders’ costs, is 2.50 basis points, inside the 2.1 to 5.2 that the formula gives for orders executed at once or evenly, with their volatility multiplier between 0.8 and 1 (our derivation). Both figures come from the same orders, so this agreement in order of magnitude, a comparison the authors do not make, lies within one dataset (our judgment).9

17.9 Impact on a prediction market and in pumps

Tsang and Yang (2026) measure Kyle’s λ\lambda on Polymarket, a blockchain prediction market, for the 2024 US presidential election. They regress the change in the price’s log-odds on net order flow, aggregated into 15-minute intervals, month by month for the Trump YES share, the most actively traded of the six shares they examine. Because the platform mints and burns outcome shares inside ordinary trades, naive aggregation overstates turnover: in October their decomposition reports $391 million of Trump-market turnover against $958 million of naive volume, naive volume being turnover plus gross market activity, so that it overstates turnover 2+κ2 + \kappa times, κ\kappa being the market’s issuance-to-turnover ratio. With the corrected flow, a $1 million net purchase moves the log-odds by between 0.15 and 0.59 in the first half of 2024 and by about 0.02 in October, roughly 4 to 15 percentage points of probability against about half a point. Chapter 18 takes up turnover as the measure of volume.

Across 212 of 223 election markets, each with its own corrected λ\lambda, price impact falls with activity at an elasticity of −0.92, and activity alone accounts for 59 percent of the variation; they estimate impact from trades placed before the race was called and measure activity over each market’s life. In this book’s reading the elasticity does not test invariance, whose formula implies an elasticity of −2/3 for impact per dollar at a fixed volatility (our derivation; the note to this section): the regressor is a lifetime quantity, the regression does not control for the volatility of the log-odds, and the paper does not cite invariance (our judgment). In the units of stocks, at a price near 0.5, their October slope makes an order of 1% of a day’s October turnover move the price by about 14 basis points, about three to seven times the 2.1 to 5.2 basis points of Box 17.4 (our calculation).

Scope. One market, Polymarket’s Trump YES share in the 2024 US presidential election, from a preprint; λ\lambda is a linear slope on 15-minute flows. Price impact and volume must be measured with turnover, since naive volume overstates it 2+κ2 + \kappa times. The comparison with stocks, about three to seven times the stock benchmark with volume counted as turnover, needs scaling by volatility, and this book has not made the comparison with invariance (our judgment). The scope boxes of Sections 1.3 and 18.3 cover the calibration of Intrade and Kalshi.

Dhawan and Putniņš (2023), whose pumps Sections 4.1 and 5.5 described, assume linear price impacts of market orders, consistent with microstructure models of market making such as Kyle’s, with Pt=Pt−1+βxtP_t = P_{t-1} + \beta x_t for an order xtx_t (working-paper version). This book has not tested whether linear impact accounts for the size of the peaks: the paper does not report each pump’s daily dollar volume, daily volatility and net buying from the signal to the peak, and a rough calculation from aggregate figures, dominated by one exchange’s pumps and taking its volatility from the very rises it is meant to explain, would be no test (our judgment). None of the 70 pumps whose messages they sampled suggests that the coin is undervalued, and prices return to their pre-pump levels (working-paper version; Sections 4.1 and 15.4), so in this book’s reading the buyers’ orders carry no information about the coin’s value, and such a test would measure the temporary impact of uninformed orders (our judgment; Section 15.3.5).10

17.10 Who quotes and who is informed

Bürgi, Deng and Whelan (2026) study the Kalshi prediction market, where, in stock exchange terminology, makers make limit orders and takers make market orders (working-paper version). For Kalshi contracts from 2021 to April 2025 traded within 10 days of closing (Section 1.3), the average return after fees was −11.99% for makers and −31.46% for takers (working-paper version). They describe makers as relatively well-informed traders who post offers and seek positive expected returns but may be slightly over-optimistic, and for now treat takers as noise traders who accept the best available offers (working-paper version). In this book’s reading the roles run opposite to Glosten and Milgrom’s, in whose model, as in Kyle’s, those who quote are uninformed and the informed trade at their prices with market orders (Sections 17.2 and 17.5); the authors do not cite Glosten and Milgrom, and their model has no adverse selection (our judgment).

On Kalshi the spread comes from makers seeking their best price and allowing for fees (Section 3.7), with no institutionalized market makers driving it (working-paper version). If makers’ arbitrage-seeking were strong enough that they only broke even after fees, the bid–ask spreads would be driven solely by fees (working-paper version). In this book’s reading, as with Hasbrouck’s three components of a spread (Section 3.1), a spread can be read for how many are informed only after fees and the quoting side’s required return are taken out, and only what remains might relate to the informed fraction (our judgment; Box 17.3).

Bürgi, Deng and Whelan also report Linnainmaa’s (2010) finding that on the Helsinki Stock Exchange, around earnings announcements, market orders perform better than limit orders (as reported by Bürgi, Deng and Whelan; working-paper version). They call it an open question whether makers or takers perform better on a market like Kalshi that revolves around reacting to news (working-paper version). In this book’s reading, the role a trader plays and an informational advantage have no fixed correspondence, and makers’ and takers’ returns around data releases could show which side a trader is on (our judgment), a test for Chapter 24 that this book does not offer as trading advice.11

17.12 A trader’s checklist

None of these questions says what to buy or sell, and good answers do not make a trade safe (our judgment).

  1. Can the people who see my orders tell them from noise? Check: in Kyle’s model, with one risk-neutral insider, market orders, normal variables and competitive risk-neutral market makers, market makers see only the total order flow, and depth is proportional to the amount of noise trading for a given amount of private information not yet in the price (Sections 1.9 and 17.2).
  2. Is there a deadline? Check: in the limit of Kyle’s continuous auction, which he derives heuristically, and under the same premises, the private information is used up by the close, its variance falling in a straight line; the order flow cumulated from the start of the insider’s trading to just before the announcement should then on average have the sign of the announced value against the starting price, while each new piece of flow stays unpredictable to market makers, a test this book has not run (our derivation; Sections 17.2 and 17.3).
  3. How much does one trade reveal? Check: in Glosten and Milgrom’s example, with unit trades and liquidity traders who buy or sell with even chances, one trade carries at most about α2/2\alpha^2/2 nats about the value, α\alpha being the share of insiders (our derivation); in Easley and O’Hara’s model, with risk-neutral informed traders who take prices as given and with event uncertainty, the lack of trade carries information too (Sections 3.3, 17.5 and 17.6).
  4. Who quotes in this market? Check: on Kalshi from 2021 to April 2025, within 10 days of closing, makers, who post the quotes, lost less on average than takers after fees (working-paper version; Section 17.10).

17.13 Summary

  • In Kyle’s model, with one risk-neutral insider, market orders, normal variables and competitive risk-neutral market makers, the private information left out of the price falls, in the continuous-auction limit that Kyle derives heuristically, in a straight line of variance to zero at the close, and in this book’s reading the deadline and market efficiency set that pace (our judgment; Sections 17.2 and 17.3; Box 17.1 notes a misprint in Theorem 1); the price at time tt then carries −12ln⁡(1−t)-\frac{1}{2}\ln(1 - t) nats about the value, at a rate that grows without bound as the close nears, so Kyle’s constant rate holds in units of variance (our derivation; Section 17.3).
  • In Glosten and Milgrom’s example, with a competitive specialist, unit trades arriving at random and liquidity traders who buy or sell with even chances, one trade carries at most about α2/2\alpha^2/2 nats about the value, so the price learns no faster than the arrival rate of trades times that amount (our derivation; Section 17.5).
  • In Easley and O’Hara’s partial-equilibrium model, with risk-neutral informed traders who take prices as given and with event uncertainty, higher normal volume slows the convergence of quotes when the uninformed are equally likely to buy or sell; in Kyle’s, with one strategic insider, it leaves the adjustment of prices unchanged (Section 17.6).
  • In Huberman and Stanzl’s model, for a trader with no better information than others and any trade size allowed, ruling out price manipulation requires a linear permanent impact when impact is the same in every period, others’ orders are zero or normal and trades come close enough together, and, for large enough initial prices, liquidity that does not rise too fast when linear impact changes over time without a temporary part; Kyle’s constant λ\lambda, a result of his continuous-auction limit, meets both, and they find his equilibrium strongly viable (our judgment; working-paper version; Section 17.7).
  • Invariance applied to Kyle’s model, with Kyle and Obizhaeva’s multipliers at ζ=2\zeta = 2 and ψ=δ=1\psi = \delta = 1, gives only an order of magnitude for price impact, about 5.2 basis points for an order of 1% of daily volume executed at once in their benchmark stock; it is a lower bound, since the formula treats every bet as noise, it is sensitive to the right tail of the lognormal order sizes, and it awaits checking (our derivation; Section 17.8 and Box 17.4).
  • On Kalshi from 2021 to April 2025, within 10 days of closing, makers, whom Bürgi, Deng and Whelan describe as relatively well informed, lost less on average than takers after fees, and in their reading the spread would be driven by fees alone if makers only broke even after fees (working-paper version; Section 17.10).

Box 17.2 A trade as a channel

Quotes. In Glosten and Milgrom’s inelastic example the ask AA and the bid BB solve απ(11−A)=0.5(1−α)(A−E),α(1−π)(B−1)=0.5(1−α)(E−B),\alpha\pi(11 - A) = 0.5(1 - \alpha)(A - E), \qquad \alpha(1 - \pi)(B - 1) = 0.5(1 - \alpha)(E - B), their (8) and (10), with E=11π+1(1−π)E = 11\pi + 1(1 - \pi). Trades to assimilate. One trade as a channel (our derivation). A trade is a purchase with probability (1+α)/2(1 + \alpha)/2 if the value is high and (1−α)/2(1 - \alpha)/2 if it is low, and the relative entropy between the two is αln⁡[(1+α)/(1−α)]≈2α2\alpha\ln[(1 + \alpha)/(1 - \alpha)] \approx 2\alpha^2 nats, the denominator of (12). Easley and O’Hara define the entropy of one distribution of trades relative to another over no trade, a buy and a sale, their (10); with buys and sales only and the uninformed equally likely to buy or sell, between good and bad news it is the same expression with μ\mu for α\alpha, and since their rate takes the smallest entropy against any other signal, the possibility of no information event can make the rate smaller. At a prior of one half the mutual information of one Glosten–Milgrom trade with the value is ln⁡2−h((1+α)/2)≈α2/2\ln 2 - h((1 + \alpha)/2) \approx \alpha^2/2 nats, hh being the binary entropy, and no prior gives more; kk trades carry at most kk times as much, and never more than ln⁡2\ln 2.13

Box 17.3 The informed fraction in the quotes

This optional box is this book’s own derivation throughout, not checked within a full model (our derivation). If arriving traders are drawn at random from a population in which a fraction I(t)I(t) already knows the news, the root model’s informed fraction, and the rest trade as Glosten and Milgrom’s liquidity traders do, then α=I(t)\alpha = I(t) in their example. At a prior of one half a trade carries about I(t)2/2I(t)^2/2 nats, so early in spreading, while the informed are few, the price barely moves, and learning speeds up with the square of II. A specialist who knows I(t)I(t) sets a spread roughly proportional to it for small II, exactly 10I(t)10I(t) at a prior of one half, a reading of the informed fraction.

Two premises carry this: the spread comes from adverse selection alone, which Section 17.10 shows need not hold, and the specialist knows I(t)I(t), without which he faces the multidimensional uncertainty of Section 14.9. Chapter 19 goes further.
