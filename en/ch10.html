Chapter 10 Limited Attention

DellaVigna and Pollet (2009) studied earnings news that arrives at the end of the week, asking whether more investors are distracted on Fridays. Their data are US quarterly earnings announcements from January 1995 until June 2006, 143,583 in the final sample, of which only 5.7% came on a Friday (working-paper version). Compared with announcements on other weekdays, Friday announcements had a 15% lower immediate response, measured over the day of the announcement and the next, a 70% higher delayed response, measured from the 2nd to the 75th trading day after it, and 8% lower trading volume around them (working-paper version).

In their model a share of investors is distracted each period and does not see the signal about the company’s performance, so that if the share is larger on Fridays, less of the price move comes at once and more of it comes later, while the whole move stays the same size (working-paper version). That is how their model reads the numbers, and the late drift could have other causes too (our judgment; Section 1.6.5). In their data the whole move is somewhat larger for Friday than for other announcements (working-paper version), which their measure of delay allows for (Section 10.2).1

10.1 Forms of limited attention

News passes through three steps on its way into a price: it becomes publicly available, so that anyone could look it up; the people who set the price see it; and they use it, giving it its due weight (Sections 1.6.5 and 4.2). In DellaVigna and Pollet’s model the Friday announcements stall between the first and second steps, because the distracted investors do not see them (working-paper version; Section 10.2); in Hirshleifer and Teoh’s model pro forma earnings stall between the second and third, because the inattentive investors see the number and take it at face value (Sections 9.3 and 10.3). Both placements come from the models.

The root model assumes unlimited attention, a default listed in Table 5.1 (Section 5.8): getting and processing information costs nothing, and everyone sees all the information there is and uses it. This chapter drops it.

Two layers of attention need to be kept apart. The observation layer is whether a person sees the news at all; the processing layer is how much weight she gives it once she has seen it, which repetition, salience and tone can change. The difference shows when the same news comes back: someone who missed it the first time trades on it as new, while someone who has already used it may count it twice (our judgment; Section 10.7). The shape of a price reaction does not tell the two layers apart, and telling them apart takes a reference signal, a comparison built into the evidence, such as the same content released a second time (our judgment; Sections 10.2 and 10.7).

Sections 10.2 to 10.7 take up six forms of limited attention, which Table 10.1 sets side by side; which layer each belongs to, and whether the capacity law covers it, is this book’s classification (our judgment). We keep the forms apart because each limits something different and calls for a different check. The capacity law of Section 3.5 covers only rational inattention; that section does not count not seeing, salience and failing to work out what one has seen against capacity, and we put the dwell window with them (our judgment). Three related costs belong to other chapters: information that costs money (Section 4.5, and with learning from prices Chapter 16), searching for others to learn from (Chapter 19) and the physical cost of processing information (Section 4.8).

10.2 Observation gating

DellaVigna and Pollet model a continuum of investors, of whom a share, the attentive investors, observe the earnings signal and use it to decide how much to hold, while the rest, the distracted investors, neither observe it nor use it (working-paper version). Given limits to arbitrage in the form of risk aversion, the distracted investors affect prices; in the model, a larger share of them shrinks the immediate response of prices and magnifies the delayed response, and the combined response is not affected (working-paper version).

They measure the delay with the delayed response ratio, the share of the price response that comes late: of the gap between the price moves after the best and the worst earnings surprises, the part that comes from the 2nd to the 75th trading day divided by the whole of it from day 0 to day 75 (working-paper version). In their model the ratio is the difference between the delayed responses to two signals divided by the difference between their long-run responses, and it gives only a lower bound on the share of distracted investors (working-paper version), a bound the manuscript states in more than one form (Box 10.2).

In round numbers the authors write that the delayed response is 60% of the total on Fridays and 40% on other weekdays, and that by their model at least 60% and 40% of investors respectively were distracted (working-paper version). Across the seven specifications of their Table II, the Friday ratio exceeds the other days’ by 0.1131 to 0.1952, with the Friday ratio between 0.5388 and 0.6187 and the other days’ between 0.4068 and 0.4491 (working-paper version). The Friday test speaks to the difference between Fridays and other days; the level on other days also holds whatever else makes prices drift after earnings news, so it says little about how many investors are distracted on an ordinary day (our judgment; Chapter 12). The larger whole move after Friday announcements departs from the model, and the authors note that the ratio controls for it by renormalizing with the long-run reaction (working-paper version).

Scope. The test covers only one kind of low-attention day, Friday, in US quarterly earnings announcements from January 1995 to June 2006 (working-paper version). The delayed share of the total response is about 54% to 62% on Fridays and about 41% to 45% on other weekdays (working-paper version); it gives only a lower bound on the share of distracted investors, and the model makes no prediction of how large that share is. The figures come from the authors’ manuscript, and we have not checked the journal version.

In their static model an investor who does not see the signal and one who sees it and gives it no weight hold the same position and leave the same price (our judgment). The Friday numbers therefore fit news that went unused at the time, and the shape of the price reaction cannot say whether it went unseen or was seen and set aside; the case counts as evidence for the observation layer only because the authors chose Fridays to pick out who was not watching (our judgment; Section 10.7). A footnote of theirs adds that in their model stock returns are the same whether the distracted investors see the signal one period late or never (working-paper version), both cases in the observation layer (our judgment).

The data leave open when the weaker reaction happens: the authors cannot separate announcements made during trading hours from those made after the close, so they cannot test whether the first reaction is weaker on the Friday or on the Monday after the weekend, and in recent years Friday announcements are significantly less likely to come after the close, which makes a direct comparison impossible (working-paper version).

Firms choose the day on which they announce: in the authors’ model, short-term managers with sufficiently negative news release it on high-distraction days because they prefer an attenuated immediate response (working-paper version; Section 15.2.2). Their ratio divides the gap between the reactions to the best and the worst surprises by the whole move, so more bad news on Fridays does not by itself raise it, as long as prices move in proportion to the surprise (our judgment). Friday announcers may still differ in other ways; the authors write that it is impossible to fully control for company heterogeneity and that their results are robust to controls for time, market capitalization and the volatility of earnings surprises (working-paper version).2

Cohen and Frazzini (2008) give a second case. In their customer data, which cover 1980 to 2004, when a firm’s main customers have a large return in a month, the supplier’s stock price covers about 60% of the initial price gap in that month and closes the remaining 40% over the next six months (working-paper version). The customers’ own prices take in nearly all of their move within the month, with a coefficient of 0.94 that is not statistically different from one (working-paper version). We read this as the same public news reaching the asset it concerns within a month and a linked asset over several months (our judgment). The supplier’s late move fits holders who did not see the customer’s news and also holders who saw it and gave it too little weight, and the tables cannot separate the two (our judgment). The authors use a proxy to find firms where attention constraints are more likely to bind and read their result as inattention (working-paper version); this book counts the case as evidence for the observation layer because of that design, since the proxy, the number of funds holding both firms, is meant to pick out suppliers whose holders are less likely to gather information about the customer (our judgment; Section 10.8).

Distraction is one of at least three mechanisms behind underreaction to public news, which Chapter 12 sets side by side (our judgment). The extra drift after Friday announcements becomes more readily apparent about 30 trading days after the announcement (working-paper version), and the data cannot separate investors who attend again later from the cut-off that the next scheduled announcement brings (our judgment; Chapter 12).3

10.3 Salience

Suppose two firms pay their managers partly in stock options, and one charges the expected cost against its earnings while the other discloses it only in a footnote; the two disclosures carry the same information. Hirshleifer and Teoh (2003) argue that with limited attention informationally equivalent disclosures can still affect investors’ perceptions differently, depending on how they are presented, and their model assumes that information presented in a salient, easily processed form is absorbed more easily, for example that costs expensed in the financial statements are more salient than costs disclosed only in footnotes.

Their Proposition 8 states that if some investors have limited attention, then under rules that do not expense the expected cost of option pay when the options are granted, the market overvalues the firm and its long-run abnormal returns, the returns beyond what is normal for a stock like it, are negative; when the rules expense the full cost, the reverse holds. Garvey and Milbourn (2003) find that the more option compensation a firm leaves unrecognized, the lower its later abnormal returns during 1996–2000, with the poor returns concentrated in the months when quarterly financial reports came out (as reported by Hirshleifer and Teoh). It is the only study across many firms on salience and returns among this chapter’s sources, it covers five years, and this book knows it only second-hand, so it carries little weight on its own (our judgment).

With pro forma earnings (Section 9.3), the inattentive investors of their model see the reported figure and treat it as if it had been adjusted to be as informative as possible, mistakenly thinking they process the information fully, and similar results hold so long as some inattentive investors lack that self-awareness. We read salience in their model as a matter of processing, since the information is public and sometimes already seen and what fails is how much of it is used; the exception is an investor who skips the footnotes and has not read it in at all (our judgment). Among their model’s limits the authors list that attention is exogenous and that they do not explicitly analyze how investors allocate it.

Sims (2003) gives an example from newspapers. Many print the Federal Funds rate every day at a predictable place in the back of the business section, and the vast majority of readers neither look at it every day nor, if they look at the page, change their behavior in reaction to it, both layers at once (our judgment); a three-column, front-page headline in the New York Times that the Fed had unexpectedly raised the rate by 1.5% would likely move many of its readers to act. In Sims’s own model a person codes what she sees as well as she can, so the same information presented two ways makes no difference to her, and presentation matters only through the coding service that a newspaper performs for its readers (our judgment).

Old news can move a price again when it comes back more prominently. In the EntreMed case of Section 1.1, the front-page New York Times story of May 3, 1998, presented virtually the same information that the newspaper had reported in November, but much more prominently; the November report had appeared on page A28, and the stock rose far more after the second story. Merton (1987) gives two readings of such a rise and says how to tell them apart.

  • A wider investor base. A newspaper or other media story that reaches many investors who do not yet hold a stock could lead some of them to start following the firm, and in his model a relative increase in the investor base lowers the firm’s cost of capital and raises its market value (working-paper version). On this reading the rise lasts.
  • A fad. The same investor and firm actions can also arise in models of nonrational behavior, in which media coverage can create and sustain fads, and one could test the fads hypothesis by asking whether the price changes are transient or permanent, provided fads do not last too long (working-paper version). On this reading the rise fades.

For EntreMed, Huberman and Regev write that their figure gives the impression that some of the May 4 run-up was temporary and a substantial portion permanent. One event cannot say which part goes with which hypothesis, nor how much each contributes (our judgment).

Novelty, Vosoughi, Roy and Aral write, attracts human attention, and we count it as one more kind of salience (our judgment; Section 9.4). How a firm chooses to present its news is the subject of Chapter 15.4

10.4 Rational inattention and the capacity law

Sims’s (2003) rational inattention models attention as a channel that carries only so much information each period: a person’s actions can depend on what she observes only through a channel of finite Shannon capacity, and choosing the character of her observation error is part of her optimization. The information she gains each period about the current state stays below a capacity κ\kappa, measured in bits, and with one quantity to track that constraint completes the problem; it is the cap of the capacity law of Section 3.5. One bit halves the possibilities, as the answer to a yes-or-no question with two equally likely answers does (Section 2.2).

Sims’s own example is a consumer keeping track of her wealth, which after consumption grows by the gross interest rate each period, with her income added to it; in the simplest case the income is independent from one period to the next. With normally distributed income and the capacity fully used, each period the uncertainty first grows, as a new income shock arrives and the old error carries over, and the period’s observations then shrink it. For a normally distributed quantity, each bit she takes in halves the standard deviation of her error, so κ\kappa bits cut its variance to 2−2κ2^{-2\kappa} of what it was (our derivation).

If the quantity is a fixed fact that takes no new shocks, such as the earnings of a quarter that has ended and that the firm has not yet reported, each period cuts the variance by that factor again and it goes to zero, so a capacity limit only slows learning (our derivation). A fact with two outcomes holds at most 1 bit (Sections 2.2 and 2.3), while a normally distributed quantity can take any value, which always leaves more to learn (our judgment).

If the quantity keeps moving, as a firm’s sales or an asset’s price do, a fresh shock arrives every period. Provided the capacity outpaces the rate at which the quantity’s own dynamics amplify old errors, the variance settles at a floor above zero, where what each shock adds equals what her readings remove (our derivation; Box 10.1); a random walk, which adds an independent shock to its last value each period, does not amplify old errors, so any positive capacity gives it a floor (our derivation). In this model she never fully catches up: her estimate is right on average, but its error stays at the floor and each new shock enters it only gradually (our derivation). For a random walk tracked at 0.5 bit a period, half of a shock enters at the first reading, three quarters after two and seven eighths after three (our calculation). The model describes one observer. In a price set by many people who can trade freely in both directions, errors that differ from person to person tend to cancel, and mainly the part they share, from a common source or a common way of reading the news, would move the price (our judgment); with short selling restricted, as in Miller’s argument (Section 10.9), the spread of errors can move the price too (our judgment; Chapter 20).

Figure 10.1 puts numbers on the two cases for capacities of 0.25, 0.5 and 1 bit a period. With a fixed fact all three lines head for zero; with a random walk they level off at 0.60, 0.25 and 0.083 of the starting uncertainty, and at 0.5 bit the floor equals the variance of one period’s shock (our calculation).5

Two numbers printed in Sims’s paper need correcting (Box 10.1). The capacity of his Figure 6, which uses a weight of 0.5 on each new reading and which he prints as 0.21 bit, is 0.5 bit by his own equations (our calculation), and with his capacity in bits the base of his steady state is 2 where he prints that of natural logarithms (our derivation).

Sims suggests that several tracking problems could share one capacity, each problem’s share set by how much it matters to her and how much more capacity would gain her on it; in his example with three components of income, the best use of capacity tracks the unpredictable components closely and leaves much more observation error on the slow-moving “aggregate” component. One budget for everything a person tracks is Sims’s modeling assumption, and Shannon’s theory gives each channel its own capacity (our judgment; Section 2.7).

None of the sources this book has read measures a person’s capacity: Sims’s numbers are numerical examples, and he does not estimate κ\kappa. He writes that a low rate of information about macroeconomic data can be justified only by assuming, as seems realistic to him, that most people devote nearly all of their information-processing capacity to other kinds of information, and that it is not unreasonable to model people as reacting to macroeconomic data as if it reached them through a low-capacity channel. The investor attention of Section 10.9, how much notice an asset draws, is a different quantity from a person’s capacity (our judgment).6

10.5 The dwell window and competing messages

Section 6.6 gave a second way of modeling limited attention, from Weng, Flammini, Vespignani and Menczer (2012). In their model a post survives in a Twitter user’s list or memory only for a finite time, and one window sets both how long a post can be seen and how long it is remembered. This book calls it the dwell window; it works in the observation layer, together with forgetting (our judgment).

A window and a capacity limit attention in different ways (our judgment). Suppose a trader saw a stock tip yesterday. Under a window, the chance that she passes it on today depends only on how long ago it arrived, whether ten new tips have come in since or a hundred; under one capacity shared by all messages, Sims’s modeling assumption (Section 10.4), every new tip leaves less attention for the old one (our judgment). In Weng et al.’s model each meme on the screen, a piece of content such as a hashtag, catches the user’s attention with a fixed probability, whatever else is on the screen, so memes crowd one another out only when she posts from memory, where each meme’s chance follows its share of what she remembers (our judgment). A test would ask whether a given old topic is retweeted less often in periods when unusually many new topics arrive (our inference); since busy periods differ in other ways too, it would have to allow for each topic’s own trend, and none of this book’s sources has run it (our judgment).

Weng et al. measure a user’s breadth of attention by the entropy of the topics she posts about, zero when all her posts are about one meme and largest when each is about a different meme. They find that a user’s breadth of attention stays essentially constant however diverse the system is, which they read as a cap on the number of memes a user can attend to, and their model assumes outright that memes survive at the expense of others. The entropy measures how spread out a user’s posts are, and the number of her posts bounds it, so a steady average could also come from a steady number of posts (our judgment). Their data are retweets from October 2010 to January 2011, though some of their figures run longer (our judgment; Section 6.7).

The authors write that the structure of the social network together with competition for finite attention is a sufficient condition for broad differences in how popular memes become, and that their results do not prove that intrinsic value plays no role (Section 6.6). When a topic or a stock is unusually popular, we would first ask whether the unevenness of the network and the dwell window can account for it, before crediting quality or information (our judgment).7

10.6 Choosing what to learn

Suppose a trader has time to study only one question about a stock and must sell within weeks of buying. What she learns earns her nothing if it reaches the price only after she has sold, so her study can pay before she sells only if the answer is announced in time or enough other traders learn the same thing and trade on it (our judgment).

Brunnermeier (2001) surveys models of this choice. Traders who liquidate their positions early care more about future price developments than about the true fundamental value, so they prefer short-run information to long-run information and might even ignore long-run information; because the future price also depends on what others gather, they have an incentive to gather the same information, and this investigative herding is the focus of Froot, Scharfstein and Stein (1992).

In Froot, Scharfstein and Stein’s model, each trader can collect only one piece of information, a signal about one of two events, and must unwind the position in the third period, though the information might reach the price only in the fourth (as reported by Brunnermeier). The asset’s value is announced before the traders unwind with some probability, and after otherwise. If it always comes after, all traders gather information about the same event and nobody gathers information about the other, and herding can still occur when the chance of an announcement before they unwind is small enough. When the announcement is sure to come before they unwind, each trader prefers to study events that are not the main focus of the others’ information gathering, so that their search efforts are “strategic substitutes”, and they are “strategic complements” when it never comes early: substitutes means that the more the others study an event, the less it pays to study it too, and complements the reverse. If traders cared only about the final liquidation value, this herding would not occur, and it would be better to have information that others do not have (as reported by Brunnermeier).

Section 3.4 argued that the common conclusion, that others learning the same news lowers its value to a holder, assumes that she holds until the news is public; with a shorter holding period their learning can raise it, though if many hold the same position and leave together their exits move the price against them (our judgment). Investigative herding is what that does to the choice of what to gather (our judgment). The choice comes before any gathering, which sets it apart from not seeing news (Section 10.2) and from a cap on what a person can take in (Section 10.4), and it counts as limited attention because each trader can gather only so much, one piece in the model (our judgment). Chapter 23 takes up holding periods that end before prices converge.8

10.7 Limited processing and decision capacity

Tetlock (2011) asked whether investors react to stale news, stories that repeat what has already appeared. His data are public news about S&P 500 firms from 1984 to 2004, over 350,000 stories in all (working-paper version). Market reactions to stale stories partially reverse in the next week, while reactions to stories with more new information reverse to a much smaller extent, or even continue (working-paper version). Among earnings news, cumulative reversals of 16 basis points, hundredths of a percent, over the second to fifth trading days follow the most stale stories, and continuations of 36 basis points follow the least stale, the freshest; his separate estimate of the difference between the two is a cumulative 54 basis points over four days, with a t-statistic of 3.30, a value chance alone would rarely give (working-paper version). The reversals after stale stories are larger in stocks with a high fraction of small trades, and he reads these and other results as consistent with individual investors overreacting to stale information (working-paper version).

In the model of his appendix, a liquidating dividend is gradually revealed to all investors over three periods, and two types of investor differ only in how they process the second period’s signal, which contains both old and new information (working-paper version). Irrational investors take part of the old signal for new information, and the root cause of both overreactions in his model is that they confuse stale and new information (working-paper version). Both types see the same signals, so the difference lies wholly in the processing rule, and we place the effect in the processing layer (our judgment). Taking old news for new is the double counting of Section 2.4 and the persuasion bias of Section 7.5, and Chapter 13 follows its effect on prices (our judgment).

Section 3.3 left it to the data to decide whether observation gating or the weight given in processing is at work, and Tetlock’s split between stale and fresh news is the kind of reference signal that lets them decide, since a stale story replays content that has appeared before (our judgment). A replay leaves a different trace in each layer: if some of the people who set the price had missed the content and the rest had weighted it by Bayes’ rule, the price moves and stays; if everyone had seen it, the price does not move; and if they had seen it and now count it again, the double counting of processing, the price first moves too far and then moves back (our judgment). The reversal after stale news therefore fits double counting and does not fit observation gating with weights set by Bayes’ rule (our judgment). The reversal is only partial, though (working-paper version): the part that reverses also fits a fad (Section 10.3) and buying drawn in by the news, the part that stays could have a lasting cause, such as a wider investor base (Section 10.3), and these data do not name the cause of either part (our judgment).

Tetlock measures the continuation after the freshest earnings news over the second to fifth trading days only (working-paper version), and a drift points to underweighting, a weight below what Bayes’ rule gives, only if it never reverses, since a drift followed by a reversal can be overweighting of an earlier signal that is still growing (our judgment). A test of the layers therefore has to follow the whole price response (our judgment).

Buying drawn in by the news is a third channel, and it too would push a price up and let it fall back (our judgment). In Tetlock’s data the reversal is larger where the story follows other news more closely: over the second to fifth trading days it is a cumulative 27 basis points, with a t-statistic of 4.79, where the firm had another story in the previous five trading days, and 18 basis points where the previous story came within 15 days, and it is completely gone, at −2 basis points and statistically insignificant, for stories that do not come soon after others (working-paper version). That dose response fits double counting and could also fit buying drawn in by attention, which may be heaviest where news comes in clusters, while busy news periods may differ in other ways too, so it does not single out one reading (our judgment).

Seeing news and failing to work it out is a different limit. Hong and Stein (1999) model two groups of boundedly rational agents, newswatchers and momentum traders, each of whom can only process a subset of all available information; if the momentum traders are restricted to simple strategies that use one variable, their best attempts at arbitrage, though profitable, must inevitably lead to overreaction at long horizons (working-paper version). What limits these agents is how much of the available information they can work out, a processing limit that leaves the capacity law of Section 3.5 as it is (our judgment). Chapter 20 takes up the momentum traders’ price rule.

Decisions can also come only now and then. Duffie (2010) writes that casual introspection and empirical evidence suggest that many investors make infrequent investment decisions, and that a simple explanation is that trading takes time away from valuable alternative activities. His illustrative model assumes that 80% of investors make trading decisions only once every 32 periods; after a supply shock the price must decline dramatically to give the relatively small mass of investors who are present enough incentive to absorb it, and it later climbs above its steady-state level before eventually converging. These investors know the state of the market and are not there to trade, so we place this limit at the point of action (our judgment). Chapter 19 takes up this attention cycle.9

10.8 Attention spread over many assets and messages

Cohen and Frazzini open their paper with one case. As of 2001, Callaway accounted for 50% of Coastcast’s total sales, and both firms traded on the New York Stock Exchange and had analyst coverage (working-paper version). Negative news in early June about Callaway’s future earnings did not affect Coastcast’s share price at all, although Callaway lost 30% of its market value in two days (working-paper version). A search of newswires and financial publications found no news about Coastcast in the two months after Callaway’s announcement, and only then did Coastcast’s price adjust; shorting Coastcast on the news of Callaway’s slowing demand would have returned 20% over those two months (working-paper version). The authors chose this case to show their point, and one case cannot show how often such a thing happens (our judgment).

The root model has a single exogenous fact (Chapter 5). With more than one thing to attend to, one message can bear on several assets, and many messages can compete for one person’s attention. Callaway’s news bore on Callaway and on Coastcast as well, and this book calls the assets that one message bears on its target asset set (our judgment); Section 10.2 gave the customers’ and the suppliers’ responses in Cohen and Frazzini’s sample.

To find where attention is short, the authors count the mutual funds that hold both firms in a given month, since managers who hold both are more likely to gather information about, or monitor more closely, both the customer and the supplier and the link between them (working-paper version). They expect news about related firms to reach prices faster where that count is high, and in value-weighted portfolios, in which larger firms count for more, trading suppliers on their customers’ returns earned 3.02% a month, with a t-statistic of 2.70, where few funds held both firms, and only 0.55% a month, not statistically different from zero, where many did (working-paper version). The gap between the two groups has a t-statistic of −1.98 in value-weighted portfolios, significant at the 5% level, and −1.44 in equal-weighted ones, in which every firm counts the same, a value that is not significant (working-paper version).

The authors read the gap as varying inattention (working-paper version). Section 1.6.5 reads the count, which measures common ownership, as the overlap between the capital that sees the customer’s news and the capital that prices the supplier, and Section 7.6 reads it as a network of holders; their tables cannot tell the three readings apart (our judgment). Chapter 19 takes up overlapping capital.

Many messages can also compete for one person’s attention, through reposting from memory in Weng et al.’s model or through Sims’s shared budget, a modeling assumption (our judgment; Sections 10.4 and 10.5). Views on a question that has no true answer, and how they rise and fall among many people, are the subject of opinion dynamics in Chapter 13.10

10.9 Measuring attention

Markets offer counts of investor attention, how much notice an asset draws, such as search volume and page views, which Section 5.4 named as possible stand-ins for how many people have heard. Before such a series is read as attention, two things need saying (our judgment).

The first is what the series counts. Daily page views and weekly searches measure attention per unit of time, a flow, which can count people arriving for the first time or people already following, each repeat visit counted again; only a count of newcomers matches a quantity of the root model, the rate at which people newly hear, and neither kind of count says how many people have heard (our judgment). The second is the series’ response to past prices: if a coin jumps on Monday, some of Tuesday’s searches come from people looking up why, and a reader who does not remove that response takes an echo of the price for attention (our judgment).

Garcia, Tessone, Mavrodiev and Perony (2014) fit a vector autoregression, a set of equations in which the previous values of several series predict each of them, with a lag of one day, to daily data from July 2010 to October 2013. Their four series are the Mt. Gox price, Google search volume, the number of downloads of the Bitcoin client and a tweet ratio, their measure of word of mouth. Downloads of the client, which the authors use as a proxy for the number of users, count new users, while search volume counts newcomers and people already following alike (our judgment). The authors describe two loops in their coefficients, in which search volume increases with the price, word of mouth with search volume and the price with word of mouth, or new users increase with search interest and the price with new users. Search volume also bears on the price directly, with a negative coefficient. Both loops start from the response of the day’s change in search to the previous day’s change in the price, the part that the second point above would remove (our judgment).

A loop gain measures how much of a move in the price comes back to it after one trip around such a loop, with zero meaning no feedback. Multiplying Garcia et al.’s point estimates around each loop gives loop gains of about 0.009 and 0.008, so less than 1% of a move comes back after one trip around either of them (our calculation). These products take the estimates as printed, without orthogonalization, the splitting of shocks that arrive together into uncorrelated parts, and the authors give each coefficient with a p-value and no standard error, so this book has not worked out how uncertain the products are (our judgment).

Adding up every path by which a shock to the price returns to it, over all later days, gives the long-run multiplier, the total change in the price after a shock to it, in units of the shock; we take the four equations of their Table 2 as one system. The preprint prints one cell, the effect of new users on word of mouth, as 8.201, more than ten times any other coefficient and far from significant, and for reasons the endnote gives we read it as a misprint and set it to 0 (our judgment). The system then gives a long-run multiplier of about 1.07 (our calculation). The price’s own lag, a coefficient of 0.153, alone would give about 1.18, so in Garcia et al.’s full sample the attention channels taken together lower the multiplier by about 0.11, about a tenth (our calculation). Within that, the positive paths through word of mouth and new users add about 0.04, about half of it through the two loops and about half through a shorter loop that the authors do not name, from the price to new users and back (our calculation), and the negative path from search to the price takes away more (our calculation).

Kristoufek (2013) uses weekly data from May 2011 to June 2013. Since he works with logarithmic differences, he writes, his responses can be read as proportional reactions to a 1% shock, and on that reading his weekly responses of search to the price and of the price to search give a loop gain of about 0.016 (our calculation). He gives no standard deviations of the innovations, the part of each week’s change that the past does not predict (our judgment). If his figure shows shocks of one standard deviation and those standard deviations are of order 0.1 to 0.3, his loop gain would lie between about 0.18 and 1.6, more than ten times larger, and a loop gain of 1 or more, taken alone, would mean feedback that does not die out (our derivation); Garcia et al.’s loop gains are products of coefficients, which do not change when each series is rescaled (our derivation). This book has not recomputed his estimate, which would need his Google Trends data, and Chapter 22 recomputes attention and price with free data (our judgment).

With Garcia et al.’s point estimates, and with the cell printed as 8.201 set to 0 because this book reads it as a misprint (our judgment), the positive feedback loops through attention and new users amplify a price shock by about 4% in their daily data (our calculation); on Kristoufek’s own unit-shock reading, his loop between search and the price amplifies it by about 1.6% in his weekly data (our calculation). Amplification of a few percent cannot by itself produce rises of orders of magnitude, but both studies fit linear models of daily or weekly changes, which cannot show feedback of other shapes, and Garcia et al.’s loops do not appear in their data up to November 28, 2012 (our judgment). The daily and weekly numbers come from different samples and cannot be compared directly (our judgment).11

Scope. Of Garcia et al.’s three periods, their two feedback loops hold only in the last, after November 28, 2012; the loops appear in the full sample and not in the data up to that date, and in those data, their first two periods taken together, search affected the price with the opposite sign. Kristoufek’s 0.016 follows only from reading his responses, as he does, as responses to a unit shock, and he does not say how he identified the shocks. His split into weeks with the price above and below its moving average uses the current price, so the groups switch as the price moves, and his results for the two groups may partly be price momentum.

How fast public news reaches a price is a second measure of attention. Section 5.5 described the CNBC data of 2000 that Busse and Green (2002) used. They found that prices of stocks discussed positively began to respond seconds after the first mention and kept responding for approximately one minute. For the 125 positive reports of the midday segment, a buy executed within the first five seconds after the report earned an average of 40 basis points, a buy within 15 seconds 16 basis points, beyond 15 seconds average returns were no longer statistically significant, and beyond 25 seconds they were negative, on mostly large firms (scope box below). We read this as an order of magnitude for how fast the gradient of public information is used up, with a time scale set by the delays of attention and of executing orders, and without word of mouth (our judgment). By the estimates the authors cite, an order that had reached the New York Stock Exchange took three seconds to arrive at the specialist’s display book and another two to appear on it, and trades took an additional 17 seconds on average to execute. Prices had risen before the reports aired, which this chapter takes only as a sign that the news had spread before it was public (our judgment).

Scope. The scope box of Section 5.5 gives the limits of these figures, and the opening of Chapter 24 repeats them for the window for profit: more than 80% of the reports were on firms in the top two NYSE size deciles, so the authors call their results an upper bound on the speed with which prices respond to information, and tick sizes and execution speeds in 2000 differed from today’s.

Attention can also move a price through who is in the market. Miller (1977) studies a market with restricted short selling in which investors have differing estimates of the returns from a risky security. With little or no short selling, he argues, the demand for a security comes from the minority who hold the most optimistic expectations about it, and he hypothesizes that anything that increases investor awareness of a stock will increase its price. He states this as a hypothesis, and the sources of this book include no test of buying driven by attention (our judgment). Chapter 19 sets this channel beside the others by which awareness reaches a price, and Chapter 20 takes up prices above the average belief under short-sale constraints.12

10.11 A trader’s checklist

None of these questions says what to buy or sell, and good answers do not make a trade safe (our judgment).

  1. Did the people who set the price see it? Check: look at the day and time of the announcement, since news that comes out when many investors are likely to be distracted may be only partly in the price at first (our judgment). In DellaVigna and Pollet’s US sample from 1995 to 2006, Friday announcements had a 15% lower immediate response and a 70% higher delayed response than those on other weekdays, and in their model the delayed share of the response gives only a lower bound on the share of distracted investors, whose size the model does not predict (working-paper version; Section 10.2). News about a supplier’s main customers may also reach the supplier’s price only slowly (our judgment): in Cohen and Frazzini’s customer data from 1980 to 2004, after a large monthly return of a firm’s main customers the supplier’s price covered about 60% of the initial gap in that month and the remaining 40% over the next six months (working-paper version; Sections 10.2 and 10.8).
  2. Where was it printed? Check: the same information can be used less when it sits in a footnote than when it appears in a headline or in reported earnings (our judgment). In Hirshleifer and Teoh’s model, if some investors have limited attention, leaving the cost of stock options out of expenses makes the market overvalue the firm (Section 10.3); Garvey and Milbourn found that the more option compensation a firm left unrecognized, the lower its later abnormal returns in 1996–2000 (as reported by Hirshleifer and Teoh).
  3. Is the quantity you track fixed or moving? Check: in Sims’s model, with normal quantities and the capacity fully used, a capacity limit only slows learning about a fixed fact (our derivation; Section 10.4). In the same model and under the same conditions, a quantity that takes a fresh shock each period keeps a floor of uncertainty, and the observer never fully catches up, provided the capacity outpaces the rate at which the quantity amplifies old errors, as any positive capacity does for a random walk (our derivation). Tracking more quantities leaves less capacity for each only if they share one budget, which is Sims’s modeling assumption (our judgment).
  4. Will others trade on it before you must sell? Check: in Froot, Scharfstein and Stein’s model, where each trader can study only one thing and must sell at a fixed date, all traders study the same thing when the value is announced only after that date, and each prefers what is not the main focus of the others’ study when it is sure to come before (as reported by Brunnermeier; Section 10.6). Whether your exit comes before the news is public is the question of Section 3.4.
  5. Is it news, or the same news again? Testable: compare the story with what ran before on the same firm. In Tetlock’s news on S&P 500 firms from 1984 to 2004, reactions to stale stories partially reversed in the next week, and reactions to the freshest earnings news continued over the second to fifth trading days (working-paper version; Section 10.7). The reversal fits double counting and does not fit observation gating with weights set by Bayes’ rule, and it also fits a fad and buying drawn in by the news, so these data do not name its cause (our judgment).
  6. What does your attention series count? Check: say whether it counts newcomers or people already following, and remove its response to past prices before reading it as attention (our judgment; Section 10.9). In Garcia et al.’s daily Bitcoin data, with their point estimates and one printed cell set to 0 because this book reads it as a misprint (our judgment), the positive feedback loops through attention and new users amplified a price shock by a few percent (our calculation; Section 10.9), and the loops do not appear in their data up to November 28, 2012. On Kristoufek’s own unit-shock reading his weekly loop between search and the price did the same (our calculation), and if his responses are to shocks of one standard deviation and his weekly innovations have standard deviations of order 0.1 to 0.3, his loop gain would be more than ten times larger (our derivation).

10.12 Summary

  • Dropping unlimited attention, the default that everyone sees and uses all the information there is at no cost, lets in the six forms of limited attention of this chapter, which limit different things and call for different checks (our judgment; Section 10.1).
  • In DellaVigna and Pollet’s US quarterly earnings announcements from 1995 to 2006, Friday announcements had a 15% lower immediate response and a 70% higher delayed response than those on other weekdays, and in their model the delayed share of the total response gives only a lower bound on the share of distracted investors (working-paper version; Section 10.2).
  • In Sims’s model, with normal quantities and the capacity fully used, a capacity limit only slows learning about a fixed fact; about a quantity that takes a fresh shock each period, it leaves a lasting floor of uncertainty and a delay, provided the capacity keeps ahead of the rate at which that quantity amplifies old errors, as any positive capacity does for a random walk (our derivation; Section 10.4).
  • The shape of a price reaction cannot tell news not seen from news seen and given the wrong weight, and telling them apart takes a reference signal, such as the same content released a second time (our judgment; Sections 10.2 and 10.7).
  • In Tetlock’s news on S&P 500 firms from 1984 to 2004, reactions to stale stories partially reversed in the next week, and reactions to the freshest earnings news continued over the second to fifth trading days (working-paper version; Section 10.7).
  • Search volume and page views can be read as attention only after one says whether they count newcomers or people already following and removes their response to past prices (our judgment; Section 10.9).
