Chapter 24 Empirical Map and a Trading-Edge Checkup

Busse and Green (2002) videotaped CNBC each trading day from June 12 to October 27, 2000, and recorded 322 stock reports over 84 trading days. Their window for profit uses only the positive reports of the Midday Call segment, 125 of them. They computed the return from buying at various times, from five minutes before to 30 seconds after a report, and selling between one and two minutes after it; to account for the bid–ask spread, the gap between the prices at which one can buy and sell, they took buy prices from buyer-initiated trades and sell prices from seller-initiated ones. A buy within the first five seconds after the report earned an average of 40 basis points, a basis point being 0.01%, and a buy within 15 seconds 16 basis points; beyond 15 seconds average returns were no longer statistically significant, and beyond 25 seconds they were negative.1

The scope box of Section 5.5 gives the limits of these figures, and this chapter keeps them: the window holds only for the 125 positive midday reports; more than 80% of all the reports were on firms in the top two NYSE size deciles, so the authors call their results an upper bound on the speed with which prices respond to information; and tick sizes and execution speeds in 2000 differed from today’s. We read the window, as Section 10.9 did, as an order of magnitude for how fast the gradient of public information is used up, on a time scale set by the delays of attention and of executing orders, without word of mouth (our judgment). This chapter judges no strategy, and nothing in it is advice to buy or sell (our judgment).

This last chapter asks which gradients the evidence shows and what keeps each alive (Section 24.1), and what a trader can check before she trusts an edge (Section 24.2).

The evidence of Section 24.1 drops, among others, the three defaults that Table 5.1 lists for this chapter: one-time injection, through scheduled announcements and broadcasts (the opening and Section 24.1.1); Bayesian updating, through wrong models of earnings, overconfidence and stale news (Sections 24.1.1, 24.1.4 and 24.1.5); and unlimited attention, through distraction, links between assets that go unseen and visibility (Sections 24.1.1 to 24.1.3). The other defaults that the chapter’s readings and models drop lie outside the relaxations that Table 5.1 lists for this chapter: one message bearing on two assets drops the single exogenous fact (Section 24.1.2); restricted short selling and trading costs drop frictionless trading (Sections 24.1.3, 24.2.2 and 24.2.3); predictable noise demand that reverses drops the exogenous counterparty (Section 24.1.5); and price impact drops the price taker, with Kyle’s risk-neutral insider in place of log utility (Section 24.2.1).

24.1 An empirical map of the information gradient

Section 1.3 split the information gradient, the information that the price has not yet reflected, into two parts: undiffused information, which has not yet reached those whose trading sets the price, and undigested information, which has reached them but which the price has not yet absorbed correctly. Section 1.8 left it to this chapter to sort the evidence by the two parts. Table 24.1 gives each item’s part, the mechanism that keeps it alive, with the chapter that gives it, and whether this book holds a quantitative benchmark for it, a number to be explained from separately measured inputs. Placing an item in a part is this book’s reading of its source: most items could have a share in both, and no source measures how much of its gap belongs to each (our judgment).

24.1.1 Earnings drift and the announcement calendar

Figure 24.1 draws, from the numbers they print, the time shape of Bernard and Thomas’s (1989) post-earnings-announcement drift, prices that keep moving for months in the direction of an earnings surprise, in NYSE and AMEX firms from 1974 to 1986 (Section 5.5, the opening of Chapter 12 and Sections 12.1 and 12.4).

The upper panel gives, for each 60-trading-day window after an announcement, the abnormal return, the return beyond a benchmark’s, of the highest less the lowest decile of SUE, standardized unexpected earnings, an autoregressive model’s forecast error in seasonally differenced earnings, scaled by its standard deviation, for small, medium and large firms, size deciles 1 to 4, 5 to 7 and 8 to 10 of all NYSE and AMEX firms by market value on January 1, against NYSE-AMEX firms of the same size decile. In each size group the window’s drift is largest in days 1 to 60 and smaller in each of the next three windows; in days 241 to 300 it is negative and significant in all three groups (Section 12.1); no later window is significant at the 5% level or better.

The lower panel gives two shares of the 60-day drift. Within 5 days of the announcement come 13%, 18% and 20% of it for small, medium and large firms, where a drift constant over the 60 days would put 8%; beside them, against NYSE firms of the same size decile, are the shares in the five days up to and including the next quarter’s announcement.

Read together, the panels show a drift that is largest at the start, shrinks over the first 240 days and turns significantly negative in days 241 to 300 (our judgment). The five days ending on the next announcement hold 25% to 40% of the 60-day drift, three to five times the 8% of a constant drift (our calculation). The authors conclude that a disproportionately large fraction of the drift is concentrated in the few days preceding and including the next quarter’s announcement, a cluster that the calendar of announcements times (our judgment). Word of mouth in the root model gives an S-curve, slow, then fast, then slow (Section 1.5); a strong external source there would also make the drift largest at the start, if the price rule of Section 1.5 holds (our inference; Section 1.9), and neither clusters at an announcement. On the evidence of the next-announcement window, the calendar sets the shape in part, and the drift outside that window keeps this book from saying that it sets all of it (our judgment; Section 12.4).

On the part of the gradient, Bernard and Thomas find the results of their Table 5 consistent with a market that fails to recognize the full implications of current earnings for future earnings, and they call the drift’s apparent upper bound their only result that is not consistent with incomplete updating of earnings expectations. Earnings, in their words, are information “widely disseminated and freely available”, so on their reading the drift is undigested (our judgment). DellaVigna and Pollet read the extra delay after Friday announcements as distraction (working-paper version; Section 10.2), and news that distracted investors have not seen is undiffused (Section 1.8); neither source measures how the drift divides between the two readings (our judgment).

Two tests could check accounts of the drift from separately measured inputs, and this book has run neither (our judgment). One, a test of the wrong model, would predict the return at the next announcement from the serial correlation of seasonally differenced quarterly earnings, measured from earnings alone, and the price response per unit of surprise, measured on announcement days alone, for comparison with Table 5 (our derivation; Section 12.5). The other would bound the drift by separately measured round-trip costs (Section 23.4.2). Neither backs its inputs out of the drift, and until one is run this book prints no number of its own on the timing.3

24.1.2 Customer news and supplier prices

In Cohen and Frazzini’s (2008) customer data, from 1980 to 2004, suppliers’ prices take months to follow large moves in their customers’ prices (working-paper version; Sections 10.2 and 10.8). Their customer momentum strategy is long the top 20% good customer news stocks and short the bottom 20% bad customer news stocks (working-paper version). Although abnormal returns are large and significant for both legs, they write, the returns are asymmetric, the long-short returns being largely driven by slow diffusion of negative news (working-paper version). In their Table III, with value weights, the three-factor alphas, returns left after allowing for three common risk factors, of the five quintiles of customer returns, from lowest to highest, are −1.062, −0.796, −0.541, −0.227 and 0.493% a month, and that of the long-short portfolio 1.555%, for stocks with a closing price of at least $5 at the end of the previous month (working-paper version).

The three middle quintiles are negative too (working-paper version). Measured from zero, the short leg, 1.062, is larger than the long leg, 0.493; measured from the middle quintile, the long leg, 1.034, is larger than the short leg, 0.521 (our calculation), all computed from the working paper’s alphas (working-paper version). With equal weights the same alphas are −1.166, −0.661, −0.446, −0.304 and 0.140%, and the short leg is larger by both measures, 1.166 against 0.140 from zero and 0.720 against 0.586 from the middle quintile, Q5’s alpha, the long leg measured from zero, having a t-statistic of 0.71 (working-paper version). The authors write that equal weights deliver similar results (working-paper version).

The customer’s news is already in the customer’s price and has not yet reached those who price the supplier, so this book places the item in the undiffused part, though the tables cannot rule out holders who saw the news and gave it too little weight (our judgment; Section 10.2); Sections 10.8 and 19.3.5 gave the mechanism, attention spread over the assets one message bears on, and, in this book’s reading, the shared holders who set the speed. In the value-weighted alphas (working-paper version) the leg that carries most of the return depends on the benchmark, so they give no clean evidence that bad news travels more slowly; the equal-weighted ones, whose short leg carries most by both measures, agree with the authors’ asymmetry, and the table as a whole does not settle it (our judgment).4

24.1.3 Attention, visibility and neglected stocks

The map’s measurement of attention is DellaVigna and Pollet’s extra delay after Friday announcements, in US quarterly earnings from January 1995 to June 2006, with its scope box in Section 10.2 (working-paper version). Miller (1977), in a market with restricted short selling in which investors differ in their estimates of returns, hypothesizes that anything that increases investor awareness of a stock will increase its price, through demand from the minority who hold the most optimistic expectations. Attention there acts on the price through who can buy, and this book’s sources do not test the hypothesis, so Table 24.1 holds it as a hypothesis only and puts the channel in neither part of the gradient (our judgment; Section 19.3.6).

Neglected stocks, those that few investors know or few analysts follow, carry two effects, one on the level of prices and one on their path, which a test has to separate (our judgment; Section 19.3.6). The level effect is Merton’s (1987), in this book’s plain words: only those who know a security bear its firm-specific risk, so the fewer they are, the higher the return they require (our judgment; Section 19.3.2), in a model that takes the share who know as given (working-paper version). The dynamic effect is Hong, Lim and Stein’s: in their data from 1980 to 1996, excluding stocks below the 20th percentile NYSE/AMEX size breakpoint, momentum strategies work particularly well among stocks with low analyst coverage, holding size fixed, which they read within a theory of gradual information diffusion (working-paper version; Section 20.2.4). The level effect is compensation for sharing risk among those who know, part of the risk-premium term in the gradient’s second part (Sections 18.6, 18.7 and 19.3.2), and the dynamic effect is undiffused information (our judgment).5

24.1.4 Drift after selected events

In Daniel, Hirshleifer and Subrahmanyam’s (1998) model of overconfident investors (Section 12.5), a selective event is one whose occurrence or size depends on the mispricing at the time, such as a repurchase when the stock is undervalued (Section 15.2.3). For such events, they write, drift results from the combination of underreaction and event selection based on market mispricing; with a fixed confidence level, events unrelated to the mispricing bring no systematic drift, while in the version in which confidence rises and falls with outcomes even those events bring predictability (Section 12.5). Underreaction and selection make the drift together, so the map does not list them as two sources (our judgment).

A test of drift after events therefore has to say how it groups them: by the realized event, such as Bernard and Thomas’s surprise against the firm’s own past earnings, by the surprise against the market’s expectation, or by the return on the event day; the three groupings ask different questions (our judgment). These are results of a model, which this book has not tested (Section 12.5). The underreaction to a public signal is undigested information, and the selection comes from senders who know the mispricing, such as managers who choose when to repurchase (our judgment; Section 15.2.3).6

24.1.5 Stale news, sentiment and noise demand

Sections 10.7, 13.11 and 23.6.2 gave Tetlock’s two studies: in news about S&P 500 firms from 1984 to 2004, reactions to stale news, news carrying much information already reported, partly reversed in the next week, and he leaves open whether the reversal survives trading costs; in a Wall Street Journal column and the Dow from 1984 to 1999, the impact of negative sentiment on the Dow is fully reversed within a week (working-paper version).

Stale news is old news counted again, undigested information (our judgment; Section 10.7). Sentiment, optimism or pessimism that is unconnected with news about fundamentals and moves demand, changes no one’s knowledge of θ\theta, the fact a price is about, so it lies outside the gradient about that fact; for a trader it is information about future noise demand, trade for reasons other than information (our judgment; Section 13.11).

Whether a move reverses is the test, but a reversal shows only that the move was temporary and does not by itself tell sentiment from double counting, from buying drawn in by attention or from a passing change in risk aversion (our judgment; Section 13.11). Reading a reversal needs averages over many events, horizons long enough to see it, and other news in the same window ruled out (our judgment).7

24.1.6 Forum research posts

Section 15.4 gave Bradley et al.’s (2024) due diligence reports on WallStreetBets, 2,340 reports issued between 2018 and 2020, a cut-off that puts all their analysis before the frenzied returns of GameStop and other meme stocks, and returns after their buy recommendations that did not reverse (working-paper version). In this book’s reading of that sample the buy reports carry information about value (our judgment; Table 15.2), but whether a report spreads facts others had not seen or an analysis of public data, the source does not separate, so Table 24.1 marks its part as not shown (our judgment).8

24.1.7 Quotes, clock time and the spread’s bias

In Easley and O’Hara’s (1992) model, quotes, and thus transaction prices, converge to their strong-form efficient values at exponential rates in clock time, time measured by the clock, and transaction prices are a biased record of the true price process; with only price data to follow, quotes may be the better data set (Section 23.4.3). This book’s rule for measuring speed or decay, the method that Section 23.4.3 left to this chapter, is to use quote midpoints, halfway between bid and ask, timed by the clock from the moment the news was released; event time in trades, or in trading days as Bernard and Thomas count it, suits a drift of months and cannot time a response of minutes (our judgment).

Glosten and Milgrom (1985) argue that in a variation of their model in which the uninformed traders expect a fixed positive return, the existence of a spread implies that, over any period, measured returns based on transaction prices tend to overstate the returns that a liquidity trader can actually realize: the initial price is on average lower than the ask he must pay, and the final price on average higher than the bid he could expect to receive. The measured return over a normal holding period is then approximately a normal return plus a return available only to insiders, the part the uninformed expect to lose to the informed (Section 18.7). So the spread arising from adverse selection, the risk of trading with someone better informed, provides in their words “a possible route” to explaining the small-firm effect and the ignored firm effect, in this book’s plain words higher measured returns on small firms’ stocks and on stocks that few follow.

In a footnote they add Blume and Stambaugh’s (1983) result that the spread can bias measured expected returns upward through the mismeasurement of true prices (as reported by Glosten and Milgrom).

Two things follow for measurement (our judgment). Blume and Stambaugh’s mismeasurement of true prices is a bias in the data, which quote midpoints remove. Glosten and Milgrom’s gap stays in returns measured from transaction prices, midpoints or values, however accurate, and goes only from returns computed at the prices a liquidity trader actually pays and receives, the ask on buying and the bid on selling, which in their variation are normal. Both are larger where spreads are wide, as in small firms (our judgment). Returns in Table 24.1’s studies that come from trade prices, in small or illiquid stocks above all, may carry either (our judgment).9

24.2 A trading-edge checkup

The rules of this section, each with the premises it needs, examine the structure of an edge; none of them says what to buy or sell, and good answers do not make a trade safe (our judgment; Section 1.9).

24.2.1 Market depth and capacity

How much of an edge can show in profit depends on the depth of the market (our judgment). In Kyle’s (1985) model a single risk-neutral insider, whose information is given from outside, hides behind noise traders. Market depth, the order flow needed to move the price by one dollar, is proportional to the amount of noise trading and inversely proportional to the private information not yet in prices, and the insider’s expected profits grow in proportion to the noise trading he can hide in, while the share of his information that reaches the price does not depend on it (Section 4.6).

This book calls capacity the trade unrelated to information that an informed trader can hide in, and strategy capacity the size at which an edge can be traded before its costs use it up. In Kyle’s model both grow with noise trading (our inference; Section 1.9), so this book treats them as one quantity seen from two sides (our judgment). The rule: an edge measured at a small size says nothing about how much of it is left at a large one, and the first question is how deep the market for that stock is (our judgment).10

24.2.2 Capacity converted across stocks

In this book’s derivation (Box 24.1), Kyle and Obizhaeva’s (2016) invariance carries such a size from one stock to another. Their hypotheses say that the distributions of risk transfers, which they call bets, and of transaction costs are the same across assets when measured per unit of business time, which passes at the rate at which bets arrive, and their trading activity, the product of dollar volume and returns volatility, differs by a factor of 150 between their lowest and highest groups (the opening of Chapter 17). Percentage transaction costs are then proportional to the product of an illiquidity measure, the cube root of returns variance over dollar volume, and some invariant function of bet size, scaled to convert it into invariant dollar risk transfer. Invariance does not restrict the shape of this function, which can be linear or square-root.

Capacity is measured here as dollar risk, the dollar size of the best order times daily volatility; divided by daily volatility, it gives dollars. If the edge per dollar traded, net of the spread term and in units of daily volatility, is the same in two stocks, capacity grows as the two-thirds power of trading activity under linear impact and in proportion to it under the square root (our derivation). For activity 150 times as large, capacity is about 28 times as large under linear impact and 150 times under the square root (our calculation). Both a linear and a square-root model explain costs well (Section 17.8), so the exponent is uncertain by that much (our judgment).

The conversion needs every one of these premises (our judgment): both invariance hypotheses, with the multipliers that link volume and volatility to bets the same in every stock; one of the two calibrated cost shapes; the edge, net of the spread term, the same in units of daily volatility in both stocks; the order executed as one bet, with no other bets of one’s own; and costs as measured on portfolio-transition orders, which the authors think unlikely to rest on short-lived private information, so an informed order may pay more, and Box 17.4’s λ\lambda, Kyle’s price move per unit of order flow, which treats every bet as noise, is a lower bound that awaits checking (our derivation; Section 17.8). Invariance itself holds only approximately: the authors’ statistical tests usually reject it, though the results are economically close, and because of market frictions they do not expect it to hold exactly across all assets and all times. The scope box at the opening of Chapter 17 gives the tests’ limits, US stocks traded in portfolio transitions from 2001 to 2005, with the constants fitted to the same orders.11

24.2.3 Three tests of an execution-cost model

Section 3.7, Box 3.2 and Section 17.7 gave Huberman and Stanzl’s (2004) conditions. Their trader has no better information about the asset’s value than others, can trade any amount he wishes, and faces others’ net trades that are zero or normally distributed, with public news and others’ orders independent and of zero mean (working-paper version). Section 3.7 defined price manipulation and quasi-arbitrage in their technical senses, round trips with positive expected profit and sequences of them whose expected profit and Sharpe ratio, expected profit over its standard deviation, grow without limit; this book uses the conditions only to test models of impact.

The first test is that permanent impact be linear. When the price impact of trades is permanent and the same at all times, only linear price-impact functions rule out quasi-arbitrage: their Proposition 1 requires a permanent impact linear with non-negative slope to rule out quasi-arbitrage, and to rule out manipulation when trades come close enough together (working-paper version; Box 3.2).

The second test is that the spread not be smaller than permanent impact. If there is no price manipulation, or no unbounded manipulation, and the fixed costs of kk trades are proportional to kak^a with a<1a < 1, their Proposition 2 gives two conditions that must hold when trades come close enough together (working-paper version). The authors read the first as saying that the expected spread at any trade size has to exceed the expected price update resulting from that trade (working-paper version). They add that the absence of quasi-arbitrage in general does not imply these conditions (working-paper version), so this test rests on the absence of manipulation and on the cost premise.

The third test is that cross-impacts be symmetric. With several assets, the absence of price manipulation is equivalent to an impact function that is linear and represented by a positive semidefinite matrix, so that every cross-price impact, the move in one asset’s price from trading another, is linear and pairwise symmetric: the impact of asset ii on asset jj is the same as that of jj on ii (working-paper version). A positive semidefinite matrix is one under which no combination of trades moves prices in its favor on balance, each asset’s price move weighted by the amount traded in it (our derivation). This holds where the authors, adjusting their single-asset Proposition 4, whose premises include normally distributed orders of others (Box 3.2), impose no temporary impact as a sufficient condition in place of its first condition (working-paper version).

When trades also have a temporary impact, the part of a move that later reverses, only the permanent impact must be linear, and the temporary one can take a more general form (working-paper version). A well-fitting square-root cost (Section 24.2.2) therefore agrees with the first test only when it is read as temporary impact, with the permanent part still linear (our judgment). Where estimates detect a permanent impact that departs from linearity, Huberman and Stanzl write, one may wonder why some price manipulation possibilities had gone unexploited, or suspect some misspecification, perhaps a time-dependent environment (working-paper version), or the immediate impact taken for the permanent one (Section 17.7).12

24.2.4 Time for an edge to show

Section 1.4 noted that a short record cannot tell skill from luck and left to this chapter how long a record must be. Its trader puts 0.60 on a contract priced at 0.30 and splits all her capital in line with that belief; when her belief is the truth she gains about 0.28 bits a bet, yet after 10 independent bets her chance of being behind is about one in six (our calculation).

The clock for an edge is the number of independent realizations of what it bets on, and trading the same outcome more often adds none (our judgment; Section 18.4). For a trader who bets all her capital in line with her belief, with a separate bet on every outcome at margin-free odds, each realization adds on average G=D(r∥q)−D(r∥p)G = D(r \| q) - D(r \| p) bits of log growth, the price’s relative entropy from the truth less one’s own, so a wrong belief costs D(r∥p)D(r \| p) a realization (Section 1.4); a record’s mean log growth per realization estimates it. For that mean to stand tt standard errors above zero takes about (t⋅sd/G)2(t \cdot \mathrm{sd}/G)^2 realizations, sd being the standard deviation of one realization’s log growth (our derivation; Box 24.2).

By the exact binomial count, the chance that Chapter 1’s trader is behind, when her belief is the truth, stays below 5% from 28 bets on (our calculation; Box 24.2). The count assumes the other conditions of Box 1.1: bets repeated independently, winnings reinvested, prices unmoved by her orders and a known joint distribution of signal and outcome. In the example the truth is known; a real record must also estimate the mean and its spread, which leaves the count of the same order (our judgment).13

24.2.5 One chance and no reinvestment

Kelly (1956) names the essential requirements of his theory as the possibility of reinvesting profits and the ability to control or vary the amount invested. The logarithm matters because it is additive in repeated bets and the law of large numbers applies to it; a gambler allowed to bet a dollar a week but not to reinvest his winnings should instead maximize his expected capital on each bet (Box 1.1; Section 4.2). Kelly also proved his gambler’s advantage only against gamblers who split their money in a fixed way.

The rule: the value of an edge that can be used once, or whose winnings cannot be reinvested, is not its number of bits, and reading a growth rate as the value of a one-off bet requires log utility (our judgment; Box 1.1 and Section 4.2). Section 1.4’s trader at a truth of 0.40 shows the other side: her direction is right, yet with winnings reinvested her capital shrinks in log terms, about 0.08 bits a bet, because she bets too much for the edge she has, although in a single bet her expected capital would rise (our calculation; Section 1.4).14

24.2.6 In-sample results discounted first

The rule: an in-sample result, one measured on the data used to find it, needs a discount before it is trusted (our judgment). McLean and Pontiff’s (2016) 26% fall in the average predictor’s return after the original samples ended, by the paper’s regression, for 97 published predictors of US-listed stocks in data ending in 2013, is an upper bound on the effect of statistical biases, since some traders likely learned of a predictor before publication, and the spread across predictors is wide, so the average does not tell what any one predictor earned (our judgment; the opening of Chapter 23). A backtest of one’s own, never tested out of sample, carries no such bound (our judgment; Section 1.9). Section 1.9 also gives, with the conditions of Box 1.1 and its cautions, a bound on the extra growth a signal can give; a backtest that beats a robust estimate of it points to look-ahead bias or overfitting (our inference).15

24.2.7 Maker and taker sides around releases

Section 17.10 gave the Kalshi data of Bürgi, Deng and Whelan (2026), a prediction market in which makers make limit orders and takers market orders, and makers’ average return after fees in contracts from 2021 to April 2025 traded within 10 days of closing was −11.99% against takers’ −31.46% (working-paper version), with the scope that Sections 1.3 and 18.3 give. Before their own evidence, and after Linnainmaa’s Helsinki finding (Section 17.10), they call it an open question whether makers or takers perform better on a market like Kalshi that revolves around reacting to news (working-paper version).

Section 17.10 proposed a test of which side a trader is on, which this book does not offer as trading advice. It aligns one’s own fills on the release times of the news they trade around, by the clock, splits them into fills of one’s posted orders and of one’s market orders, and measures each fill’s return from its price to the quote midpoint some fixed time later, or to settlement, net of fees. If one’s posted orders lose systematically in the moments after releases, better-informed traders may be taking them, and at those moments one may sit on the uninformed side (our judgment). Bürgi, Deng and Whelan’s averages are taken over contracts and prices without alignment on release times, so they cannot answer this question (our judgment).

The test needs a record of one’s own fills with time stamps and roles, enough releases to average over and returns from quotes in clock time (Section 24.1.7); and makers’ returns include the return they require and the fees they pay (Section 17.10), so a gap between returns is not by itself information (our judgment).16

24.2.8 The window for profit after public news

The opening’s window for profit is the checkup’s item on execution speed (our judgment). It sets one’s own delay, from the release of the news to one’s fill, against how long the window lasted in such data, seconds in Busse and Green’s; Section 10.9 gave the times they cite for routing and executing orders.

Prices had risen before the reports aired, which the authors read as consistent with some market participants’ awareness of the information before the segment aired, so the moment of a broadcast need not be the moment its news began to spread (our judgment). The window holds only within the limits that the opening gives, and this book does not offer it as a strategy.17

24.4 A trader’s checklist

These questions extend the six of Section 1.9. None of them says what to buy or sell, and good answers do not make a trade safe (our judgment).

  1. Which part of the gradient, and what keeps it alive? Check: find the edge’s item in Table 24.1, its part and its mechanism, and keep in mind that most items could have a share in both parts, which no source splits (our judgment; Section 24.1).
  2. Was it measured on quotes in clock time, and out of sample? Check: quote midpoints remove the mismeasurement of true prices, but realizable returns need the prices one trades at, the ask to buy and the bid to sell, since in Glosten and Milgrom’s variation in which the uninformed expect a fixed positive return, returns from any other price series hold a part available only to insiders (our judgment; Section 24.1.7); and discount a backtest never tested out of sample (Section 24.2.6).
  3. How large can it be traded, here and in another stock? Check: ask how deep the market is for that stock, and carry a size to another stock only under all the premises of Section 24.2.2, with capacity counted as dollar risk (our judgment; Sections 24.2.1 and 24.2.2).
  4. Does the cost model pass three tests? Check: ask whether its permanent impact is linear with non-negative slope, its spread at least its permanent impact and its cross-impacts symmetric, each under Huberman and Stanzl’s premises (working-paper version); a square-root cost passes the first test only when read as temporary impact (our judgment; Section 24.2.3).
  5. How many independent outcomes has the record seen, and can winnings be reinvested? Check: count realizations of what the edge bets on, and do not read the value of a one-off edge, or of one whose winnings cannot be reinvested, as its number of bits (our judgment; Sections 24.2.4 and 24.2.5).
  6. Which side are your fills on around releases, and how fast are they? Check: used only as a test, which this book does not offer as trading advice, compare the returns of one’s posted and market orders aligned on release times by the clock, from quotes and net of fees, keeping in mind that makers’ returns also include the return they require; and set one’s delay against the window for profit within the limits of Section 24.2.8 (our judgment; Sections 24.2.7 and 24.2.8).

24.5 Summary

  • In Bernard and Thomas’s NYSE and AMEX firms from 1974 to 1986, the drift per 60-day window is largest at first, shrinks through the first 240 days and turns significantly negative in days 241 to 300, and the five days ending on the next announcement hold 25% to 40% of the 60-day drift against the 8% of a constant drift, so the calendar of announcements sets the time shape in part, unlike word of mouth’s S-curve, and this book has tested no account of the timing (our judgment; Section 24.1.1).
  • In the value-weighted three-factor alphas of Cohen and Frazzini’s working draft, customer data from 1980 to 2004 and stocks priced at least $5, the short leg of customer momentum is larger measured from zero and the long leg measured from the middle quintile, while with equal weights the short leg is larger by both measures (our calculation; working-paper version), so the value-weighted alphas give no clean evidence that bad news travels more slowly (our judgment; Section 24.1.2).
  • Quote midpoints remove the mismeasurement of true prices found by Blume and Stambaugh (as reported by Glosten and Milgrom), but in the variation of Glosten and Milgrom’s model in which the uninformed expect a fixed positive return, returns from any price series exceed what a liquidity trader can realize by about a return available only to insiders, which goes only from returns at the ask on buying and the bid on selling, and Glosten and Milgrom offer it only as a possible route to the small-firm effect (our judgment; Section 24.1.7).
  • Under both invariance hypotheses with the same multipliers in every stock, one of the two cost shapes, the same edge net of the spread term in units of daily volatility, one bet with no others of one’s own, and costs as measured on portfolio-transition orders, which the authors think unlikely to rest on short-lived private information, strategy capacity measured as dollar risk, which divided by daily volatility gives dollars, grows as the two-thirds power of trading activity under linear impact and in proportion to it under the square root (our derivation); invariance holds only approximately, so the exponents are approximate too (our judgment; Section 24.2.2).
  • An edge shows over independent realizations of what it bets on: Chapter 1’s trader, betting all her capital in line with a belief of 0.60 that is the truth, on a contract priced at 0.30, with a separate bet on each outcome at margin-free odds, bets repeated independently, winnings reinvested, prices unmoved and a known distribution, needs 28 bets before her chance of being behind stays below 5% (our calculation), and the value of an edge that comes once or cannot be reinvested is not its number of bits (our judgment; Sections 24.2.4 and 24.2.5).

24.7 Boxes for quantitative readers

Box 24.1 Capacity across stocks under invariance

This optional box is this book’s derivation, except where it cites Kyle and Obizhaeva (our derivation). Let an order be a share xx of a stock’s daily volume VV, at price PP, with daily volatility σ\sigma and trading activity W=σPVW = \sigma P V, and measure costs and the edge per dollar traded in units of σ\sigma. Their cost is the product of an illiquidity measure and an invariant function of bet size, and average bet size, as a share of volume, is inversely proportional to the two-thirds power of activity. The measure, (σ2/PV)1/3(\sigma^2/PV)^{1/3}, is σW−1/3\sigma W^{-1/3}, so in units of σ\sigma the cost is

c(x)=W−1/3[s+a(W2/3x)z],c(x) = W^{-1/3}\left[s + a\,(W^{2/3}x)^{z}\right],

with the spread term ss and the impact coefficient aa the same in every stock, z=1z = 1 for linear impact and z=1/2z = 1/2 for the square root. With an edge kk, net of the spread term k′=k−sW−1/3k' = k - s W^{-1/3}, the expected profit in dollars, xW[k′−aW−1/3(W2/3x)z]xW\left[k' - aW^{-1/3}(W^{2/3}x)^{z}\right], is largest at x*=[k′/((1+z)a)]1/zW(1−2z)/(3z)x^* = [k'/((1+z)a)]^{1/z}\, W^{(1-2z)/(3z)}. Capacity measured as dollar risk is x*Wx^* W, proportional to W(1+z)/(3z)W^{(1+z)/(3z)}: W2/3W^{2/3} for linear impact and WW for the square root, in line with Kyle and Obizhaeva’s remark on the square root; it grows as k′1/zk'^{1/z}, and dividing it by σ\sigma gives dollars. The break-even size, at which the edge net of costs is zero, is (1+z)1/z(1+z)^{1/z} times x*x^*, so its ratios across stocks are the same. For activity 150 times as large, capacity is about 28 times as large under linear impact and 150 times under the square root, and x*x^* is about 5.3 times smaller and unchanged (our calculation). The spread term differs across stocks, so an equal k′k' is a condition of the comparison, and the box draws nothing else from it.18

Box 24.2 Bets before an edge shows

Chapter 1’s trader holds belief pp on a contract priced qq and splits all her capital in line with her belief, so a success multiplies it by p/qp/q and a failure by (1−p)/(1−q)(1-p)/(1-q). With truth rr her expected growth is G=D(r∥q)−D(r∥p)G = D(r \| q) - D(r \| p) bits a bet (Section 1.4), and the standard deviation of one bet’s growth is

sd=|log⁡2pq−log⁡21−p1−q|r(1−r)\mathrm{sd} = \left|\log_2\frac{p}{q} - \log_2\frac{1-p}{1-q}\right|\sqrt{r(1-r)}

(our derivation). After nn independent bets with mm successes she is behind when mlog⁡2(p/q)+(n−m)log⁡2((1−p)/(1−q))<0m \log_2(p/q) + (n-m)\log_2((1-p)/(1-q)) < 0, an event with a binomial probability. For p=r=0.6p = r = 0.6 and q=0.3q = 0.3, G≈0.28G \approx 0.28 and sd≈0.89\mathrm{sd} \approx 0.89; the chance of being behind is about 0.166 after 10 bets, stays below 5% from 28 bets on and below 1% from 57 bets on (our calculation). It does not fall smoothly, being higher after 25 bets than after 20, because successes come in whole numbers. By the normal approximation the mean growth stands tt standard errors above zero after about (tsd/G)2(t\,\mathrm{sd}/G)^2 bets (our derivation): 27.6 for t=1.645t = 1.645 and 55.3 for t=2.326t = 2.326 (our calculation).
