Chapter 3 Five Constraint Laws

Joel Hasbrouck’s (1991) estimates for 80 stocks listed on the New York and American stock exchanges in the first quarter of 1989 suggest that large trades are associated with a widening of the bid–ask spread, the gap between the prices at which a dealer will sell and buy a stock at the same moment, and that trades made when the spread is relatively wide have a larger price impact, that is, move the price further. He finds the widening consistent with a dealer who infers from a large trade that new information has more likely arrived. We read it as a trade becoming a signal to those who see it, a case of one of the five constraint laws that every model in the book obeys (our judgment).

3.1 A large order and a wider spread

Hasbrouck studied a specialist market, in which a designated market maker, a dealer who takes the other side of orders, posts a bid at which it will buy and an ask at which it will sell. His data are a sequenced record of trades and quotes from the New York, American and regional exchanges over the 62 trading days of the first quarter of 1989, for 80 stocks listed on the first two.1

He treated trades and quote revisions as a vector autoregression, equations that predict several series from the past values of all of them, counting time in transactions, and measured a trade’s information effect as the final impact on the quote of its unexpected part, the part that past trades and quote revisions did not predict.

In his equation for the spread of his representative stock, Ames Department Stores (ADD), a test of all the trade terms together rejects the hypothesis that trades leave the spread unchanged at a level better than 0.001. Across the sample, the data reject that hypothesis at the 0.05 level for 77 of the 80 firms, though the 80 tests are not independent (our judgment). For ADD alone, he traces the spread his model implies after purchases of 1,000, 10,000 and 50,000 shares: “For the largest trade size, the spread temporarily widens”. He plots that widening for one stock; for the full sample he reports that test.

Hasbrouck expects trades made when the spread is high to move the price more, since information asymmetry, the gap between what the two sides of a trade know, raises both spread and impact. For ADD, the added impact of a wider spread has the same sign as that of trades alone, with statistical significance substantially lower than theirs; across the sample, the hypothesis that the spread makes no difference to a trade’s impact is rejected at the 0.05 level in 76 of the 80 cases. Posted New York Stock Exchange spreads were then often 1/8 or 1/4 point, a point being a dollar a share. For ADD, his model implies that a purchase of 10,000 shares revises the quote by $0.026 in all through the 20th transaction when the spread is 1/8, and by $0.058 when it is 1/4.

His reading draws on a model by Easley and O’Hara (1987), in which the market maker is unsure whether a trader might be informed and whether such a trader has in fact received a signal. In one equilibrium of their model, informed traders make large trades, so after a large trade the market maker and others think it more likely that news worth trading on has arrived, an information event in their terms, and “this may reasonably lead the market-maker to widen the spread”.

With one quarter of 1989, 80 stocks on two exchanges, a designated market maker and time counted in trades, the results do not carry over directly to today’s markets (our judgment). The spread also reflects trading costs and the dealer’s monopoly power, which the spread alone gives no obvious way to separate from the effect of asymmetric information. Inventory control, a dealer moving quotes to undo an unwanted position, offers a rival reading of the dealer’s response (our judgment); its price effects are transient and those of information permanent, so persistence, Hasbrouck (1988) suggests, only partly separates the two. Hasbrouck’s abstract says his estimates “suggest” these findings, he calls his account of how impact varies with trade size tentative, and where a trade’s impact reverses over the long run, he warns, a model with few lags is likely to overstate the long-run impact.

We read the large trade as a signal to the market maker who saw it, and so as one example of the price endogeneity law, taken up in Section 3.6 (our judgment).

3.2 Constraint laws and their premises

The book’s statements are of three kinds. The constraint laws are five rules that every model in the book obeys (Section 1.7). The first three, the observation law, the non-rivalry law and the capacity law, concern how people get and pass on information; the price endogeneity law and the no-arbitrage law concern prices. Default assumptions are settings that the book’s simplest model adopts all together and later chapters drop one or more at a time (Chapter 5). Results that rest on both the laws and the defaults, such as the value of information (Chapter 4), come later.

A model in which someone knows something without having observed it, or earns money from publicly available information alone without risk and without capital, is one the book counts as wrong (our judgment). Empirical studies test particular models that use these rules, while the laws themselves come from modeling conventions and from theorems (our judgment). The no-arbitrage law rests on a theorem of Harrison and Kreps (1979) that assumes, among other things, frictionless markets, with no trading costs and no limit on short sales (selling borrowed shares), and it changes form when such premises change (Section 3.7).2

Conservation of energy constrains every heat engine without saying which engine runs well, and the constraint laws do the same for the book’s models (our analogy). The analogy fails on evidence. A physical law has passed many precise measurements and holds throughout its range, while the constraint laws are the book’s conventions, and in real markets with frictions the no-arbitrage law holds only in a weakened form (our judgment).

3.3 The observation law

The observation law says that what a person newly knows can come only from observation, though people can forget. News that should have come and did not is itself an observation. An observation, in this book, is anything a person sees: news, other people’s actions and prices. A person’s information set is what she knows at a given moment; forgetting shrinks it, which the law allows.

The law constrains information sets and does not constrain prices. It rules out models in which someone knows what she never saw, and no price path breaks it by itself (our judgment). A price can move for reasons other than news about the state of the world θ\theta, the fact traders want to know, and a replay of stale news, made public some time before, can move it without breaking the law. For EntreMed (Section 1.1), the New York Times story of May 3, 1998, carried virtually the same information as the paper’s November report, much more prominently, and EntreMed’s stock, ENMD, rose from a Friday close of $12.063 to a Monday close of $51.81, or 330%. The story added expert comment, its most enthusiastic line attributed to James Watson, who was later reported to have denied it; what it repeated was the science (our judgment). Under the law, whatever Monday’s buyers newly knew came from what they saw, such as the story, the price and other traders’ orders. The law neither explains the rise nor judges it; an explanation has to look at what the buyers saw (our judgment). Huberman and Regev (2001) offer their reading tentatively, writing that it “seems” the market underreacted to the hard news of November and overreacted to the publicity of May; their Figure 1 gives “the distinct impression” that some of the rise was temporary and a substantial portion permanent. Tetlock (2011) finds that reactions to stale news about S&P 500 firms from 1984 to 2004 partially reverse in the next week (working-paper version; Section 1.8 gives the size).3

The law acts on what a person has seen. Publicly available information, which anyone could look up, need not have been seen (Section 1.6.5). Attention may decide whether a person sees public news at all, which the book calls observation gating. Repetition, salience (how prominently news is presented) and tone instead set the weight given to what is seen, which belongs to a person’s processing rule, the rule that turns an information set into beliefs. Both satisfy the law; the data decide which is at work (Chapter 10).

The law makes a quiet tape, a stretch with no news or no trades, an observation, and leaves what it says to the model. In Easley and O’Hara’s (1992) model, traders learn both from trades and from the lack of trades: trades signal the direction of any new information, and “the lack of trade provides a signal of the existence of any new information”. No trade is more likely when there is no new information, though a single no-trade observation does not in itself reveal whether there has been an information event. Hirshleifer (1971) writes that in discovery, nature will not reveal even a favorable state herself, so “no news is bad news”, and only human action can extract the information. In Diamond and Verrecchia’s (1987) model, no trade is bad news, being more likely when short-sale prohibitions keep informed traders from selling; with the prohibitions relaxed, the absence of trade has no information content at all (as reported by Easley and O’Hara). Chapter 20 takes up other readings.4

3.4 The non-rivalry law

The non-rivalry law says that telling others leaves one’s own knowledge intact, but the state of being the only one who knows ends.

Arrow (1962) calls a piece of information an indivisible commodity, frequently very cheap to transmit. Without special legal protection, any one buyer can destroy its owner’s small monopoly by reproducing it at little or no cost, and using it in production is bound to reveal it, at least in part. We read the law’s first half in the indivisibility and cheap transmission, and its second half in the lost monopoly (our judgment). For a trader, trading on a signal leaks it through trades and prices (our judgment; Section 1.6.2).5

The common conclusion that information loses value to its holder as others learn it assumes holding until disclosure, keeping a position until the information is made public. If her holding period is shorter than the time the news takes to become public, others learning can raise the value of her information, since their trading moves the price her way (Section 4.3), though if many hold the same position and leave together, their exits move it against them (our judgment).

In a model with perfect and costless markets, Hirshleifer separates foreknowledge from discovery. Whatever happens will in due time be evident to all, so the only advantage foreknowledge may give is knowing in advance. Having made a speculative commitment, the informed person in his model has every incentive to publicize the information, and the profits from the price revaluations he anticipates give him a reason to spread it. Within his model, the informed person profits by taking a position first and publicizing the information afterward (our judgment).

That speculative gain also needs counterparties who do not infer the news from the trade (our judgment). In Milgrom and Stokey’s (1982) no-trade theorem, private information creates no incentive to trade from a starting allocation that no trade could improve for someone without hurting someone else. There every trade is a bet, so the other side’s willingness to take it is evidence to at least one trader that his own side is unfavorable. The result depends crucially on its being common knowledge that a trade is feasible and acceptable to all, common knowledge meaning known to all, known by all to be known, and so on (Section 1.6.3; Chapter 4). Chapters 4 and 15 qualify the value of information further.6

3.5 The capacity law

The capacity law says that the information a person can absorb per unit of time has an upper limit, and tracking anything, prices included, draws on the same budget. What person ii learns in a time Δ\Delta about everything she tracks, that is, what her observations tell her beyond what she already knew (conditional mutual information, Section 2.3), is at most κiΔ\kappa_i \Delta (Box 3.1).

Here κi\kappa_i is her attention capacity, which can be read as the rate at which she actually takes information in, coding what she sees as well as she can. Sims (2003) models attention this way, with actions that depend on observations only through a channel of finite Shannon capacity and the delay of optimal coding ignored. One budget for everything a person tracks is an extension Sims suggests, an assumption Shannon’s theory does not supply (Section 2.7). His model has no asset prices, its nearest case being capacity spent on monitoring the aggregate price level of goods. This chapter cites no measurement of κ\kappa, and Chapter 10 takes up how attention is measured.

The symbol κi\kappa_i borrows the name of channel capacity and is not the Shannon capacity of any physical channel, a maximum over all sources that could feed the channel, approached in general only by coding over long blocks of symbols. The world does not code its signals for a trader, and the law uses only the ceiling that no code can exceed (our judgment; Section 2.7). A physical channel of capacity CC caps what each use adds at CC if its noise is independent from use to use and conditionally independent of what she already knows (our derivation; Box 3.1).7

The law covers only one form of limited attention, a cap on how much a person can absorb per unit of time. Not seeing public news at all (Section 3.3), salience, and failing to work out what one has seen are not counted against capacity (Section 1.6.5; Chapter 10). The book also keeps the price channel, the price as a carrier of information, apart from a person’s capacity; there, how much information a trade carries and how fast the price learns are two unequal quantities in the same units (our derivation; Chapters 16 and 17).

3.6 The price endogeneity law

The price endogeneity law says that the price is set by trading and becomes a signal to those who see it. That everyone sees it assumes centralized trading with public prices, all trades passing through one market whose prices anyone can see. In a decentralized market a trade’s price is seen by its two sides only, and the trade itself spreads the news (our judgment). Duffie and Manso (2007) model private auctions in which only those taking part learn each other’s information from the bids (Chapter 19).

The price depends on all trades so far and on noise (Box 3.1). In Huberman and Stanzl’s (2004) definitions, a trade’s price impact, how far it moves the price, has a permanent part and a temporary part, and the immediate impact is the two together (working-paper version). Hasbrouck measured information by the persistent impact, since the immediate one “may be contaminated by transient liquidity effects”, liquidity being the ease of trading without moving the price. For ADD, his estimates put the quote midpoint, halfway between bid and ask, roughly $0.014 higher on average right after a purchase, while a trade’s full impact arrives only with a protracted lag, which the law allows (our judgment).

His estimates make ADD’s impact positive, increasing and concave in trade size, so that a larger trade moves the quote more, though less than in proportion to its size, a concavity “quite typical of all the stocks in the sample”. Hasbrouck suggests that the information problem may differ for large block trades, negotiated between participants who deal with each other repeatedly. Measured impact can also look nonlinear if impact changes over time or if immediate and permanent impact are not told apart (our judgment; Box 3.2). In the case of Section 3.1, the law says only that the large trade became a signal to the market maker who saw it; how much it revealed is for models and measurement to answer (Chapter 17; our judgment).8

3.7 The no-arbitrage law in four forms

The no-arbitrage law says that, using only publicly available information, there is no way to earn money without risk and without capital; there is no free lunch, a strategy that costs nothing, cannot lose and may gain. Price-implied probabilities, the chances prices give to outcomes, are defined from it (Chapter 18). The form of the law changes with its premises (Table 3.1).

Price taking. Here no one’s trades move prices, and Harrison and Kreps (1979) assume that markets are frictionless, with no trading costs and unrestricted short sales, that everyone holds the same probabilities, and that all share one public information structure, the events they can tell apart at each date. They also restrict positions to change only at finitely many dates fixed in advance, in what they call simple strategies. A model is then viable, meaning that some agent who prefers more to less can find a best trade, if and only if there is at least one equivalent martingale measure. That is a set of probabilities that agrees with the true ones on which events are impossible and under which prices, counted in units of a security whose price is always positive, are martingales, expected to stay where they are. With finitely many states and dates, a model is viable if and only if there is no simple free lunch, so there the plain statement and the form in Box 3.1 agree (our derivation). A model is viable with every claim priced by arbitrage, each price fixed by those of traded securities, if and only if that measure is unique. In the book’s reading, a price-implied probability is therefore unique only if traded securities can reproduce every event about the state of the world, and otherwise lies in a range (our judgment).9

Price impact. In Huberman and Stanzl’s (2004) model, trades move prices and traders cannot know beforehand the prices they will get (working-paper version). Their trader has no better information about the asset’s value than others and can trade any amount; public news and others’ orders are independent with zero mean (working-paper version), which amounts to no risk premium (our judgment). They study price manipulation, in their technical sense a round trip of trades netting to zero with positive expected profit, and quasi-arbitrage, a sequence of round trips whose expected profit and Sharpe ratio, expected profit over its standard deviation, both grow without limit (working-paper version). If impact is the same in every period and others’ orders are zero or normally distributed, ruling out quasi-arbitrage requires a permanent impact linear in volume with non-negative slope, and so does ruling out manipulation when trades come close enough together (working-paper version). Under further conditions (Box 3.2) these requirements become equivalences. The book uses these conditions only to check models of impact (Chapter 17).10

Binary contract with funding costs and fees. A binary contract pays $1 if an event happens and nothing otherwise. Page and Clemen (2013) give traders a discount factor 1/b1/b below 1, so later payoffs are worth less and money tied up earning no interest keeps traders whose beliefs are near the price out. If every belief is π\pi, no one trades at prices between π/b\pi/b and 1−(1−π)/b1 - (1 - \pi)/b, a band 1−1/b1 - 1/b wide (our derivation; Box 3.2); for the drug contract of Section 1.3, with every belief at 0.30 and money tied up a year at 5%, so that b=1.05b = 1.05, the band runs from about 0.286 to 0.333 (our calculation). The example assumes one bb for all traders; interest paid on money tied up would bring bb nearer 1 and narrow the band (our derivation). In Bürgi, Deng and Whelan’s (2026) study of the Kalshi exchange from its start in 2021 to April 2025, makers, traders who post offers, seek their best price and allow for fees, so the best Yes and No offers on an option always sum to more than $1 (working-paper version). Reading a price as a probability also needs traders with no reason to hedge, the event touching their wealth only through the contract (working-paper version), and one of two alternative models. In the simplest version of Wolfers and Zitzewitz’s (2006) model, traders value wealth by its logarithm (log utility), and the market price equals the mean belief among them (working-paper version), which is right only if that mean is right. Page and Clemen generalize the other kind of model, with risk-neutral traders, who care only about expected gains, on limited budgets. There market clearing, the matching of purchases and sales, typically puts the price at a percentile of beliefs closer to 1/2 than the median. Which point of the band is read as the probability must be stated. No if-and-only-if theorem stands behind this form, and reading the no-trade band as a band of prices is the book’s own step (our judgment).11

Short-sale constraints and the bid–ask spread. With short-sale limits, Jouini and Kallal (1995a) show that there is no free lunch if and only if some unit of account and some equivalent measure exist with two properties. The unit grows at a rate between the lending and the borrowing rate, and under the measure, prices counted in it are supermartingales, “nonincreasing on average”, for securities that cannot be sold short and submartingales, nondecreasing on average, for those that can only be held short. With a bid–ask spread, Jouini and Kallal (1995b) show that there is no multiperiod free lunch if and only if, under some equivalent measure, some martingale lies between bid and ask. Both papers allow only simple strategies, give everyone one information structure, and take prices as unmoved by trades (our judgment). A security that cannot be sold short may thus be expected to fall without offering an arbitrage, since exploiting the fall would need a short sale (Chapter 20).12

Tests of the law need prices one could have traded at, such as the bid and the ask (our judgment); in Easley and O’Hara’s model, the series of transaction prices is a biased record of the true price process, with its variance overstated.13

3.8 Evidence cited in this chapter

Hasbrouck (1991) covers 80 stocks on the New York and American stock exchanges over the first quarter of 1989, with time counted in trades, and says his estimates “suggest” his findings. Huberman and Regev (2001) study one firm, EntreMed, in 1997 and 1998, and say the circumstances they exploit are unlikely to repeat. Tetlock (2011) covers news about S&P 500 firms from 1984 to 2004 (working-paper version). Page and Clemen (2013) analyze 1,787 Intrade markets begun from August 2002 to February 2007, and Bürgi, Deng and Whelan (2026) Kalshi contracts from 2021 to April 2025 (working-paper version). The other works are theory, and no study here measures the capacity law’s κ\kappa. The studies were chosen to illustrate the laws, so they neither prove nor test them; the laws are the book’s conventions, and the chapter’s only number of its own is the worked example in Section 3.7 (our judgment).14

3.9 A trader’s checklist

The questions below check a trading idea against the five laws, one law each. None says what to buy or sell, and good answers do not make a trade safe (our judgment).

  1. Who has seen it? Has the news reached those who set the price, or is it only publicly available (Section 3.3)? Testable: in Tetlock’s (2011) study of news on S&P 500 firms from 1984 to 2004, reactions to stories with more new information reversed much less than those to stale news, or even continued (working-paper version).
  2. When does the edge pay? Check: when is the news likely to become public, as at a scheduled release, and does the planned exit come before then (Section 3.4)? Held until disclosure, the edge shrinks as others learn; closed earlier, others’ trading can add to the gain, which with no likely date has nothing to rest on (our judgment).
  3. What else are you watching? Check: list the series you track each day, prices included, and before adding one, decide which will get less of your attention; under the capacity law, at your limit, each added series leaves less for the rest (our inference; Chapter 10).
  4. Is your own order the news? Testable: Hasbrouck’s estimates for 1989 suggest that large trades are associated with a widening of the spread and trades at relatively wide spreads with a larger price impact (Section 3.1; Chapter 17).
  5. Can you trade at those prices? Check: rerun a backtest, a test of a trading rule on past data, at the bid, the ask and the price after impact (Section 3.7). With short-sale limits, funding costs or fees, a predictable move need not offer an arbitrage (our judgment).15

3.10 Summary

  • Hasbrouck’s estimates for 1989 suggest that large trades are associated with a wider spread and trades at wide spreads with a larger price impact. He finds the widening consistent with a dealer judging news likelier after large trades; the book reads it as a case of the price endogeneity law (our judgment).
  • The observation law constrains information sets and does not constrain prices, so stale news moving a price does not break it. No news is an observation whose meaning depends on the model.
  • Telling others leaves one’s knowledge intact, but the state of being the only one who knows ends. That others learning lowers the information’s value assumes holding until disclosure; with a shorter holding period it can rise (our judgment).
  • The capacity law’s κ\kappa borrows the name of channel capacity and uses only its ceiling (our judgment); one budget for everything tracked is an extension Sims suggests.
  • The price is set by trading and becomes a signal to those who see it, which is everyone only with centralized trading and public prices.
  • The no-arbitrage law takes four forms, each under its own premises (Table 3.1). For a binary contract with funding costs and fees, the book reads only a band of prices (our judgment).

The trade-size premise matters, since in Allen and Gale’s (1992) equilibria uninformed traders manipulate profitably and this persists because trade sizes are limited (as reported by Huberman and Stanzl; working-paper version).
  • Proposition 2. If there is no manipulation, or no unbounded manipulation, and fixed costs for kk trades are proportional to kak^a, a<1a < 1, then P̂imm(q)−P̂imm(−q)≥Û(q)\hat P^{\mathrm{imm}}(q) - \hat P^{\mathrm{imm}}(-q) \ge \hat U(q) for q≥0q \ge 0 and all sufficiently small ΔN\Delta_N, the expected spread covering the expected permanent impact (working-paper version). No quasi-arbitrage does not in general imply this (working-paper version).
  • Proposition 4 and Corollary 1. If Pimm(q)≥U(q)/2P^{\mathrm{imm}}(q) \ge U(q)/2 and Pimm(−q)=−Pimm(q)P^{\mathrm{imm}}(-q) = -P^{\mathrm{imm}}(q) for q≥0q \ge 0, and others’ orders are normally distributed, then UU linear with non-negative slope, almost everywhere, is equivalent to no manipulation for all sufficiently small ΔN\Delta_N, to no unbounded manipulation and to no quasi-arbitrage (working-paper version). No quasi-arbitrage is then equivalent to strong viability, the trader’s best demand existing uniquely and being zero (working-paper version). We read this beside Harrison and Kreps’s finite case (Section 3.7) as no arbitrage being equivalent to viability (our analogy), though they define viability for different traders under different premises.

For linear impacts that change over time (working-paper version), with slopes λn,N\lambda_{n,N} for the permanent and μn,N\mu_{n,N} for the immediate impact, Theorem 2 says that for all sufficiently large initial prices, no manipulation is characterized by ΛN≽0\Lambda_N \succeq 0, where ΛN≡[2μ2,Nλ2,Nλ2,N⋯λ2,Nλ2,N2μ3,Nλ3,N⋯λ3,Nλ2,Nλ3,N2μ4,N⋯λ4,N⋮⋮⋮⋱⋮λ2,Nλ3,Nλ4,N⋯2μN,N].\Lambda_N \equiv \begin{bmatrix} 2\mu_{2,N} & \lambda_{2,N} & \lambda_{2,N} & \cdots & \lambda_{2,N} \\ \lambda_{2,N} & 2\mu_{3,N} & \lambda_{3,N} & \cdots & \lambda_{3,N} \\ \lambda_{2,N} & \lambda_{3,N} & 2\mu_{4,N} & \cdots & \lambda_{4,N} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ \lambda_{2,N} & \lambda_{3,N} & \lambda_{4,N} & \cdots & 2\mu_{N,N} \end{bmatrix}. With no temporary impact, λn,N=μn,N\lambda_{n,N} = \mu_{n,N}, this implies that liquidity cannot increase over time by too high a rate (working-paper version). With a risk premium, a round trip with positive expected profit could be payment for bearing risk, so only no quasi-arbitrage would remain apt (our judgment).

Short sales and spreads. In Jouini and Kallal’s theorems the equivalent measure QQ, their P*P^*, has 𝔼[(dQ/dP)2]<∞\mathbb{E}[(dQ/dP)^2] < \infty, and the unit of account is Z0*(t)=exp⁡(∫0tr0(s)ds)Z_0^*(t) = \exp(\int_0^t r_0(s)\,ds) with 0≤r0≤r̃0 \le r_0 \le \tilde r, the lending rate being zero and the borrowing rate r̃\tilde r.

Binary contract. With price p1p_1, belief qq and discount factor 1/β<11/\beta < 1, Page and Clemen find that a trader with 1−(1−p1)β<q<p1β1 - (1 - p_1)\beta < q < p_1 \beta neither buys nor sells. Writing bb for β\beta, if every belief is π\pi no one trades strictly between π/b\pi/b and 1−(1−π)/b1 - (1 - \pi)/b, a band of width 1−1/b1 - 1/b (our derivation); for π=0.30\pi = 0.30 and b=1.05b = 1.05, about 0.286 to 0.333 (our calculation). By their example, at 5% interest, contracts a year or more from expiry should not trade above 0.95 or below 0.05, yet they observe such trades.17
