Chapter 20 Bad News, Short-Sale Constraints and Momentum

Hong, Lim and Stein (2000) sorted US stocks by size and measured momentum, the return of past winners less that of past losers, in each size group, in data from 1980 to 1996 (working-paper version). In the very smallest stocks, with a mean market capitalization of $7 million, momentum was negative; it peaked in the third size decile, where market capitalization averaged about $45 million, at 1.43% per month (t-statistic 6.66), almost three times the value for the whole sample, and then declined to essentially zero in the largest stocks (working-paper version).

Their second test uses residual analyst coverage, the number of analysts who follow a stock measured relative to its size, and excludes all stocks below the 20th percentile NYSE/AMEX size breakpoint, because the vast majority of those never have any analysts (working-paper version). Momentum was 1.13% per month among stocks with low residual coverage and 0.72% among those with high, a difference of 0.42% with a t-statistic of 3.50, and the effect of coverage came entirely from the losers, low-coverage losers doing worse than high-coverage losers by 0.70% per month, with a t-statistic of 5.16 (working-paper version). Among winners the difference went slightly the “wrong way”, statistically significant in one of their tables and, the authors write, much more than their other results, fragile (working-paper version).

The authors read these findings as consistent with the hypothesis that firm-specific information, especially negative information, diffuses only gradually across the investing public (working-paper version). Section 20.1 asks where bad news loses speed under short-sale constraints, limits on selling borrowed shares, and Section 20.2 where momentum comes from in Hong and Stein’s (1999) model, whose prediction across stocks Hong, Lim and Stein test on its continuation side (Section 20.2.4).1

20.1 The speed of bad news and short-sale constraints

The root model’s price rule needs short selling, part of its default of frictionless trading (Sections 1.5 and 5.3; Table 5.1). This section drops that part: short sales banned, costly, or restricted in the use of their proceeds.

20.1.1 Three layers of slow bad news

Bad news can be slow at three layers (our judgment; Table 20.1). At the awareness layer the news has not reached enough people, for example because managers may hold bad news back. At the price layer those who know cannot push the price down far enough, because short sales are restricted and differences between people cannot be learned from the price, or because a constraint changes how fast private information enters a price set with rational expectations. At the action layer those who know may still wait, because a short position costs more to hold than a long one. Feedback from prices to managers’ decisions adds an asymmetric limit to arbitrage (Section 20.1.7), and since the same slow bad news can come from any layer, a test has to say first which layer it addresses (our judgment).2

20.1.2 Senders who hold back bad news

Hong, Lim and Stein’s own reading (Section 15.2.2) is that managers with good news probably have every incentive to push it out fast, while managers with bad news are likely to be less forthcoming, so outside analysts matter more; short-sale constraints may also be part of the explanation, though alone they would not seem to explain why the effect is stronger where analysts are fewer (working-paper version). The evidence is the interaction in the opening, low coverage with past losers; the managers’ behavior is the authors’ interpretation, which they do not test directly (our judgment; Section 15.2.2).

The same interaction does not separate the awareness layer from the action layer (our judgment). Abreu and Brunnermeier (2002) write that their account of bad news traveling slowly conforms with Hong, Lim and Stein’s finding that stocks with low analyst coverage seem to react more sluggishly to bad news than to good. If fewer analysts means that arbitrageurs learn of a mispricing in a more scattered way, the action-layer mechanism of Section 20.1.6 gives the same interaction, and telling the two apart needs another test, for example of how long the same kind of mispricing lasts in stocks that are cheap and dear to borrow (our judgment).

Hong, Lim and Stein’s prediction also assumes that smart-money arbitrage does not completely eliminate differences in momentum across stocks (working-paper version), in this book’s reading a question of the risk-bearing capacity of arbitrageurs, taken up in Chapter 23 (our judgment).3

20.1.3 Differences that cannot be learned

When short sales are restricted, whether the price sits above the average valuation depends, in the settings of Miller, of Harrison and Kreps and of Scheinkman and Xiong, on whether the differences between people can be learned from the price (our judgment). They cannot in two cases. In the first, no one draws rational inferences from what trading reveals. Diamond and Verrecchia (1987) write that existing studies, Miller’s among them, conclude that constraining pessimists without constraining optimists imparts an upward bias to stock prices, that such work assumes unchanged “rules”, and that none of these papers have investors with rational expectations, which here means inferring from prices and trades what others know (Section 16.1). In Jarrow’s (1980) model, they note, the bias can be positive, negative or zero (as reported by Diamond and Verrecchia), so the upward bias is not general even without rational expectations (our judgment). The root model’s traders, who do not learn from prices, belong to this case too (our judgment). In the second case people differ in their priors or in how they read the same information, as in Harrison and Kreps (1978) and Scheinkman and Xiong (2003). Dropping the default of Bayesian updating with its common prior, as these two models do, suffices in them together with the ban on short sales; it is not necessary, since traders who update by Bayes’ rule on their own information and do not learn from prices also keep differences that the price does not teach (our judgment).

Miller’s (1977) static argument (Section 19.3.6; Section 5.3 gives his setting) runs as follows. If each investor can buy only one share and there are NN shares, the shares end up owned by the NN investors with the highest evaluation of the return. As long as a minority of the potential purchasers can absorb the entire supply, as is typically the case, the market price is above the mean evaluation of the potential investors, and more divergence of opinion raises the market-clearing price; only when there is no disagreement does the average evaluation set the price. Dropping his assumption of risk neutrality leaves the argument unchanged, since his investors use standard portfolio theory, which allows for risk aversion, and enough short selling could force the price down to the average valuation of all investors. As a counterweight, if enough investors use uncertainty premiums in evaluating investments, the prices of risky investments tend to fall; since divergence of opinion is likely to increase with risk, he concludes, expected returns may well be lower for risky securities. The argument concerns the level of a market-clearing price and says nothing about the speed of adjustment, and it answers Section 10.4: under restricted short selling, a wider spread of individual errors can itself raise the price (our judgment).

In Harrison and Kreps’s market (Section 13.3) investors share all information though classes may read it differently, and the lowest consistent price typically exceeds every investor’s expected present worth of the dividends, a speculative premium whose precise results rest on risk neutrality, infinite wealth and a ban on short sales and which does not occur with one period remaining, with identical investors or with complete and perfect markets for contingent claims. Scheinkman and Xiong (2003) build a version in continuous time, with a single risky asset in limited supply and many risk-neutral agents; information is available to all, while two groups each overestimate the informativeness of a different signal and agree to disagree because of behavioral limitations. With short-sale constraints a buyer acquires an option to sell the asset to others when they are more optimistic, the resale option, so agents pay more than their own valuation of future dividends; the bubble comes from the divergence of opinions, and on average the agents are neither optimists nor pessimists. The authors rule out short sales outright for technical reasons and write that their qualitative results would survive costly short sales as long as owners can expect a profit when others value the asset more. In their equilibrium bubbles come with large trading volume and high price volatility.

This book also reads the resale option as a branch of the default of a single exogenous fact, since part of the price rests on others’ future views of the fact; Scheinkman and Xiong’s dividends do not depend on trading and meet that default’s letter, so the placing is a reading only (our judgment; Chapter 22).

In models without rational expectations, Diamond and Verrecchia describe an intuition that costly short sales leave only relative optimists in the pool of traders and so slow the adjustment of prices to private bad news; Harrison and Kreps’s investors have the same information, so private bad news does not arise there, and the overvaluation that Section 18.7 counted as a negative part of a measured excess return (our derivation; Table 18.1) comes from the two cases of this section, each with restricted short sales (our judgment).4

20.1.4 Rational expectations and the speed of adjustment

Diamond and Verrecchia (1987) ask what a short-sale constraint does when traders form rational expectations, a model that drops no learning from prices together with the short-selling part of frictionless trading (our judgment). In their model, which follows Glosten and Milgrom (1985), market makers are risk-neutral, face no inventory costs or constraints and earn zero expected profits from each trade, and traders are risk-neutral and either informed or uninformed. When it is common knowledge that short selling is constrained and differences in trades come from information rather than tastes, rational expectations change the “rules” and remove any upward bias to prices. The price is the conditional expectation of the asset’s value given all public information, so with a zero interest rate its unconditional expected change on any date is zero, a result that depends only on rational expectations and risk neutrality.

The direction depends on whom the constraint blocks (our judgment). By their Proposition 1, when all traders who can sell short can do so costlessly, the expected numbers of periods for prices to adjust to bad news and to good news both rise with the proportion of traders prohibited from short selling, and so does the ratio of the time for bad news to that for good. Their cost function is independent of a trader’s level of information, so the prohibited share is the same among informed and uninformed traders, and a period of no trade is then both somewhat informative and bad news (Box 20.2). By their Proposition 2, under proceeds-restrictions, which deny short sellers the immediate use of the proceeds, both expected adjustment times and their ratio fall as the proportion of traders subject to them rises, and a period of no trade leaves future bid and ask prices unchanged.

The authors argue that these effects are unlikely to dominate, since in a more general model with very informed and somewhat informed traders a cost that touches just the somewhat informed can reduce efficiency as a prohibition does. Their conclusion is that constraints that prohibit trades by the population fractions of informed and uninformed traders reduce unconditional informational efficiency, especially with respect to private bad news, and that, as a theoretical matter, constraints that restrict only uninformed trades improve it, especially with respect to private bad news, the first effect being, they argue, likely to dominate in practice.

Rational expectations make prices unbiased, so the model does not predict that the absolute price change is larger, on average, when bad news is announced. Where prohibitions dominate, the case they think more likely, with the reverse prediction in the other case, the authors predict that introducing options trading should reduce the left skew of price changes on announcement dates; the paper itself does not test the prediction (our judgment).5

20.1.5 An expected bubble with rational expectations

With rational expectations, Allen, Morris and Postlewaite (1993) show that an expected bubble, a price above every agent’s marginal valuation at some date and state, requires short-sale constraints to bind strictly in some future contingency (as reported by Brunnermeier); Section 21.2.1 gives their definitions and Tirole’s condition. In their four examples of strong bubbles the gains from trade come from heterogeneous beliefs, from state-dependent utility, from random endowments with identical concave utility, or from an interim Pareto inefficient initial allocation with different concave utility functions (as reported by Brunnermeier). The first is the second case of differences that cannot be learned in Section 20.1.3; in the other three, with rational expectations and private information, the branch is not such a case (our judgment), and in this book’s reading of Brunnermeier’s list they share a common prior (our inference); this book has not read the original.6

20.1.6 Costlier short positions

At the action layer, Abreu and Brunnermeier’s (2002) arbitrageurs become sequentially aware of a new fundamental value at a uniform rate, so at no point is the mispricing common knowledge among them, and for simplicity all are risk neutral (Section 21.2.4). Outside the relaxations that Table 5.1 lists for this chapter, a mispricing that is never common knowledge drops first-order knowledge, the default that traders need to know only the fact (our judgment; Section 21.2). The authors summarize the explicit and implicit costs of keeping a position open by a holding cost cc, which makes arbitrageurs cautious and gives them an incentive to postpone their trades, and which can also be viewed as a shortcut for the risk premium required for a risky arbitrage position. One example is recall risk: as soon as the lender of borrowed shares recalls them, short sellers have to cover by buying at unfavorable terms.

The model could be extended, the authors note, to asymmetric holding costs, which are typically higher for short positions than for long ones; in their model, they write, overpricing then persists longer than underpricing, so in effect “bad news travels slowly”. They add an exception: even a risk-averse arbitrageur who buys an undervalued asset might want to short close substitutes to hedge, so shorting costs can also arise if a security is underpriced. Their Section 5.1 discusses this extension in words and does not model it (our judgment). Since cc joins frictions and a risk premium, a delay alone cannot tell which of the two holds arbitrageurs back (our judgment); Chapter 23 takes up risk-bearing capacity.

Two earlier cases fit here (our judgment): the squeeze of Section 11.6 is a friction of the same family as recall risk, and in EntreMed’s case (Section 1.6.3) costly or hard short sales would be one way a holding cost delays a correction.7

20.1.7 Feedback and other asymmetric limits

Section 16.8 gave two asymmetries that feedback creates when managers learn from the stock price, in Goldstein and Guembel’s model uninformed selling that can pay while uninformed buying loses, and in Edmans, Goldstein and Jiang’s a limit to arbitrage that deters informed selling and encourages informed buying (as reported by Bond, Edmans and Goldstein; working-paper version). The survey’s authors write that informational feedback generates natural explanations of several phenomena that otherwise seem puzzling, among them manipulative short selling and the asymmetric dissemination of bad news and good news (working-paper version). In this book’s reading feedback slows bad news beside short-sale constraints and managers who hold back bad news, and it suggests a test: negative private information should enter prices more slowly in firms whose managers can more flexibly change their decisions in response to the price (our judgment).8

20.1.8 Expected falls without arbitrage

In Jouini and Kallal’s (1995a) result, with short-sale constraints, for traders who may use only simple strategies and share one information structure, and with prices that in this book’s reading are unmoved by trades (our judgment), there is no free lunch, roughly a gain without risk or capital, if and only if, in some unit of account that grows at a rate between the lending and the borrowing rate and under some equivalent measure, one with the same possible events, the prices of securities that cannot be sold short are supermartingales, processes that do not rise on average, and those of securities that can only be held short are submartingales. A security that cannot be sold short can thus be expected to fall without offering an arbitrage, since exploiting the fall would need a short sale; the fall is expected in the unit of account under the pricing measure, and under the true probabilities a risky drift is not bound by the band that frictions set (our judgment; Section 5.4). Section 3.7 gives the result in full.

Equilibrium says more. Unless an asset is in zero net supply, Jouini and Kallal write, the absence of free lunch does not exhaust the implications of equilibrium: an asset in positive supply must be held by some agent, so its price must be a martingale, a process with no drift, for the probability measure induced by this agent’s intertemporal marginal rates of substitution, while other assets need not be, since they may be held by other agents with different marginal rates of substitution because of the short-sales constraints.

Harrison and Kreps’s example shows both. Under their consistent prices all investors view speculation as no better than a fair game; for the class that holds the stock in a state the game is exactly fair, while in the state with no dividend the other class expects a gross return over one period of 11/9 against a riskless 4/3, and the lowest consistent price is the present value of the dividends under a measure spliced from the holders’ one-step probabilities (our derivation; Box 20.1).9

20.1.9 Three readings of missing news

This book reads a stretch with no trades in three ways (our judgment). In Easley and O’Hara’s (1992) model the market does not know whether an information event has happened, and no trade is more likely when there is no new information (Section 3.3). In Diamond and Verrecchia’s model no trade is bad news when short sales are prohibited and leaves the quotes unchanged under proceeds-restrictions alone (Section 20.1.4). In Lee’s (1998) model, transaction costs may prevent many traders who trade in sequence from revealing their private information, so hidden information accumulates in the market and may be released by a small trigger, with high volatility and no accompanying event (Section 21.2.5). The same silence thus has different meanings depending on whether it is uncertain that anything happened, on a ban on short sales, or on fixed trading costs, and a reading has to say first which case applies (our judgment).10

20.1.10 Bad news on television

Section 5.5 gave Busse and Green’s (2002) measurement of stock reports on CNBC’s Morning Call and Midday Call in 2000: prices responded to negative reports more gradually than to positive ones, within the limits that its scope box states. For both of their measures of abnormal return, negative Midday Call reports lead to cumulative abnormal returns, returns beyond the normal added up, that become increasingly negative and increasingly significant during the following month, which the authors read as a sign that the initial response could be the beginning of a larger, long-horizon reaction. After negative Morning Call reports, a sample the authors call small, 19 reports on just five trading days, the abnormal performance disappears after the day of the report.

The authors offer two readings, higher costs of short selling and a gradual response to other negative public news about the firm or industry (Section 5.5), and test neither; the awareness and action layers of this section could give the same shape, so this book adopts neither reading and prints no new number from the paper’s tables (our judgment).11

20.1.11 Disclosure that reaches the holders

When short sales are restricted and the price is set by those who trade on their own views without reading others’ information from trades or prices, as in Miller’s argument, only holders can sell, so bad news moves the price only once it changes holders’ views, and disclosure has to reach them (our judgment). With rational expectations a lack of informed selling can move the quotes by itself, as no trade does under a prohibition in Diamond and Verrecchia’s model (Section 20.1.4 and Box 20.2). Ljungqvist and Qian’s (2016) short sellers publish reports after taking a position (working-paper version), and publishing takes the information to the shareholders, whose capital can push the price down (our judgment; Sections 1.6.3, 12.9 and 19.3.1).

20.2 Gradual information diffusion and momentum

Hong and Stein’s (1999) model is a bridge that joins a fixed spreading process to prices; this section sets it beside three other sources of momentum and comes back to analyst coverage.

20.2.1 Newswatchers on a fixed timetable

Hong and Stein’s newswatchers base their forecasts only on news they privately observe and do not read prices (working-paper version; Section 16.1). They are divided into zz equal-sized groups, every group sees the current dividend level, and at each time tt news about a future shock begins to come out, broken into zz packets that sum to it; at tt each packet is known by one group, at t+1t + 1 by two, and so on until t+z−1t + z - 1, when every group has seen all of the information (working-paper version). All newswatchers have CARA utility, constant absolute risk aversion, under which the stake put at risk does not depend on wealth, with a common risk-aversion parameter; the riskless rate is zero and the supply of the asset is fixed at QQ (working-paper version). With newswatchers alone the price follows their equation (1), so new information works its way into the price over zz periods, returns are positively correlated over horizons shorter than zz, and prices never overshoot their long-run values (working-paper version; Box 20.3). If newswatchers did read prices, with QQ common knowledge, the price would fully reveal the news, so the assumption that they do not builds the underreaction into the model (working-paper version; Sections 16.5 and 16.10).

The spreading side is an input fixed in advance, a deterministic, linear timetable that adds one group per period, with no contact between people and no S-curve (our judgment). In a footnote on frontrunners (Section 20.2.2) the authors note that prices should then no longer have the unnatural feature of adjusting linearly to news in the absence of momentum trade (working-paper version). Abreu and Brunnermeier’s arbitrageurs also learn on a fixed timetable (Section 20.1.6), and Chapter 21 compares the two.

Against the root model, in this book’s reading, Hong and Stein change several defaults (our judgment; Table 5.1). A fixed timetable with no contact between people replaces the external source and word of mouth, leaving homogeneous mixing nothing to apply to and the path deterministic, as under the deterministic mean field. News arriving in every period drops one-time injection; groups that each process only a subset of the information (Section 10.7) drop unlimited attention, which Table 5.1 lists for this chapter in parentheses; and momentum traders who read past prices by a rule (Section 20.2.2) drop no learning from prices, which stays for the newswatchers, together with unlimited attention. Outside the relaxations that Table 5.1 lists for this chapter, CARA utility replaces log utility, a constant discount for holding the positive supply, the term in QQ of equation (1), replaces the fact unrelated to aggregate risk, and the momentum traders’ order flow, the only moving supply, replaces an exogenous counterparty.

The newswatchers trade as if they will hold their positions to the end, a time-inconsistent shortcut that the authors adopt because it dramatically simplifies the analysis and that, they write, they have no reason to believe colors any of their important qualitative conclusions (working-paper version).12

20.2.2 Momentum traders and overreaction

Momentum traders also have CARA utility but finite horizons: at every time tt a new generation enters and holds its position for jj periods (working-paper version), a holding period that Hong and Stein set from outside the model (our judgment). They condition only on the cumulative price change over the past kk periods, and much of the paper sets k=1k = 1, the last price change; their order flow is a constant plus ϕ\phi times the last price change, equation (3), which the newswatchers treat simply as an uninformative supply shock (working-paper version; Box 20.3). If momentum traders could forecast with nn lags of price changes, each with its own weight, the authors suspect that for sufficiently large nn many of their results would go away (working-paper version).

In equilibrium ϕ\phi solves their equation (7), in which γ\gamma is the momentum traders’ aggregate risk tolerance, their combined willingness to bear risk (working-paper version; Box 20.3). Such an equilibrium may not exist for arbitrary parameter values, and the authors cannot generically rule out multiple equilibria, so their numerical search always picks the one with the smallest ϕ\phi (working-paper version). In any covariance-stationary equilibrium, one whose variances and covariances do not change over time, ϕ>0\phi > 0, so momentum traders rationally chase trends (working-paper version).

Two propositions hold in any covariance-stationary equilibrium. By Proposition 1, after a positive one-unit shock the cumulative impulse response of prices, the total price change since the news began to spread, peaks strictly above one, and if j≥z−1j \ge z - 1 it peaks at t+jt + j and then declines, eventually converging to one; by Proposition 2, if returns are positively correlated at short horizons, then with risk-neutral momentum traders they are negatively correlated at some horizon no longer than j+1j + 1 (working-paper version). A momentum trader who buys shortly after good news imposes a negative externality on those who follow him, and momentum traders cannot tell whether a price rise comes from news or from earlier rounds of momentum trade (working-paper version; Section 16.7).

In their baseline, zz is 12 months, the news shocks have a standard deviation of 0.5 per month and γ\gamma equals 1/3; with jj = 12 months, ϕ\phi is 0.2605 (working-paper version). The impulse response then peaks 12 months after a shock at 1.342, so at the peak prices overshoot the change in long-run fundamentals by 34.2%, and the first negative autocorrelation of returns comes at a lag of 10 months (working-paper version). Figure 20.1 redraws this baseline (our calculation); with newswatchers alone the price climbs in a straight line to one by month 11 (our derivation; Box 20.3).

An extension keeps its own scope. Frontrunners, in the authors’ definition, use the news they privately observe to forecast short-run price changes, and the basic results stand as long as they are somewhat risk-averse and, like the newswatchers, do not extract information from prices; they speed news into prices, but as long as they are risk-averse they never completely eliminate underreaction (working-paper version). The authors also say plainly that they designed their model to deliver positive correlations at short horizons and negative ones at longer horizons (working-paper version).13

20.2.3 Sources of momentum compared

Momentum followed by reversal has several model sources in this book, and none of them is the only explanation (our judgment; Table 20.2). Andrei and Cujean’s noisy rational expectations model (Section 19.2.1) has momentum at a lag if and only if precision elasticity there exceeds one, which needs private information to flow in at an increasing rate. They write that learning from prices is instrumental in generating momentum in their model, whereas momentum does not obtain in Hong and Stein’s if investors learn from prices, so learning from prices works in opposite directions in the two models (our judgment). In Pedersen’s model (Section 19.2.2) a network that keeps echoing views gives momentum of its own (our judgment), and his proof that a value-and-momentum strategy has a positive expected return in the model covers the symmetric naive case.

In Daniel, Hirshleifer and Subrahmanyam’s (1998) model (Section 13.4), fixed overconfidence makes price changes unconditionally negatively autocorrelated at short and long lags, while confidence that changes through biased self-attribution, with gradual enough overreaction or correction, gives unconditional short-lag positive and long-lag negative autocorrelation; overconfidence thus gives unconditional momentum there only when it moves with outcomes (our judgment), while in Scheinkman and Xiong’s model overconfidence about different signals gives a resale option (Section 20.1.3).

All four models can give some continuation, each under its own premises, so continuation followed by reversal cannot by itself tell them apart; what can separate them are their premises, the shape of the information flow, whether anyone learns from prices, the network, and whether confidence moves with outcomes (our judgment; Section 13.4).14

20.2.4 Analyst coverage as the external source

In Hong and Stein’s Table 3, more gradual information diffusion makes stocks more attractive to momentum traders and so, for a wide range of parameter values, leads to more pronounced overshooting and stronger reversals; analyst coverage might serve as a crude proxy for the speed of diffusion, and if one accepts this proxy, stocks with scantier coverage, presumably smaller ones, should show greater long-run overreaction-type predictability (working-paper version).

Hong, Lim and Stein read their inverted U in size through the informal theory of their introduction: smaller firms may have slower information diffusion, which would lead to more momentum, but probably also thinner market-making capacity, which can lead to more pronounced reversals after supply shocks (working-paper version). In event time, momentum ran out after about ten months among stocks with many analysts for their size and continued to about the two-year mark among those with few (working-paper version; Section 1.8). Within the theory of gradual information diffusion they emphasize, the authors read this as high-coverage firms underreacting by roughly 9% to the information in lagged six-month returns and catching up in a little less than a year, and low-coverage firms underreacting by about 20%, numbers that use beta-adjusted returns, sorted on residual coverage, in the same sample (working-paper version).

Two limits apply (our judgment). The test measures the continuation, the underreaction side, and does not test for the long-run reversal that Hong and Stein predict (working-paper version). Andrei and Cujean add that Hong, Lim and Stein’s study concerns cross-sectional momentum, comparing stocks with one another, while Hong and Stein’s model and theirs concern time-series momentum, each asset’s own past, two forms strongly related but not identical; they also explain reversals for small firms through liquidity shocks, a reading beside the thin market-making above.

Section 1.6.5 reads analyst coverage as a proxy for the root model’s external source α\alpha, whose strength can differ by stock and by whether the news is good or bad (our judgment); here the same proxy serves both sections, through the managers’ choice of Section 20.1.2, behind the asymmetry between losers and winners, and through Hong and Stein’s slow diffusion, behind the link between coverage and momentum (our judgment).15

20.2.5 Small-firm drift within two years

Bernard and Thomas’s (1989) post-earnings-announcement drift, prices moving for months in the direction of an earnings surprise, measured for NYSE and AMEX firms from 1974 to 1986 (Sections 5.5 and 12.1), gives a contrast. In their Table 1 the drift of small firms, the highest earnings surprises less the lowest, is 9.90% over trading days 1 to 180 and 9.99% over days 1 to 480, while for medium firms it falls from 8.85% to 7.72% and for large firms from 4.47% to 3.61%, declines of about 13% and 19% (our calculation). The cumulative drift of small firms thus shows no net reversal by day 480, about two years of trading (our calculation), though over days 241 to 300 the difference is significantly negative for all three sizes (Section 12.1), so “no net reversal” describes only the small firms’ cumulative drift up to day 480 (our judgment).

Earnings are public news that everyone can see on one date, while Hong and Stein’s overreaction concerns private news that spreads gradually (our judgment). If momentum traders can condition on the date of a public announcement and chase trends only right after it, Hong and Stein note, there need be no overreaction to public news, and the impulse response may be everywhere increasing; if they are not so sophisticated, the impulse response to public news is also hump-shaped (working-paper version). The important point, they write, is that the logic of their model admits, and even strongly suggests, that the response to public news may look different from that to private information, which they call clearly testable (working-paper version). The small firms’ net drift to day 480 fits the first case, though their negative drift over days 241 to 300 does not, and the medium and large firms’ declines fit the second; these data cannot decide between those cases and Bernard and Thomas’s reading of a market that fails to recognize the implications of current earnings for future earnings (our judgment; Section 12.4).16

20.4 A trader’s checklist

None of these questions says what to buy or sell, and good answers do not make a trade safe (our judgment).

  1. At which layer is the bad news slow? Check: whether the news has not reached enough people, those who know cannot push the price down, or they can and wait because a short costs more to hold than a long, a delay that needs them to become aware one after another, the mispricing never common knowledge, and a holding cost large enough relative to the mispricing; in Hong, Lim and Stein’s data (working-paper version), low coverage with past losers cannot separate the awareness layer from the action layer (our judgment; Sections 20.1.1, 20.1.2 and 21.2.4).
  2. Can the differences behind the price be learned? Check: with restricted short sales and differences that cannot be learned, Miller’s price sits above the average valuation when a minority can absorb the supply; with rational expectations, risk-neutral traders and market makers, a constraint that is common knowledge and differences from information, Diamond and Verrecchia’s price is unbiased given public information, a prohibition slows bad news, and a constraint that restricts only uninformed trades speeds it, an effect the authors judge unlikely to dominate (our judgment; Sections 20.1.3 and 20.1.4).
  3. Who can sell? Check: with restricted short sales and no one reading others’ information from trades or prices, as in Miller’s argument, only holders can push the price down, so bad news has to reach them; with rational expectations a lack of informed selling can move the quotes (our judgment; Sections 20.1.4 and 20.1.11).
  4. Early or late in the cycle, after private or public news? Check: in Hong and Stein’s model, momentum traders who use one past price change cannot tell whether a rise came from news or from earlier momentum trading, and if they can condition on the dates of public announcements and chase trends only right after them, there need be no overreaction to public news (working-paper version; Sections 20.2.2 and 20.2.5).

20.5 Summary

  • Bad news can be slow at three layers, awareness, price and action, and feedback from prices to managers adds an asymmetric limit to arbitrage; a test has to say which layer it addresses (our judgment; Section 20.1.1).
  • In Hong, Lim and Stein’s US data for 1980 to 1996, among stocks above the 20th NYSE/AMEX size percentile, momentum was stronger where residual analyst coverage was low, driven by past losers (working-paper version); managers’ reticence about bad news is the authors’ interpretation, which they do not test directly, and the interaction cannot separate the awareness layer from the action layer (our judgment; Sections 20.1.2 and 20.2.4).
  • With restricted short sales and differences that cannot be learned from the price, Miller’s static argument, when a minority can absorb the supply, and Harrison and Kreps’s and Scheinkman and Xiong’s resale option, when opinions differ and the most optimistic group can change, put the price above the average valuation, and in the last two dropping Bayesian updating with its common prior suffices and is not necessary (our judgment); in Jarrow’s model, also without rational expectations, the bias can be positive, negative or zero (as reported by Diamond and Verrecchia; Section 20.1.3).
  • With rational expectations, risk-neutral traders and market makers, a short-sale constraint that is common knowledge and differences from information, Diamond and Verrecchia’s price is unbiased given public information; prohibitions that bind informed and uninformed traders in proportion slow adjustment, especially to bad news, while constraints that restrict only uninformed trades speed it, an effect the authors judge unlikely to dominate (Section 20.1.4).
  • In Jouini and Kallal’s model, with simple strategies, one information structure for all and, in this book’s reading, price takers (our judgment), a security that cannot be sold short need only be a supermartingale, in a unit of account growing at a rate between the lending and the borrowing rate and under some equivalent measure, so it can be expected to fall without offering an arbitrage; in equilibrium an asset in positive supply must also be a martingale under the measure its holder’s marginal rates of substitution induce (Section 20.1.8).
  • In Hong and Stein’s model news spreads among newswatchers who do not read prices on a deterministic, linear timetable fixed outside the model (our judgment); with newswatchers alone prices underreact and never overshoot, and momentum traders who use one past price change make the price overshoot and then reverse in any covariance-stationary equilibrium, peaking at 1.342 in their baseline, results many of which, the authors suspect, would go away if momentum traders used enough lags (working-paper version; Section 20.2.2 and Figure 20.1).
  • Momentum has several model sources in this book, Hong and Stein’s slow diffusion with rules of one variable (working-paper version), Andrei and Cujean’s accelerating inflow of private information with learning from prices, Pedersen’s network and Daniel, Hirshleifer and Subrahmanyam’s self-attribution, and continuation followed by reversal cannot by itself tell them apart (our judgment; Section 20.2.3).
