Chapter 11 Complex Contagion and Critical Mass

In January 2021, retail investors coordinated on the Reddit forum WallStreetBets to target short selling of GameStop shares by hedge funds. A short seller borrows shares, sells them and buys them back later, so he loses when the price rises; a short squeeze is a rise that forces short sellers to buy back and so pushes the price up further. In the background that Lucchini et al. (2022) give, users of the forum started the squeeze on January 22, 2021; by January 26 the price had risen more than 600%, on January 28 it reached its all-time intraday high, and on February 1 and 2 it fell substantially (as reported by Lucchini et al.).

Lucchini et al. studied the forum from November 27, 2020 to February 3, 2021. They count a commitment when a user shows proof that he has money at stake in the stock, and they identify 36,128 commitments by 30,133 users. Showing proof of one’s position when starting a new discussion about investments is one of the forum’s widely shared norms (as reported by Lucchini et al.). Before the announcement of a new board of directors at GameStop, users posted a few dozen commitment posts a day; the number rose tenfold on the day of the first price spike, January 14, and kept growing, and for the eleven days until the next spike, on January 25, it grew while returns did not.1

The root model of Chapter 5 assumes simple contagion: hearing a message once is enough to believe it and act on it. Table 5.1 in Section 5.8 lists this default with the others, and this chapter drops it to ask what changes when one hearing is no longer enough. Sections 11.6 and 11.7 return to the case.

11.1 Simple and complex contagion

Suppose one trader hears a stock recommended three times in a week by the same friend, and another hears it once each from three people who do not know one another. The first has one source, heard three times; the second has three sources.

Centola and Macy (2007) call a contagion complex when it requires social affirmation from multiple sources, that is, when a person catches it only after contact with two or more sources of activation. When behaviors are costly, risky or controversial, the willingness to take part may require independent affirmation or reinforcement from several sources. The distinction between simple and complex contagion refers to the number of sources of exposure that activation needs. Their example is disease: depending on how contagious it is, infection may require several exposures to carriers without requiring exposure to several carriers. If each exposure transmits the disease with probability PP, the chance of catching it after EE exposures is 1−(1−P)E1 - (1 - P)^E, so for any PP above zero a single encounter can be enough, and each contact with the same carrier counts as one more exposure. A simple contagion, in this sense, is one that a single source can pass on.

The friend who repeats a tip is the carrier who comes back. As long as each telling persuades with the same probability, whatever came before it, the repetitions change only the rate of word of mouth, β\beta in the root model of Chapter 5, and leave it a simple contagion; in that model, where nobody stops spreading, a higher rate changes only how fast the tip spreads (our judgment).

Information can be a complex contagion too. Centola and Macy note that while hearing about a job once is usually enough, gossip may not be believed until independent sources confirm it. Granovetter (1978) read his thresholds the same way for rumors: some people may need to hear a rumor from more than one other person before they believe it enough to spread it. This is the belief threshold that Section 5.3 gave as the first reason why people who know may still not act: a person waits to hear from several sources before she believes.2

Under homogeneous mixing, where anyone can meet anyone at random and two people who meet have used no common source, the chance of meeting the same person twice in a large group goes to zero, so kk contacts come from kk different people; only on the lasting links of a network does one source come back again and again (our judgment; Section 11.4).

11.2 Critical mass without a network

In Granovetter’s riot, one person in a crowd has threshold 0, one has threshold 1, one threshold 2, and so on up to the last, with threshold 99. A person’s threshold here is how many others he must see join before he joins. The person with threshold 0 starts; this activates the person with threshold 1, the two of them activate the person with threshold 2, and so on, until all 100 people have joined. Now remove the person with threshold 1 and replace him by one with threshold 2. The instigator riots, nobody now has threshold 1, and the riot ends with one rioter.

Granovetter defines a threshold as the number or proportion of others who must make a decision before a given person does, the point where that person’s net benefits begin to exceed his net costs. Write F(x)F(x) for the share of the group with a threshold at or below xx, and r(t)r(t) for the share who have joined by round tt. Everyone whose threshold the current share has reached joins in the next round, so r(t+1)=F[r(t)].r(t + 1) = F[r(t)] . With no provision for removing participants, r(t)r(t) cannot oscillate and the process always reaches an equilibrium, at the point where FF first crosses the 45° line from above, the line on which the share next round equals the share now. Granovetter notes that the model makes an implicit assumption of complete connectedness, which is often inappropriate: each person responds to the behavior of all the others, that is, to one number, the share of the whole group acting, and the model has no network (our judgment).

Granovetter then gives his 100 people normally distributed thresholds with a mean of 25 and lets their standard deviation, a measure of how widely the thresholds are scattered, grow. Up to a critical standard deviation the equilibrium number of rioters rises gradually to about six; past that point, approximately 12.2, it jumps to nearly 100 and then declines. Below the critical standard deviation, FF crosses the 45° line three times: at a low point from above, then once from below, and later from above again. At the critical standard deviation the first two crossings merge into a point where FF touches the line.

The middle crossing, where FF crosses from below, is unstable: if the share acting starts above it, for whatever reason, the process climbs to the high crossing, and if it starts below, it falls back to the low one, provided people whose threshold is no longer met stop acting, as the map r(t+1)=F[r(t)]r(t + 1) = F[r(t)] implies (our derivation). We read that middle crossing as the critical mass, the share beyond which adoption carries itself along (our judgment). Granovetter does not use the term, and he follows only the path that starts from nobody acting and stops at the first crossing. Our recomputation of his example puts the critical standard deviation at 12.22 people and the merged crossing at 6.2 people (our calculation).

Section 5.6 described adoption driven purely by social influence, which peters out as it approaches the first fixed point of the distribution of thresholds (working-paper version), and Granovetter’s first crossing is that point (our judgment). In a group without a network, a process that starts from nobody acting stops where the distribution of thresholds alone decides (our derivation).3

The critical mass comes from a threshold compared with the share acting at the time (our derivation). In the contact form of the root model, where people meet at random and nobody forgets or stops spreading, requiring two different sources only slows the start: if two people act at the start, everyone acts in the end, with no critical point (our derivation).4

Figure 11.1 draws both examples; for a standard deviation of 0.10 of the group, 10 people, its unstable crossing lies at 0.14 of the group, 14 people (our calculation).5

11.3 Four mechanisms behind a threshold

Centola and Macy write that there are at least four social mechanisms that might explain why complex contagions require exposure to several sources: strategic complementarity, credibility, legitimacy and emotional contagion. This book puts legitimacy and emotion together under norms and sentiment and adds visible actions and common knowledge as a fourth (our judgment). A model can give all four the same threshold, and they differ in what a public signal does to them (our judgment).

11.3.1 Strategic complementarity

The costs and benefits of investing in public goods often depend on the number of prior contributors, the critical mass that makes more effort worthwhile. Economists call this strategic complementarity: the more others do something, the more it pays each person to do it too. Granovetter’s model requires that the costs and benefits of each choice depend in part on how many others make which choice, and in a riot the dependence runs this way: the cost of joining falls as the riot grows, because the chance of being arrested is smaller the more people take part.

Cong, Li and Wang (2021) model a market case, a platform whose users transact in its own token, where adoption decisions are complements both through the platform’s transactions and through the cost of holding tokens, which depends on how the token’s price moves (working-paper version). With no users, each person gets no surplus from holding tokens and chooses not to join (working-paper version), so a user base of zero keeps itself at zero (our judgment). Given the platform’s productivity and the expected rise in the token’s price, an assumption on how users differ, a monotonic hazard rate, guarantees a unique positive number of users; once the authors relax it, several user bases can meet the conditions of equilibrium (working-paper version). Their model has complete information (working-paper version), so nobody is waiting for news, and the threshold is a best response to what others do (our judgment). This is an adoption threshold, a number of others acting beyond which acting pays; it concerns payoffs, and the threshold of the next section concerns belief (our judgment).

11.3.2 Confirmation from independent sources

Centola and Macy’s second mechanism is credibility. Innovations often lack credibility until neighbors adopt them, and hearing the same story from different people makes it seem less likely that surprising information is nothing more than the informant’s invention.

The count of sources helps fully only when their errors about the fact are independent; sources that share errors count for less, and copies of one report count as one (our judgment). Five accounts that all pass on one original report add nothing about the fact beyond that report, and counting them as five independent pieces of evidence is persuasion bias, which Chapter 13 takes up (our judgment). Four analysts with their own data and methods come closer to four sources, as long as their data and methods share no error (our judgment). The belief threshold of Section 5.3 acts on what a person believes and leaves unchanged whether she has heard (our judgment).

11.3.3 Norms and sentiment

The third mechanism is legitimacy: having several close friends take part in a collective action often increases a bystander’s acceptance of the movement’s legitimacy. Centola and Macy’s fourth mechanism is emotional contagion: models of collective behavior commonly assume expressive and symbolic impulses that can be communicated and amplified in gatherings that are close in space and in social ties. This book counts legitimacy and sentiment, optimism or pessimism unconnected with fundamental information, among departures from updating beliefs by Bayes’ rule (our judgment). On the GameStop forum, Lucchini et al. measure group identity by the share of the plural “we” among first-person pronouns in posts about the stock, and they find that expressions of a shared identity emerged together with the rise in commitment and might have helped sustain it, and that identity is unlikely to have been the origin of the collective action.

11.3.4 Visible actions and common knowledge

A threshold acts on the participation that people can see. Granovetter notes that judgments may be inaccurate because those who have decided to do something do not make their action public; many thresholds may then have been passed while the people who hold them do not know it, and so they do not act. A screenshot of a position turns participation that nobody could see into participation that everyone on the forum can see (our judgment).

Under strategic complementarity each person also wants to know how many others will act and whether they know it too, a chain that leads toward common knowledge, where everyone knows something, everyone knows that everyone knows it, and so on without end (our judgment). To select among several equilibria, Cong, Li and Wang write that one may take a global game approach (working-paper version), which needs, among other premises, each player to have only a noisy private signal and common knowledge to be missing (our judgment); Chapter 21 takes up both. A public signal does different things under different mechanisms: one can break an information cascade, in which later people copy earlier ones and ignore their own information, and under strategic complementarity it changes what each person expects the others to do (our judgment; Chapters 12, 14 and 21). For a belief threshold, a public signal is one source for everyone who sees it, however often it is repeated (our judgment).6

11.4 Sources, clustering and wide bridges

In Centola and Macy’s models a network can hinder complex contagion in ways that help simple contagion: as thresholds rise, long ties, which link people who are far apart in the network, can impede diffusion, and complex contagions depend primarily on the width of the bridges across a network. A bridge here is a set of ties from one part of the network into another. Its critical width is the minimum number of nonredundant ties that a contagion needs to spread into a neighborhood where nobody is active yet. For simple contagions it is 1, whatever the network. Here sources and repetitions part: a person who needs two sources needs ties to two different active people, and contacts repeated along one tie count once (our judgment).

Simple contagions can spread on a connected network even if every tie is random, and they spread faster the larger the share of random ties. For complex contagions there is a critical upper limit of randomization above which they cannot spread, and the limit falls as thresholds rise. The authors simulate a lattice in which each person has 8 neighbors, with 40,000 people and averages over 100 runs, and they rewire a share of the ties at random by a technique that keeps the network connected and each person’s number of links constant. Complex contagions gained nothing from a little rewiring; more rewiring first helped and then hindered them, and past a critical share they failed entirely. Below that share cascades spread to the whole population, and above it they reached less than 1% of the nodes; for a contagion that needs 2 of the 8 neighbors to be active, the critical share is about 0.1. These are simulations on lattices, and the authors compare them with no measured network (our judgment).

Unequal numbers of links could make complex contagion more fragile still: a scale-free network can be even more sensitive to perturbation than the regular lattices. The authors simulate a scale-free network with high clustering, clustering being how far a person’s contacts also know one another, with 40,000 people, many of whom have fewer than 5 links and a few more than 100, every person needing the same number of active neighbors. There, contagions that needed 2 active neighbors were almost entirely blocked once the authors rewired more than 0.001 of the ties, against about 0.1 for the regular lattice, though the frequency of cascades dropped off noticeably more gradually. The authors also argue that a well-connected manager may hear of an innovation sooner but adopt it later than an isolated one who hears from one of a few peers, because those who have not adopted press back on her. The argument assumes that what counts is the share of contacts acting, and their scale-free runs, with one required number for everyone, do not test it (our judgment). Hubs, people with far more links than most, act differently in each kind of spreading (Section 7.5), and complex contagion is one more kind (our judgment).

In Watts’s (2002) threshold rule of Section 7.5, a person adopts once at least a threshold fraction of her neighbors have adopted. Watts calls a person vulnerable if one active neighbor is enough to make her adopt, and unless the innovators are connected to a community of early adopters, no cascade is possible. On his sparse random networks he finds two regimes in which very large cascades, which he calls global cascades, occur very rarely. He conjectures that such a cascade requires the vulnerable people to form a connected cluster that takes up a finite share of the network. If his conjecture holds, global cascades in his model travel through people for whom one active neighbor is enough, so what crosses his sparse random networks is a simple contagion for those who pass it on, and people who need two or more active neighbors are reached only where such neighbors cluster (our judgment). His cascade window is the range of thresholds and average numbers of links in which his condition for a global cascade holds. Just inside its upper boundary, where the network is highly connected and global cascades become larger but increasingly rare, a system looks highly stable, with only tiny cascades after many shocks, until a shock like any other sets off a global cascade. His results are for models on random networks (our judgment).

Centola and Macy conclude that long, narrow bridges are useful for spreading information, while too much randomness can be inefficient for spreading the social reinforcement needed to act on it, especially as thresholds increase or connectedness declines. They knew of no empirical studies that had directly tested the need for wide bridges. The closest, in their account, is one study by Backstrom et al. of joining LiveJournal blogging communities, an indirect test in which having friends in a community who are also friends with one another raised the probability of joining (as reported by Centola and Macy).

The difference between sources and repetitions, the help that clustering gives and the need for wide bridges all arise only when complex contagion meets a network; the critical mass of Section 11.2 needs no network (our judgment).7

11.5 Seeding and submodularity

Whoever wants a story to spread, an issuer, an analyst or a forum’s organizer, chooses whom to tell first. Kempe, Kleinberg and Tardos (2003) frame the choice as picking a fixed number of people, the seeds, so that the expected number finally reached is as large as possible; Section 7.5 described their experiment. The greedy algorithm they use starts with no seeds and repeatedly adds the person who gives the largest marginal gain.

Its guarantee rests on submodularity, a property of diminishing returns: the marginal gain from adding a person to a set of seeds is at least as high as the gain from adding the same person to a larger set that contains the first. By a theorem of Nemhauser, Wolsey and Fisher, for a function that is non-negative, monotone and submodular the greedy choice reaches at least 1−1/e1 - 1/e of the best value (as reported by Kempe, Kleinberg and Tardos), provided the values of the function are computed exactly; with estimates from simulation it loses a little more (Box 11.2). Kempe, Kleinberg and Tardos prove that the reach is submodular in the independent cascade model of Chapter 6 and in the linear threshold model, where each person adopts once a weighted share of her neighbors reaches a threshold drawn at random between 0 and 1. In the second model the property holds only on average over the random thresholds (our judgment): for a fixed choice of thresholds, the number activated is not in general submodular in the seeds. In simple models that build in a fixed threshold, such as 1/2, at every person, no algorithm that runs in reasonable time can guarantee any non-trivial share of the best reach on every network, assuming P≠NP\mathrm{P} \neq \mathrm{NP}, the unproved conjecture that some problems whose answers are quick to check cannot be solved quickly.

Kempe, Kleinberg and Tardos conjectured that whenever every person’s activation function, which they call a threshold function, is monotone and submodular, the reach is monotone and submodular as well. With thresholds uniform on [0,1][0, 1], as in that model, a person’s activation function gives her chance of adopting for each set of active neighbors; monotone means that more active neighbors make her more likely to adopt, and submodular that each neighbor’s marginal effect falls as the set of active neighbors grows. Mossel and Roch (2010) proved the conjecture under these premises: thresholds independent and uniform on [0,1][0, 1], activation functions monotone, submodular and zero when no neighbor is active, and a progressive process, in which an active person stays active forever. The premise on activation matters: any activation function that is not submodular admits a network on which the reach is not submodular, and with thresholds from a general distribution the condition becomes that the chance of adopting, given the set of active neighbors, is increasing and submodular, a condition that the authors show to be necessary.

With a fixed threshold that needs two or more different active neighbors, the chance of adopting is 0 or 1 for each set of active neighbors, and for a threshold of two the first active neighbor adds nothing and the second everything; such a function is not submodular, so complex contagion of this kind lies outside the theorem, and some network gives it a reach that is not submodular (our derivation; Box 11.2).

In their simulations with higher thresholds, Centola and Macy seeded each run by choosing a node at random and activating it together with its neighbors. For a trade idea that people act on only after two or more of their own contacts have, seeds placed together among people who know one another can reach people whom as many scattered seeds cannot, and the greedy algorithm’s guarantee does not cover the choice (our judgment). Where a source is best placed can differ between spreading by thresholds and spreading in which people average their neighbors’ views (our judgment). Chapter 13 takes up averaging, and Chapter 15 the sender’s choice of seeds.8

11.6 The GameStop case

Lucchini et al. rationalize the events by recent findings on how a small fraction of committed individuals may trigger behavioral cascades: in that theory, as they describe it, minorities of committed individuals may mobilize large fractions of a population even when they are extremely small, and Chapters 13 and 15 take up such minorities. They write that the increase of commitment on the forum predated the initial surge in price, and that commitment predates price surges and is sustained also in the absence of gains.

Users who committed early occupied a central position in the forum’s network of replies, measured by the k-core, the set of users each connected with other members of the set by at least k links. Against a benchmark model, both commitment and its reach, the users that commitment posts reached, were concentrated in the core of the network until January 19, and from then on commitment reached further into the periphery.

This chapter’s tools give four readings of the case, all of them this book’s (our judgment), and the paper models and tests none of them: posting a screenshot made an action visible to everyone on the forum (Section 11.3.4); each buyer stood to gain more the more others bought, which is strategic complementarity (Section 11.3.1); in a squeeze, short sellers have to buy back to cover their positions, and that buying, a friction and one of the limits to arbitrage of Section 20.1.6, amplified the buyers’ collective action; and the redundant links among users in the core fit the wide bridges that complex contagion needs (Section 11.4).

The data limit what the case can show. Commitment posts rose tenfold on the day of the first price spike, so what the rise in commitment led was the main squeeze from January 22, and at the first spike the two came on the same day (our judgment). The study covers a single event, and the paper reads the lead from its plots, with no statistical test (our judgment). For three days after the first price surge, commitments from posts labeled “Gain”, which show profits, outnumbered those from posts labeled “YOLO”, the forum’s label for a large bet and otherwise the main kind, and a post that shows a gain is by its nature a result of the rise (our judgment). The paper describes its benchmark in two opposite ways, in its results as keeping commitment and randomizing the network and in its methods as keeping the network and reshuffling commitment over it. The benchmark of the methods does not match users by how often they post, so part of the concentration in the core may come from core users posting more (our judgment). The 30,133 users with a commitment are about 6.7% of the 450,710 nodes of the reply network (our calculation), and they include people who joined after the squeeze, so this share says nothing about how many were committed before the cascade (our judgment). The paper estimates no threshold and no critical share, and its commitment is a position shown in public, unlike the never-changing opinion of a committed minority in the theory it cites (our judgment).

Chapters 19 and 21 take up the case’s other side, the bubble and coordination.9

11.7 Receiving and acting

A trader or a researcher needs to measure the share who have heard a message apart from the share who act on it (our judgment). Young (2009) reports Ryan and Gross’s survey of farmers in two Iowa communities: although at least two-thirds of them had heard about the advantages of hybrid corn as early as 1931, only about 8 percent had adopted it by then (as reported by Young; working-paper version). The farmers listed seed salesmen as their most common first source of knowledge and neighbors as the most influential in deciding whether to adopt (as reported by Young; working-paper version). Ryan and Gross argued that the survey suggested some form of learning: in some sense, early adopters gave their neighbors a community laboratory with the new seed (as reported by Young; working-paper version). Young finds social learning consistent with the curve of adoption, though perhaps not its only cause (working-paper version). Chapter 14 takes up learning from neighbors’ harvests.

On GameStop the paper keeps acting and attention apart. Before the board’s announcement users already posted a few dozen commitment posts a day, and in the network of interactions of one week in mid-December the central users were those who committed more; broad attention followed the price with a delay.

A trader can measure attention, such as searches and mentions, apart from holdings or flows of money and compare when each moves; for Bitcoin, this book’s pilot has not run this test, and Chapter 22 sets out the data it would need (our judgment). Granovetter notes that a distribution of thresholds can be inferred from the exact time sequence of adoptions, a tautological procedure that would explain little but could at least relate thresholds to background characteristics, attitudes and social positions; such variables, measured before anyone acts, are what can explain a threshold (our judgment).

Chapters 14, 19 and 21 take up Section 5.3’s other reasons why people who know may not act.10

11.9 A trader’s checklist

None of these questions says what to buy or sell, and good answers do not make a trade safe (our judgment).

  1. Are you counting sources or repetitions? Check: if each repetition persuades with the same probability whatever came before it, one account repeating a tip changes only the rate of word of mouth, and in the root model, where nobody stops spreading, only how fast the tip spreads; copies of one report count as one source, and sources that share errors count for less (our judgment; Sections 11.1 and 11.3.2).
  2. Does the threshold come from payoffs or from belief? Check: an adoption threshold comes from payoffs, as in this book’s reading of the GameStop squeeze, where each buyer stood to gain more the more others bought; a belief threshold is waiting for several independent sources. A public signal changes what people expect others to do under the first and counts as one source under the second (our judgment; Sections 11.3 and 11.6).
  3. Can others see who has acted, and could a small change tip the outcome? Check: in Granovetter’s account, when those who have decided to act keep it private, many thresholds may have been passed without their holders knowing it, so they do not act (Section 11.3.4). For his 100 people with normal thresholds of mean 25 and no network, the end point reached from nobody acting went from about six people to nearly 100 as the standard deviation passed about 12.2 people; reading the unstable middle crossing as a critical mass is our judgment (Section 11.2).
  4. Are there wide bridges among the people who must act? Check: in Centola and Macy’s lattice simulations, contagions that needed two or more active neighbors failed once the authors rewired more than a critical share of ties at random, so a large account’s reach may bring attention without buyers to a trade that people join only after several of their own contacts (our judgment; Section 11.4).
  5. Does your measure count who heard or who acted? Check: in Ryan and Gross’s survey of farmers in two Iowa communities, at least two-thirds had heard of hybrid corn by 1931 and about 8% had adopted it (as reported by Young; working-paper version); measure attention apart from holdings or flows of money and compare their timing (our judgment; Section 11.7, and Chapter 22 for the data such a test would need on Bitcoin).

11.10 Summary

  • Complex contagion, in Centola and Macy’s definition, needs several different sources before a person acts; if each repetition persuades with the same probability whatever came before it, one source that repeats itself changes only the rate of word of mouth, and in the root model, where nobody stops spreading, only how fast a message spreads (our judgment; Section 11.1).
  • With no network, everyone responding to the share of the whole group acting, a distribution of thresholds alone can give several equilibria and a jump: for Granovetter’s 100 people with normal thresholds of mean 25, the equilibrium reached from nobody acting jumped from about six people to nearly 100 past a standard deviation of about 12.2 people; reading the unstable middle crossing as a critical mass is our judgment (Section 11.2).
  • The adoption threshold of strategic complementarity, where acting pays more as more people act, is a different thing from the belief threshold, where a person waits for several sources, which count fully only when their errors are independent (our judgment; Section 11.3).
  • The difference between sources and repetitions, the help of clustering and the need for wide bridges arise only on a network (our judgment), and in Centola and Macy’s lattice simulations complex contagions failed once the authors rewired more than a critical share of ties at random (Section 11.4).
  • With thresholds independent and uniform, activation functions monotone, submodular and zero when no neighbor is active, and nobody turning inactive, Mossel and Roch prove the reach of a set of seeds submodular, the property behind the greedy algorithm’s guarantee; a fixed threshold that needs two or more sources lies outside this theorem (our derivation; Section 11.5).
  • On one forum from November 2020 to February 2021, GameStop commitment posts rose tenfold on the day of the first price spike and kept rising for eleven days without gains, and early committed users sat in the reply network’s core, against a benchmark the paper describes in two opposite ways; with one event and no statistical test, the readings that commitment led only the main squeeze and that each buyer stood to gain more the more others bought are this book’s (our judgment; Section 11.6).
  • A trader needs to measure the share who have heard apart from the share who act (our judgment): in Ryan and Gross’s survey of farmers in two Iowa communities, at least two-thirds had heard of hybrid corn by 1931 and about 8% had adopted (as reported by Young; working-paper version; Section 11.7).

For a continuous FF the path from r(0)=0r(0) = 0 rises and settles at the smallest fixed point (our derivation), Granovetter’s first crossing from above.

Stability. Near a fixed point, r(t+1)−r*≈F′(r*)[r(t)−r*]r(t + 1) - r^* \approx F'(r^*)\,[r(t) - r^*], so the point is stable when F′(r*)<1F'(r^*) < 1 and unstable when F′(r*)>1F'(r^*) > 1; an unstable crossing between two stable ones divides the shares that climb to the higher one from those that fall to the lower one, if people whose threshold is no longer met stop acting, as the map implies (our derivation), and we read it as the critical mass (our judgment).

Normal thresholds. For normal thresholds the density of thresholds equals 1 at most at two points (two when σ\sigma is below about 0.40; note 11), so F(r)−rF(r) - r falls, rises and falls again and has at most three zeros (our derivation). The critical standard deviation is where F(r)=rF(r) = r and F′(r)=1F'(r) = 1 hold together, Granovetter’s tangent point (our derivation); for a mean of 0.25 it is 0.12222, his approximately 12.2 people out of 100 (our calculation).

The rigorous versions of this box’s own derivations are to be given in the appendix.11

Box 11.2 Submodularity and the greedy guarantee

Definitions. Let σ(S)\sigma(S) be the expected number of people active at the end when the set SS is seeded; this σ\sigma is Kempe, Kleinberg and Tardos’s reach, unrelated to the standard deviation σ\sigma of Box 11.1. A function ff of sets is submodular if f(S∪{v})−f(S)≥f(T∪{v})−f(T)f(S \cup \{v\}) - f(S) \geq f(T \cup \{v\}) - f(T) for all elements vv and all pairs of sets S⊆TS \subseteq T, and monotone if adding an element never lowers it. For a non-negative, monotone submodular ff, the greedy set of kk elements reaches at least (1−1/e)(1 - 1/e) times the best value over sets of kk elements (as reported by Kempe, Kleinberg and Tardos), if ff is computed exactly; for any ε>0\varepsilon > 0 there is a γ>0\gamma > 0 such that with (1+γ)(1 + \gamma)-approximate values it reaches 1−1/e−ε1 - 1/e - \varepsilon.

The theorem. In the general threshold model person vv has a monotone activation function fvf_v from sets of her neighbors to [0,1][0, 1], with fv(∅)=0f_v(\emptyset) = 0, and becomes active once fvf_v of her active neighbors reaches her threshold θv\theta_v. Mossel and Roch’s Theorem 1, with thresholds independent and uniform, makes the weighted reach σw\sigma_w, which is σ\sigma when ww counts people, monotone and submodular when the fvf_v and ww are. In their §3, for thresholds of distribution FvF_v, the condition is that Fv∘fvF_v \circ f_v be increasing and submodular, which their construction shows to be necessary.

Fixed thresholds. With a fixed threshold, Fv∘fvF_v \circ f_v takes only the values 0 and 1. A monotone function with values 0 and 1, and 0 when no neighbor is active, is submodular if and only if it equals 1 exactly when at least one member of some set UU of neighbors is active (our derivation). A fixed threshold of two or more different sources breaks this, so by Mossel and Roch’s construction some network gives it a reach that is not submodular (our derivation).12
