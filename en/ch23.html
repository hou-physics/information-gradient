Chapter 23 Anomaly Decay and Limits to Arbitrage

McLean and Pontiff (2016) followed 97 predictors of stock returns from 79 academic studies, each as a long-short portfolio, most of them buying the stocks in one extreme fifth of a ranking and selling those in the other. The average portfolio earned 0.582% a month in the original studies’ samples, 0.402% after a sample ended but before the study was published, and 0.264% after publication, with on average 323, 56 and 156 months in the three periods, before trading costs, which the paper does not deduct (our judgment). By the paper’s regression, returns fell 26% after the samples ended and 58% after publication (Box 23.1).1

Figure 23.1 also shows the spread of the predictors’ measured mean returns: their standard deviations are 0.395, 0.651 and 0.516 in the three periods, and in the two later periods they exceed the mean (our judgment). Part of that spread is the sampling error of means taken over 56 and 156 months on average, and the largest spread comes in the shortest period, so the average alone does not tell what any one predictor earned (our judgment).

Scope. The figures cover the 97 published cross-sectional predictors studied, rules that rank stocks, as long-short portfolios, mostly of the extreme fifths, built from CRSP data, which cover US-listed stocks, equal-weighted except in one study, in data that end in 2013. The 26% is an upper bound on statistical bias because some traders likely learned of a predictor before publication, and the 32% a lower bound on the effect of publication. As exact figures, both would need the assumption that no one knew a rule before publication; the authors assume only that more investors know of it afterward than before, the date being only a proxy for when traders learn of it. Whether the remaining 42% or so is a cost of arbitrage or compensation for risk cannot be told apart (our judgment).

This raises two questions (our judgment): why does a gradient that has been published shrink, and why not to zero? This chapter judges no strategy, and nothing in it is advice to buy or sell (our judgment).

The chapter’s models drop, among others, the four defaults that Table 5.1 lists for this chapter: the single exogenous fact, one fact whose true value trading does not touch (Section 23.1); log utility, whose default is log utility with equal, fixed wealth (Section 23.3.1); frictionless trading, short selling allowed with no capital constraints or trading costs (Sections 23.3 and 23.4); and spreaders as traders, the default that those who spread news trade on it, dropped only together with frictionless trading (Section 23.3.2), hence the parentheses around this chapter in its row. Outside the relaxations that Table 5.1 lists for this chapter, Grossman and Stiglitz’s example, which enters at an interior equilibrium with supply noise, drops unlimited attention and no learning from prices through its costly information and learning from the price, while its supply noise keeps the exogenous counterparty (Section 23.2); holding until disclosure is a candidate default that Section 5.8 keeps outside both tables (Section 23.3.4).

23.1 Publication and self-defeating knowledge

Publication brings no new observation about any firm; what spreads is a rule for turning public data into expected returns (our judgment; Section 12.7). This book calls such a rule self-defeating knowledge: a published pattern, itself a product of prices, that weakens as more people know it and trade on it (our judgment). Trading here changes the very pattern the rule describes, which drops the single exogenous fact (our judgment).

McLean and Pontiff read their two regression coefficients, for the months after a sample and after publication, against four cases: both at −0.582, the negative of the average in-sample mean, if statistical biases are the source of in-sample predictability; the post-publication one at −0.582 and the post-sample one close to zero if returns are entirely mispricing that arbitrage after publication fully corrects; both at zero if there are no statistical biases and papers have no influence on investors; and, if predictability reflects only rational expectations, equal predictability before and after publication once statistical bias is removed.

They reject both the hypothesis that predictability disappears entirely and the hypothesis that it does not change after publication, and the test that the post-sample and post-publication changes are equal gives a p-value of 0.024. Declines after publication are greater for predictors with higher in-sample returns, and the authors conclude that their findings suggest investors learn about mispricing from academic publications.2

The design gives only the bounds of the opening’s box, so the decline can be read neither as pure data mining nor as pure arbitrage (our judgment); Section 12.7 gave its timing by year. Predictability that falls after publication is the testable difference that Table 22.2 gives for self-defeating knowledge (our judgment). The rule works on public data, so it acts on the second part of the information gradient, undigested information, news seen by those who set the price but not yet correctly digested in it (our judgment; Section 1.3). Outside the relaxations that Table 5.1 lists for this chapter, a publication that arrives after trading has begun also drops one-time injection (Chapter 12).

23.2 Decay of the gradient and two residuals

Unable to prove their propositions in general, Grossman and Stiglitz (1980; Sections 4.5 and 16.4) worked out an example with constant absolute risk aversion and normally distributed variables, price-taking traders, the supply of the risky asset per person as the only noise in the price and a signal of fixed precision that a trader has or not according to whether he has paid its cost; such equilibria, they add, may not exist in general. At an equilibrium in which some traders are informed and others are not, the price’s informativeness rises if the cost of information falls.

What the informed know beyond the price then equals risk aversion times the cost, aca c in nats, units of information measured with natural logarithms, so it shrinks with the cost of information (our derivation; Box 4.1). This book calls it the information-cost residual, the part of the gradient that those who pay for information must earn back; it holds only under the conditions that put the equilibrium share of informed traders between zero and one.

After publication a rule costs almost nothing to learn, though applying it each month still takes data that are not free, so the cost of information is likely to be a small part of the remainder that McLean and Pontiff measure (our inference), about 42% of the in-sample return by the regression coefficients or 45% by the Table I means (our calculation; Box 23.1). Whether the rest is a cost of arbitrage or compensation for risk, the question that Section 12.7 left, the design cannot tell (our judgment; the opening’s box).

McLean and Pontiff name the second residual’s source themselves. If predictability reflects mispricing that publication leads sophisticated investors to learn about and trade against, they expect the returns to disappear or at least decay; decay, as opposed to disappearance, will occur if frictions prevent arbitrage from fully eliminating mispricing, such as systematic noise trader risk (De Long et al. 1990) and idiosyncratic risk and transaction costs (Pontiff 1996 and 2006), effects that principal–agent problems between investors and investment professionals can magnify (Shleifer and Vishny 1997). Their “if” makes frictions one sufficient reason for decay without disappearance, so the decay alone does not prove that frictions caused it (our judgment). Returns after publication are lower for predictors less costly to arbitrage, those concentrated in liquid stocks and in stocks with low idiosyncratic risk.3

Table 23.1 sets the two residuals side by side: they shrink for different reasons (our judgment). The arbitrage-cost residual, what keeps a known mispricing from being traded away, shrinks only as trading, short selling, funding and bearing risk get cheaper, and it stays when information is free (our judgment). Like Table 18.1 (Sections 18.6 to 18.8), this chapter counts the compensation for noise trader risk, and for the idiosyncratic and funding risk of specialized arbitrage capital, in the gradient’s risk-premium term, so the arbitrage-cost residual is the mispricing that those risks and the costs of trading leave in place (our judgment). In De Long et al.’s own model everyone knows the fact, so the gap there sits in the resale value, outside the gradient (Section 18.8); the model enters here for the resale-price risk that limits arbitrage, which applies as well where a known gradient remains, as after publication (our judgment).

23.3 Three limits to arbitrage

Limits to arbitrage are the constraints on capital, counterparties, short selling and time that keep a visible mispricing from being traded away at once. This book sorts them into three branches, each with its own source and premises (our judgment): risk bearing over a short holding period (De Long, Shleifer, Summers and Waldmann), capital that follows performance (Shleifer and Vishny), and synchronization risk (Abreu and Brunnermeier).

23.3.1 Noise trader risk and short horizons

Section 18.8 gave De Long, Shleifer, Summers and Waldmann’s (1990) result: noise traders, whose expectations are faulty and stochastic, make an asset without fundamental risk risky, and sophisticated investors hold it only if paid for the risk that noise traders turn bearish and its price falls (working-paper version). Noise trader risk is this risk, that noise traders push the price further off before it returns. Four premises decide where the result applies.

The first is that noise traders stay. As the distribution of their misperception converges to a point mass at zero, the equilibrium price converges to its fundamental value of one (working-paper version; Box 23.2). The branch keeps the exogenous counterparty, traders who trade for reasons other than information; without them the price sits at fundamental value and there is nothing to limit (our judgment).

The second is a finite horizon: with lives longer than two periods the equilibrium comes closer to the fundamental one (working-paper version; Section 18.8), and the overlapping generations make each agent’s horizon short, so that no agent can wait until the price “recovers” before selling (working-paper version). An infinitely lived sophisticated investor never faces a settlement date and could push the price to its fundamental value of one, and the longer the holding period, the smaller the excess return needed for a given price risk (working-paper version). A long horizon can still help little, they add: with sufficient dividend risk and persistent misperceptions, agents might well find buying and holding for very long periods unattractive, since prices may deviate even further from fundamental values before they might have to sell (working-paper version).

The third is that there is no last period while the payoff carries no fundamental risk. Equilibrium exists as long as the returns to holding the risky asset are always uncertain, which the overlapping generations assure by having no last period; with a last period in which the asset pays a sure dividend and is liquidated, both kinds of trader would seek what they see as riskless arbitrage (working-paper version). With fundamental dividend risk, the authors add, the qualitative properties of their equilibrium hold even with a known terminal date (working-paper version; Section 18.8). The fourth is unbounded prices: that normally distributed asset prices are unbounded is, in their words, “essential for the existence of equilibrium in the basic model” (working-paper version).

The model puts constant absolute risk aversion and two-period lives (working-paper version) in place of log utility (our judgment; Box 23.2). How far a mispricing can persist here depends on the sophisticated investors’ risk-bearing capacity, how much risk they are willing and able to bear, against the resale-price risk that noise traders create over a short holding period (our judgment; Sections 16.3 and 19.3.8). Whether it is why smart money leaves Hong, Lim and Stein’s momentum differences (working-paper version) in place stays this book’s reading, untested (our judgment; Section 20.1.2).4

23.3.2 Capital that follows performance

Sections 16.8 and 19.3.3 gave Shleifer and Vishny’s (1997) performance-based arbitrage, in which poorly informed outside investors move funds according to arbitrageurs’ past performance (working-paper version).

In their model arbitrageurs and their investors are fully rational, and risk-neutral arbitrageurs take positions against the mispricing that noise traders generate (working-paper version), so this branch’s limit comes from funding and needs no aversion to risk (our judgment). In an extreme situation arbitrageurs try to sell even though prices are far below fundamental values, and precisely when prices are furthest from those values they take the smallest position (working-paper version).

The result rests on three key assumptions about agency problems in arbitrage: in extreme circumstances arbitrageurs are already fully invested, at their leverage constraint with no access to fresh equity; investors in arbitrage funds allocate fresh equity by past performance because they do not know the structure of the model; and markets are segmented, with no capital flowing in from outside investors who are both sophisticated and not already invested (working-paper version). Under these assumptions arbitrage fails to bring prices back to fundamental values when they have moved far away (working-paper version).

The authors add that a substantial number of arbitrageurs with resources independent of past performance can undo the effects of performance-based liquidations (working-paper version), though after a noise trader shock as large as the one in their model most arbitrageurs in a market are likely to be fully committed, so in extreme circumstances performance-based arbitrage is likely to hold and little fresh capital will be available to stabilize the market (working-paper version).

Anomalies that persist over the long term, they argue, must be unpredictable enough to make arbitraging them away risky for specialized arbitrageurs, a risk that need not be correlated with any macroeconomic factor and can be purely idiosyncratic fundamental or noise trader risk, and they expect anomalies to reflect high idiosyncratic return volatility of the arbitrage trades needed to eliminate them (working-paper version). McLean and Pontiff’s lower returns after publication where idiosyncratic risk is low (Section 23.2) point the same way without testing this mechanism (our judgment).

The arbitrageurs’ leverage constraint drops frictionless trading in its part of no capital constraints, and the separation of brains from resources drops spreaders as traders with it, since those who know trade with other people’s money (our judgment).5

23.3.3 Synchronization risk

Sections 20.1.6 and 21.2.4 gave Abreu and Brunnermeier’s (2002) branch: risk-neutral arbitrageurs become aware of a mispricing one after another, it is at no point common knowledge among them, and synchronization risk, the uncertainty about when the others will act, combined with holding costs typically makes them delay, unless the mispricing exceeds bounds set by the dispersion of opinion among arbitrageurs and other parameters (Box 21.2). Since risk-neutral arbitrageurs may find it optimal to submit orders of infinite size, the authors cap each one’s long and short position in shares. For each period away from the neutral portfolio an arbitrageur pays holding costs of cpt|xi|c p_t |x_i|, with ptp_t the price and xix_i her position, costs amplified because she might be forced to end the strategy before it pays off.

Their cc joins frictions and a risk premium (Section 20.1.6), so a delay alone cannot say which of the two holds arbitrageurs back. Each branch puts one limit in front, risk bearing, funding or coordination, under its own premises, though Abreu and Brunnermeier’s holding cost and position limits can stand for the other two (our judgment).6

23.3.4 Holding periods and convergence times

The three branches share one ingredient (our judgment): an arbitrageur must carry a position until the price returns to fundamental value, which takes a time this book calls the convergence time, and bears the price risk or the costs in between. In De Long et al.’s model the price has no tendency to return to fundamental value of itself (working-paper version), so the convergence time has no bound (our judgment). In Shleifer and Vishny’s, the underpriced asset’s price rises to its fundamental value VV at the last date for sure, and withdrawals after losses can force arbitrageurs to sell before then (working-paper version). In Abreu and Brunnermeier’s, holding costs accrue until the mispricing is corrected, at the latest at t0+τ‾t_0 + \bar\tau even without the arbitrageurs’ selling.

In the root model, whoever holds to the end bears only the risk in the fact and none in the resale price, which implies the candidate default of holding until disclosure, keeping a position until the information is made public (our judgment; Sections 3.4 and 5.4). A holding period shorter than the convergence time lets in the resale price’s risk, the form of noise trader risk that Section 5.4 named (our judgment), and such a trader needs others to learn the same thing in time (Section 10.6). Frictions can scale with the holding period too: investors usually do not have full use of the proceeds from short sales of stocks, which effectively represents a short-selling cost proportional to the holding period.

Table 23.2 sets the three branches side by side. Outside the relaxations that Table 5.1 lists for this chapter, De Long et al.’s noise traders drop Bayesian updating, and their price for a payoff that everyone knows drops the fact unrelated to aggregate risk, whose default is that with full information the price equals the probability; Shleifer and Vishny’s investors, whom the authors call fully rational, read ability from the price’s move, which drops no learning from prices (our judgment).7

23.4 Trading and short-sale costs

Predictability that stays within trading costs cannot be used (our judgment). In Jouini and Kallal’s form, a predictable move without risk can be no arbitrage while it stays within a band set by the frictions, and a risky drift is not bound by the band (our judgment; Section 3.7). Section 18.7 gave two readings of a friction: compensation within the required return, or a bound on how much of the other parts of a return can be captured (our judgment). This section drops frictionless trading in its parts of short selling allowed and no trading costs.

23.4.1 The size of friction-type limits

The costs that Jouini and Kallal report, which set the band’s width, come from sources of 1983 to 1991. In the Treasury market the short-selling cost is the spread between the repo and the reverse repo rate, typically between 0.25% and 0.65% in Stigum (1983) and much larger for specific bonds (as reported by Jouini and Kallal). For stocks, Sharpe (1987) reports an average bid–ask spread of 0.52% for companies worth more than 1.5 billion dollars and up to 6.55% for those worth less than 10 million, plus a typical retail commission of roughly 1% for orders of 100 shares or more in stocks above 100 dollars, while spreads and fees in the Treasury market are much smaller (as reported by Jouini and Kallal). The size of such costs, Jouini and Kallal write, “varies considerably across securities”; these figures speak for their markets and their years, and this book has no data on present costs (our judgment).8

23.4.2 Earnings drift inside trading costs

Chapter 12 gave Bernard and Thomas’s (1989) post-earnings-announcement drift, prices that keep moving in the direction of an earnings surprise after the announcement, in NYSE and AMEX firms from 1974 to 1986, and Section 18.7 its bound, which falls with firm size as Stoll and Whaley’s transaction costs do (as reported by Bernard and Thomas).

The authors write that the abnormal returns appear to be within round-trip transactions costs for the small individual investor. Doubling the costs for a combined long and short position, with a turnover of 78% of the portfolio each quarter on average, they put the implied cost at about 6% a quarter for small stocks and 3% for large ones, about the size of the 60-day abnormal returns of their Table 1; doubled, Stoll and Whaley’s costs exceed the drift’s bounds (as reported by Bernard and Thomas). The costs are the small investor’s in the era of negotiated commissions after 1975. As one alternative description, the authors suggest that the drift may appear at all levels of implied excess returns yet never exceed a threshold equal to the cost of exploiting the information, whatever the size of the earnings surprise.

They raise difficult questions for this explanation themselves: why trading continues after the announcement if costs keep traders out, why traders who pay no commissions and can bypass the specialist’s spread do not eliminate the drift, why costs would necessarily cause underreaction instead of simply adding noise to prices, and why so much of the drift is concentrated around the next quarter’s announcement.

The apparent upper bound is their only result inconsistent with incomplete updating of earnings expectations. One possibility that could reconcile the two, they write, is that prices fail to reflect the full implications of current earnings for future earnings until the discrepancy exceeds a threshold that gives speculators enough incentive to trade, which leaves unanswered why some investors are willing to trade at the “wrong” price in the meantime. The evidence fits the bound reading of a friction: what a public, free earnings number implies can stay out of the price within the costs, an arbitrage-cost residual, while the authors’ questions stay open (our judgment).9

23.4.3 Spreads in measured decay

A spread biases measured prices as well (our judgment). In Easley and O’Hara’s model, the series of transaction prices is a biased record of the true price process, its variance overstated (Section 3.7). If one can follow only price data, they write, quotes may be the better data set, since quotes, unlike transaction prices, occur continually and have no optional sampling problem.

Tetlock skips the first day after the news partly because of bid–ask bounce, and he notes that bounce and nonsynchronous trading would predict larger reversals in illiquid stocks, the opposite of his large reversals in highly liquid stocks and minimal ones in illiquid stocks (working-paper version). McLean and Pontiff’s are monthly returns from CRSP data. An estimate of decay needs the same care with spreads, from quotes in clock time, and Chapter 24 gives the method (our judgment).10

23.5 Gradients left by market structure

Two further gradients survive for reasons other than cost, each only within its own model (our judgment).

23.5.1 Market power

Sections 4.6 and 17.4 gave Kyle’s (1989) model, a single auction of demand schedules with normal variables, exponential utility and positive noise trading. An informed speculator who recognizes that he trades against an upward-sloping residual supply curve restricts his trading like a monopolist, so that with imperfect competition “prices never reveal more than one-half the private precision of informed speculators”, an upper bound; with noise trading exactly zero and traders not risk neutral, the market shuts down (Section 4.6).

The part of the gradient that a speculator with market power leaves out of the price is his own choice, and it differs from both residuals of Table 23.1: it applies to information already in hand, in a model with no trading costs, and it needs positive noise trading; it holds within Kyle’s model, and this book does not state it as a general result (our judgment). Outside the relaxations that Table 5.1 lists for this chapter, the speculator’s price impact drops the price taker, the default that no trader’s orders move the price.11

23.5.2 Decisive information

In Milgrom’s (1981) auction of a fixed number of objects, as bidders multiply, the price converges in probability to the most that any bidder would pay under perfect information if and only if, in this book’s reading of his condition, some signal can be arbitrarily strong evidence for a higher value (our judgment; Sections 14.8 and 16.6); other models have other conditions, each with its exceptions (Table 14.2). In Grossman and Stiglitz’s example signals are normal and unbounded in strength, so decisive evidence is possible there, and the cost of information at an interior equilibrium keeps their price short of full revelation (our judgment; Section 16.6). Whether a signal can be decisive is a condition for full aggregation within particular models, and this book does not state it as a general law (our judgment).12

23.6 Decay and persistence in three markets

Two studies measure decay or persistence and one model suggests a test, each in a different market.

23.6.1 Volatility after one-sided runs

Section 21.2.5 gave Lee’s model of sequential trade with a fixed transaction cost, in which partial cascades hide information as silence and an avalanche releases it, with Lee’s own caveat that the price movement could be smaller in reality than the model predicts. His Proposition 5 gives the variance of the price, given the state, after a trigger that ends a partial cascade of NN periods (Box 23.3), and Lee writes that the computation confirms that the volatility increases with the length of the cascade preceding the avalanche. Corrected for a misprinted factor, the formula bears this out on average over the two states (our derivation), and given the state it can fall when the price already leans toward the true state (our calculation; Box 23.3).

Lee’s testable hypothesis, within his model, is that after a longer period of a monotone movement the market is more likely to have greater volatility, since the number of agents with hidden information increases with the length of the cascade.

A test, this book’s design and not run, would take three steps (our judgment). It would define a one-sided run, a price that moves the same way period after period, and its length from quote midpoints in clock time (Section 23.4.3), and measure realized volatility after the run ends with the number of trades and their size, since Jones, Kaul and Lipson (1994) show that volatility is correlated with the number of transactions and less with the size of each order (as reported by Lee); pooled over many runs whose outcomes occur as often as prices say, that volatility estimates the state-averaged quantity of Box 23.3 (our judgment). And it would set Lee’s footprint, many traders who had stayed out each placing an order, against coordination and synchronization (Chapter 21), which can also give sharp moves after long runs. The test asks only how large the move is and says nothing about its direction.

The model drops frictionless trading through its fixed cost and, outside the relaxations that Table 5.1 lists for this chapter, no observational learning and no learning from prices (Section 21.2.5).13

23.6.2 Reversals that faded

Section 10.7 and the opening of Chapter 13 gave Tetlock’s (2011) finding, in public news about S&P 500 firms from 1984 to 2004, that market reactions to stale news, stories that repeat what has already appeared, partially reverse in the next week (working-paper version).

Starting from the observation that behavioral anomalies often diminish over time as arbitrageurs seeking high risk-adjusted returns discover them, Tetlock redoes his regressions for two nearly equal subperiods, 1984 to 1994 and 1995 to 2004, holding the reversal portfolio over the second to fifth days (working-paper version). The reversal after public news is much lower in the second half in 9 out of 10 possible comparisons in his Table XI, stale and other news alike, yet stays much larger after stale news than after other news for all five of his proxies for staleness in both subperiods (working-paper version). After news that is not stale, the reversals disappear in the second subperiod in four out of five panels (working-paper version).

His readings are tentative (our judgment): these results, he writes, “suggest that arbitrage has not yet eliminated the stale news reversal”, and of the other news, “It is possible that trading by rational arbitrageurs has eliminated the reversal after these other news events” (working-paper version). The paper does not test which arbitrage or which friction weakened the reversal or left it in place, so this book records the decay in the second half as a pattern the source does not explain (our judgment).14

23.6.3 A prediction-market bias by year

Section 18.3 gave Bürgi, Deng and Whelan’s (2026) regression of the outcome less the price on the price for Kalshi contracts within 10 days of closing, from 2021 through April 2025, with a slope ψ\psi of 0.034 over the whole sample (working-paper version), a favorite–longshot bias, in which favorites win more often than their prices suggest and longshots less often.

Running it year by year in their Table 9, the authors reject in each year the null hypothesis that prices are unbiased forecasts of outcomes, and they see some evidence of a weakening of the bias because the slope for 2025 is smaller and less statistically significant (working-paper version). The slopes for 2021 to 2025 are 0.041, 0.023, 0.036, 0.048 and 0.021 (working-paper version), which do not fall year after year (our judgment), 2024’s being the largest (working-paper version). The 2025 data end in April (working-paper version), before the paper’s first version of September 2025, so the smaller 2025 slope cannot be an effect of the paper’s own publication (our judgment).

The authors ask why investors have not come in to undercut the profits of makers, traders who post orders for others to accept, on contracts costing 50 cents and over, whose return after commission was 1.9% in their sample, and give three potential reasons: small volumes; riskiness, those contracts’ return having a standard deviation of 33%, which may have led rational investors to see the positive expected returns as no more than an appropriate return for the risk; and participants who may not have been aware of the bias (working-paper version). They want to see whether the patterns persist now that they are publicly documented, or disappear as other financial market anomalies tend to once publicized (working-paper version).

Small volumes limit capacity, and riskiness, read as the authors read it, earns compensation for risk, which Table 18.1 counts in the risk premium; the bias that the two leave in place is the arbitrage-cost residual (our judgment). Awareness is what publication can spread, so a decay after publication would be self-defeating knowledge of the kind in Section 23.1, which the 2025 figures cannot test (our judgment).15

23.8 A trader’s checklist

None of these questions says what to buy or sell, and good answers do not make a trade safe (our judgment).

  1. Is the pattern published, and is it an average? Check: McLean and Pontiff’s 97 published predictors, in US-listed stocks with data ending in 2013 and before trading costs, earned less after their samples ended and less again after publication, and in both later periods the spread of their measured means, sampling error included, exceeded the mean, so the average does not tell what any one predictor earned (our judgment; the opening and Section 23.1).
  2. Which residual is left? Check: after publication a rule costs almost nothing to learn, though applying it takes data that are not free, so the cost of information is likely a small part of what remains (our inference); McLean and Pontiff’s design cannot tell a cost of arbitrage from compensation for risk (our judgment; Section 23.2).
  3. Can the position be held until the price converges? Check: set the holding period against the convergence time (our judgment). De Long et al.’s noise trader risk needs noise traders kept, finite horizons, no last period without fundamental risk and unbounded prices, and weakens as horizons lengthen, though perhaps little with enough dividend risk and persistent misperceptions; Shleifer and Vishny’s result needs arbitrageurs fully invested in extreme circumstances, funds that follow past performance and segmented markets, and a substantial number of arbitrageurs with resources independent of past performance can undo it (working-paper version); Abreu and Brunnermeier’s risk-neutral arbitrageurs, aware one after another so that the mispricing is never common knowledge, with capped positions, pay for each period held and typically delay unless the mispricing exceeds bounds set by their dispersion of opinion and other parameters (Section 23.3).
  4. Is the predictability inside the costs? Check: with frictions, a predictable move without risk can be no arbitrage while it stays within the band that spreads or short-sale costs set (Section 5.4); Bernard and Thomas’s drift, in NYSE and AMEX stocks from 1974 to 1986, appears to be within the small investor’s round-trip costs of the post-1975 era of negotiated commissions, and the authors raise difficult questions for a transactions-cost explanation (Section 23.4).
  5. Was the decay measured on quotes or on trades? Check: a decay estimated from transaction prices can carry the spread’s bias; in Easley and O’Hara’s model quotes, which have no optional sampling problem, may be the better data, and Chapter 24 gives the method (our judgment; Section 23.4.3).

23.9 Summary

  • McLean and Pontiff’s 97 published predictors, long-short portfolios of US-listed stocks in data ending in 2013, before trading costs, earned on average 0.582% a month in sample, 0.402% after the samples ended and 0.264% after publication; in both later periods the spread of their measured means, sampling error included, exceeded the mean, so the averages do not tell what any one predictor earned (our judgment; the opening).
  • Some traders likely knew a predictor before publication, so the 26% fall after the samples, by the paper’s regression, is an upper bound on statistical bias and the 32% a lower bound on the effect of publication; the 42% or so that remains by the same measure, or 45% by the Table I means (our calculation), is unlikely to be much compensation for the cost of information, since a published rule costs almost nothing to learn, though applying it takes data that are not free (our inference), and the design cannot tell a cost of arbitrage from a risk premium in it (our judgment; Sections 23.1 and 23.2).
  • In Grossman and Stiglitz’s example, with constant absolute risk aversion, normal variables, price takers, supply noise and a signal of fixed precision bought or not, at an equilibrium with some traders informed and others not, cheaper information makes the price more informative and the informed know aca c nats beyond the price, which shrinks with the cost (our derivation); this information-cost residual differs from the arbitrage-cost residual, which stays when information is free and shrinks only as trading, short selling, funding and risk bearing get cheaper (our judgment; Section 23.2).
  • Each limit to arbitrage has its own premises: De Long et al.’s noise trader risk needs noise traders kept, finite horizons, no last period without fundamental risk and unbounded prices, and weakens as horizons lengthen, though perhaps little with enough dividend risk and persistent misperceptions, and Shleifer and Vishny’s risk-neutral arbitrageurs fail when fully invested in extreme circumstances, funded by past performance in segmented markets, a failure that enough arbitrageurs with resources independent of past performance can undo (working-paper version); Abreu and Brunnermeier’s risk-neutral arbitrageurs, aware one after another, with capped positions and a cost for each period held, typically delay unless the mispricing exceeds bounds set by their dispersion of opinion, and in all three the holding period against the convergence time is the common ingredient, which meets holding until disclosure (our judgment; Section 23.3).
  • With frictions, a predictable move without risk can be no arbitrage while it stays within the band, whose width varies considerably across securities in Jouini and Kallal’s words and in the figures of their time that they report (as reported by Jouini and Kallal); Bernard and Thomas’s drift in NYSE and AMEX stocks from 1974 to 1986 appears to lie within the small investor’s round-trip costs of the post-1975 era, and the authors raise difficult questions for a transactions-cost explanation (Section 23.4).
  • In Kyle’s single auction with normal variables, exponential utility and positive noise trading, a speculator with market power keeps part of the gradient out of the price on purpose, the price revealing at most half of the informed speculators’ private precision (our judgment; Section 23.5.1).
  • In Tetlock’s news about S&P 500 firms the reversal fell from 1984–1994 to 1995–2004 in 9 of 10 comparisons, stale news included, while staying larger after stale news than after other news, and Bürgi, Deng and Whelan’s slope for Kalshi contracts within 10 days of closing was smaller in 2025 (working-paper version); Tetlock tests no explanation for the decay, the five yearly slopes do not fall steadily, and the 2025 data end in April, before the paper appeared (our judgment; Sections 23.6.2 and 23.6.3).

Box 23.2 De Long et al.’s pricing rule

Noise traders are present in measure μ\mu and sophisticated investors in measure 1−μ1 - \mu; the mean misperception ρ*\rho^* measures the noise traders’ average bullishness, and σρ2\sigma_\rho^2 is the variance of their misperceptions of the expected return per unit of the risky asset (working-paper version). Utility is U=−e−(2γ)wU = -e^{-(2\gamma) w}, and the authors call γ\gamma the coefficient of absolute risk aversion (working-paper version). With rr the safe asset’s return and ρt\rho_t this period’s misperception, the price of the asset without fundamental risk is

pt=1+μ(ρt−ρ*)1+r+μρ*r−(2γ)μ2σρ2r(1+r)2p_t = 1 + \frac{\mu(\rho_t - \rho^{*})}{1 + r} + \frac{\mu \rho^{*}}{r} - \frac{(2\gamma) \mu^{2} \sigma_{\rho}^{2}}{r (1 + r)^{2}}

(working-paper version), whose last term pays sophisticated investors for the risk that noise traders turn bearish (Section 18.8). As the distribution of ρt\rho_t converges to a point mass at zero, the price converges to its fundamental value of one (working-paper version): ρt\rho_t, ρ*\rho^* and σρ2\sigma_\rho^2 go to zero, and with them every term but the first (our derivation), the formula’s form of the premise that noise traders must be kept (our judgment).17

Box 23.3 Lee’s Proposition 5, corrected

Lee’s Proposition 5 gives the variance of pt+1p_{t+1} given state ss after a trigger, with λn\lambda_n the likelihood of state B against state G given a sample of size NN with nn signals H. In the printed formula the second probability factor, raised to N−nN - n, repeats the first, the probability of H. Given the state, the mean of pt+1p_{t+1} is in general not ptp_t, so the sum is the mean squared move from ptp_t, which the paper calls a variance. Averaged over the two states with weights ptp_t and 1−pt1 - p_t, the sum is the variance of pt+1p_{t+1} under the market’s own probabilities, under which pt+1p_{t+1} has mean ptp_t; a longer cascade reveals more, so that variance grows with NN for every ptp_t.
