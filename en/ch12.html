Chapter 12 Announcements and New Public Information

Bernard and Thomas (1989) measured post-earnings-announcement drift, prices moving for months in the direction of an earnings surprise, in 84,792 firm-quarters of data for NYSE and AMEX firms from 1974 to 1986. Their surprise is the error of a forecast of a quarter’s earnings from the firm’s own past earnings, scaled by how widely such errors varied over an earlier period. Their Table 5 ranks firms by this quarter’s surprise and follows a position long the best tenth and short the worst tenth over just the five trading days up to and including the next quarter’s announcement. Those portfolios are completely identified from information available about three months earlier, so if prices fully reflected public information their returns should, on average, hold no surprise. The position earned a cumulative abnormal return, the return beyond what is normal for such stocks, of 2.14% for small firms, 1.33% for medium and 0.68% for large, or 40%, 29% and 25% of the drift over the 60 trading days after this quarter’s announcement. The five days are only 8% as long as the 60, so a smooth drift would have put 8% of it there. The authors conclude that a disproportionately large fraction of the drift is concentrated in the few days preceding and including the next quarter’s earnings announcement. These are the authors’ measurements, and this book has not yet tested an explanation of them on data of its own.1

The root model of Chapter 5 assumes that all public news is there at the start: one message from a constant external source, and nothing announced later. Table 5.1 in Section 5.8 lists this default, one-time injection, with the others, and this chapter drops it. With attention limited among those who have not heard (Section 12.2), the chapter also drops unlimited attention together with one-time injection, which is why Table 5.1 lists this chapter for it in parentheses. Section 12.9 drops first-order knowledge for announcements (our judgment; Table 5.1). The model of Section 12.3 also drops permanent spreading, the default that those who know never stop telling, outside the relaxations that Table 5.1 lists for this chapter.

12.1 News that arrives after the start

Public news can arrive after the start as scheduled announcements, such as quarterly earnings, as news stories and as academic publication. The external source of the root model then changes over time, with shocks of news, the announcement cut-off, the cut-off that a scheduled announcement puts on spreading, and pre-announcement drift, a price moving before an announcement toward where it will stand after it (our judgment). A source that keeps sending the same message at a constant rate stays inside the default (our judgment; Section 5.2). Section 8.5 left to this chapter a background intensity that varies with time, as in the daily pattern of activity; this chapter takes up only its sharpest form, an announcement at a set time (our judgment).

Public has two meanings here: available, when anyone could look the news up, and seen, when someone has (our judgment; Section 1.6.5 and Chapter 10).

The first reaction and the full reaction also differ. A widely broadcast report can draw a first reaction within seconds, as Busse and Green (2002) found for positive stock reports in CNBC’s midday segment in 2000 (Sections 5.5 and 10.9), while the full reaction to earnings news can take months. Bernard and Thomas write that most of the drift occurs during the first 60 trading days, about three months, after the announcement, with little evidence of statistically significant drift beyond 180 trading days. Yet in their Table 1 the difference between the best and the worst surprises is significantly negative over days 241 to 300 for small, medium and large firms alike, a result their text does not take up (our judgment). Section 10.2 gives how much of the response to earnings news comes late; this chapter prints no new number from Busse and Green or from DellaVigna and Pollet (2009), and the scope boxes of Sections 5.5 and 10.2 state their limits.

An announcement is a disclosure. Settlement, when a contract pays out on how the fact turned out, clears all information whoever is watching, while a public disclosure reaches the price only through those who see it (our judgment; Section 5.4).2

12.2 The cut-off and who is watching

DellaVigna and Pollet model a share of investors who are distracted and do not observe the signal; given limits to arbitrage in the form of risk aversion, a larger distracted share shrinks the immediate response of prices and magnifies the delayed response while the combined response is not affected (working-paper version; Section 10.2).

A scheduled announcement informs those who are watching when it comes out, so how much of the spread it cuts off depends on the share who are attentive (our judgment).

Word of mouth before the announcement adds a second share. Suppose a share I(T)I(T) has heard by word of mouth before an announcement at time TT. If a share aa of the population is attentive, and whether a person is attentive does not depend on whether she has already heard, then right after the announcement I(T)+a[1−I(T)]I(T) + a[1 - I(T)] know (our derivation; Box 12.1). DellaVigna and Pollet’s model has no word of mouth, and putting the two together is this book’s step (our judgment).

Word of mouth can also leave people who never hear. In Daley and Kendall’s (1965) rumor model, under the premises listed in Section 6.3, a closed population, homogeneous mixing, no outside source, one first spreader and a spreader who stops at the first meeting with someone who already knows, their deterministic version for a large population leaves a share of about 0.203 who never hear. That is a mathematical result, and no source of this book compares it with any real message’s coverage (our judgment). Those who never hear can learn the news only from a public disclosure, so an announcement can still move the price after word of mouth has run its course (our judgment).

Section 6.5 drew a testable implication: the price reaction left for an announcement depends on the stopping rule, and under the rumor model, once the rumor has died out, little on how early word of mouth began (our derivation). A test needs the volume of discussion on social media and prices around announcements; this book has not assembled such data or checked whether free sources hold them, so the implication remains untested (our judgment).3

12.3 The shape of spreading times

Take two pieces of news that reach a few people five days before an announcement, each passed on one day after hearing, on average: a short headline, which some pass on at once and some much later, so that the most likely delay is zero, and a long filing, which everyone needs about a day to read first. How many have heard each by the day of the announcement?

In Karrer and Newman’s (2010) example of SIR on a network, which Section 8.3 describes, an exponential time of transmission and a “top hat”, in which a person becomes infectious a fixed time after infection and recovers at a later fixed time, given the same mean and total probability of transmission, reach the same outcome at late times along different courses, the top hat in waves.

This book’s model asks the same question in a simpler setting (our derivation; Box 12.1). The population is large and homogeneously mixing; 1% hear at time 0, and no one hears from an outside source afterwards. A person’s spreading time is the delay from hearing to passing the news on. Each person who has heard spreads her tellings over time according to its distribution and tells R=2R = 2 people in all on average, counted in a population where no one has heard. After that she tells no one, so spreading ends of itself, as in SIR (Chapter 6), and the model also drops the root model’s permanent spreading. Both shapes have a mean spreading time of 1, the unit of time: an exponential shape for the headline and, for the filing, a top hat in which each person tells at a constant rate from 0.8 to 1.2 after hearing. RR, the seed, the window and the date of the announcement are this book’s choice.

At the announcement, at T=5T = 5, 0.588 of the population has heard in the exponential case and 0.325 in the top hat case, and both would reach 0.800 in the end (our calculation; Figure 12.1). The announcement has about 0.41 of the population left to inform in the first case and about 0.68 in the second (our calculation), and with limited attention it informs only the attentive share of them (our derivation; Section 12.2). In this model the share who hear in the end depends only on RR and the seed, whatever the shape, while the share who have heard by a cut-off depends also on the mean and on the whole shape of the spreading time (our derivation; Box 12.1).

In this model, news that must be digested before it is passed on, like the long filing, spreads in waves, and at any cut-off up to about 11.6 mean spreading times after the start fewer people have heard it (our calculation). An announcement at such a date is the first news for more of them (our judgment; Section 8.3). Figure 12.1 counts hearing under homogeneous mixing and does not reproduce Karrer and Newman’s count of the infected on a network (our judgment).4

12.4 Two ways a drift ends

Take an earnings surprise announced on a Friday. In DellaVigna and Pollet’s data, the extra drift after Friday announcements becomes more readily apparent about 30 trading days after the announcement (working-paper version; Section 10.2). Two mechanisms can end such a drift, and this book keeps them apart (our judgment).

  • Renewed attention. Investors who were distracted later look at the news on their own. DellaVigna and Pollet note that in their model stock returns are the same whether the distracted investors observe the signal one period late or never (working-paper version), so the model does not say when they look back (our judgment).
  • A cut-off by the next scheduled announcement. The next quarter’s announcement is a new disclosure. It informs those watching at the time, and its earnings show what this quarter’s earnings implied for the next quarter’s (our judgment).

Bernard and Thomas’s Table 5, in the opening of this chapter, is direct evidence for the second mechanism, though it does not measure how much drift renewed attention ends between announcements (our judgment).

DellaVigna and Pollet’s evidence does not settle which mechanism ends the extra drift after Friday announcements (our judgment). Their delayed response runs from the 2nd to the 75th trading day after the announcement (working-paper version). For a firm that reports every quarter, the next announcement comes about three months later, some 60 trading days, so the window usually takes in the next announcement too (our judgment). One interpretation the authors offer is that investors do not realize that they have ignored an announcement until they revisit their investment decisions for other reasons, such as later news about the same company (working-paper version). They align returns on this quarter’s announcement, so their figures do not show how much of the extra drift each firm’s next announcement ends (our judgment).

A test, which this book has not run, can compare how far a drift moves between announcements with how far it moves in the days around the next one, or firms whose next announcement comes early with firms whose next one comes late, to see whether the end of the drift moves with the calendar (our judgment).

When a drift ends and why it lags are separate questions. Bernard and Thomas read Table 5 as consistent with a market that fails to recognize the full implications of current earnings for future earnings. That is a reason for the lag, one of the mechanisms of Section 12.5; the cut-off concerns when the drift ends (our judgment).5

12.5 Three mechanisms of underreaction

Underreaction means that a price at first moves less than the news warrants and then keeps moving the same way. On ordinary weekdays the level of the delayed response mixes the mechanisms of this section with the timing of cut-offs, so it does not say how many investors are distracted on an ordinary day (our judgment). The difference between Fridays and other weekdays isolates the extra delayed response on Fridays, provided the other mechanisms and the calendar of cut-offs are the same on both kinds of day (our judgment). Even then, in DellaVigna and Pollet’s model the ratio on each kind of day gives only a lower bound on that day’s share of distracted investors (working-paper version; Section 10.2).

At least three mechanisms can produce underreaction to public news, and each comes with a prediction that points to it more than to the other two (our judgment; Table 12.1).

  • Distraction, in the observation layer: some people do not see the news. In DellaVigna and Pollet’s model, if more are distracted on Fridays, there should be less immediate response to Friday announcements followed by more delayed response, with the total long-term response unchanged (working-paper version).
  • A wrong model, in the processing layer: people see the news and give it the wrong weight. Bernard and Thomas write that the concentration of the drift around the next announcement is difficult to explain except as prices that fail to recognize fully the serial correlation in seasonally differenced quarterly earnings, that is, how a quarter’s change in earnings from the same quarter a year before carries over into the next quarter’s; limited attention is not among the explanations they weigh (our judgment). They add that this reading is consistent with Foster’s evidence that estimates of unexpected earnings which ignore that serial correlation are more highly correlated with stock returns than those which reflect it (as reported by Bernard and Thomas). In their Table 5 this quarter’s surprise forecasts the average reaction at the next announcement, but distraction cut off by the next announcement (Section 12.4) would put a forecastable reaction there too, so that reaction does not by itself set the wrong model apart (our judgment).
  • Overweighting private signals. In Daniel, Hirshleifer and Subrahmanyam’s (1998) model, whose premises Section 13.4 gives, informed investors underestimate the error variance of their private signal, and prices overreact to private signals and underreact to public ones. With a fixed confidence level, the model predicts no systematic drift after events unrelated to the prior mispricing, while for events selected on the mispricing, drift results from the combination of underreaction and that selection. When confidence rises and falls with outcomes, there is event-based predictability even after events unrelated to the mispricing, in the authors’ words a distinct explanation for post-earnings announcement drift. These are model results, which this book has not tested (our judgment).

This book reads old news replayed around an announcement through Chapter 10’s two layers (our judgment; Section 10.7). In Tetlock’s (2011) news on S&P 500 firms from 1984 to 2004, market reactions to stale stories partially reverse in the next week (working-paper version); the part that reverses fits double counting, a processing bias outside the three mechanisms above, and also a fad or buying drawn in by the news, so these data do not name its cause (our judgment; Chapter 13). Section 10.3 sets out Merton’s (1987) two readings of a rise on old news.6

12.6 Information or only a date

An earnings announcement brings news about a firm’s value. A scheduled event can also move prices before its date with no news about value at all.

In Duffie’s (2010) illustrative model of slow-moving capital, an announcement at date 1 says that a block sale will take place at date 32, an issuance anticipated by all investors, and 80% of investors make trading decisions only once every 32 periods. Because investors know in advance that a large supply will be released, the price declines before the issuance. The parameters are Duffie’s choice, and in the model the sale brings no news about the asset’s payoff (our judgment). Duffie also shows a figure of U.S. Treasury issuances from January 1980 to March 2008, with 332 auctions of 2-year notes, 210 of 5-year notes and 132 of 10-year notes: as his model suggests, the price of a note goes down as the date of the anticipated new supply approaches, and then recovers in subsequent days (as reported by Duffie). The note prices recover after the date, while pre-announcement drift as Section 12.1 defines it moves toward the price after the date, so a move that reverses after a scheduled date fits expected supply better than news leaking out, a case to rule out before reading a move as news (our judgment).

A date known in advance need not help coordination. In Abreu and Brunnermeier’s (2003) model of a bubble, the analysis already allows arbitrageurs to condition on absolute time, so perfectly anticipated events have no impact on the analysis; the authors take up unanticipated synchronizing events separately, as Section 12.9 describes.

A date can also pass with no announcement. Milgrom’s (1981) verifiable-disclosure game, in which the sender can prove what he says, gives the reasoning. His salesman may report or conceal any of the facts but cannot misreport them, a feature that might arise if the information is verifiable by a product demonstration or if there are truth-in-advertising laws. At every sequential equilibrium the salesman uses a strategy of full disclosure, because the buyer suspects that any information withheld is unfavorable. The model assumes that the buyer can detect when the seller is concealing information, and Milgrom leaves unexplored a seller whose number of facts cannot be verified and costly communication. By Milgrom’s reasoning, investors can read a firm’s silence at a date when it would normally report as bad news when its information can be verified, they know that it has the information, and disclosure costs little; when one of these fails, his game does not say how much silence reveals (our judgment). Chapter 15 takes up how a sender chooses when to speak.

Cheap talk, words that cost nothing to say and can be false, is a third case, which Chapter 15 takes up.7

12.7 Publication of trading rules

Academic publication is public news of another kind. McLean and Pontiff (2016) follow 97 predictors of returns from 79 academic studies, and Section 1.6.6 and Chapter 23 give how much less they earned after their original samples ended and after publication, with the bounds that go with those numbers. This section adds the timing. In their estimates, returns stay for the first two years after publication at the level they had from a year after the original sample ended until publication, and then decay further, and the authors caution that statistical power is lower once they partition the results in this way and that theory does not guide them on how to partition. The publication date is only a proxy for when market participants learn about a predictor.

Publication brings no new observation about any firm; what spreads is a rule for turning public data into expected returns (our judgment). In these estimates the fall in returns is larger in the third and fourth years after publication, and after the fifth, than in the first two, which sets publication apart from a scheduled announcement, where those watching learn the news on the day (our judgment). Whether the return that remains is a cost of arbitrage or compensation for risk is a question for Chapter 23, and Chapter 24 maps the evidence on drift.8

12.8 Breaking an information cascade

An information cascade, in Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer and Welch’s (1992) definition, occurs when it is optimal for a person, having observed the actions of those ahead of him, to follow the behavior of the person before him without regard to his own information. Their Result 3 states that the release of a small amount of public information can shatter a long-lasting cascade, where a small amount means a signal less informative than the private signal of a single person; they prove it in the binary signal example of their previous section. In their intuition, cascades aggregate the information of only a few early individuals’ actions, so the public information needs only to offset the information conveyed by the action of the last person before the cascade started, even if millions imitated afterward. Their Proposition 2 adds that if all individuals’ signals are drawn from the same distribution, then after a cascade has begun, all individuals welcome public information.

Releases can also repeat. In a numerical example in which a signal drawn independently from the same distribution as each person’s is sometimes released publicly, and pp is the probability that a signal is high when the true value is one, with p=0.75p = 0.75 the chance of being in the correct cascade at the 1,000th person is 0.81 without public releases, 0.86 with one release per 1,000 people on average, and 0.98 with ten, numbers from one example of their model (our judgment).

Their Result 2 states that the release of public information before the first person’s decision can make some people worse off in an ex ante sense, in an example in which the public information is very noisy (Section 4.7), and the authors add that a highly informative disclosure is likely to benefit everyone. Because that release comes before anyone has acted, this book does not count it as news arriving after the start (our judgment).

Under strategic complementarity a public signal changes what each person expects the others to do, which Section 12.9 takes up (our judgment; Section 11.3.4); Chapter 14 takes up cascades.9

12.9 Coordination and common knowledge

Common knowledge holds when everyone knows something, everyone knows that everyone knows it, and so on without end. Morris and Shin (1998) give the town hall meeting as the canonical arrangement conducive to achieving it: an announcement is made to an audience gathered in a single room, where everyone can observe that all other participants are in an identical position.

Their policy argument starts from currency crises that may be set off by higher-order beliefs, beliefs about what others believe, even though participants believe that the fundamentals are sound. In that case, they write, the policies that will stabilize the market aim to restore transparency, in an attempt to restore common knowledge of the fundamentals, and the most effective means would be a prominent public announcement commonly known to convey information to all relevant participants. This is a verbal argument in their Section III.A, and their model has no public signal (our judgment).

In their model, self-fulfilling currency attacks lead to multiple equilibria when the fundamentals are common knowledge, and a small amount of noise in the speculators’ signals about the fundamentals makes the equilibrium unique. Suppose an announcement makes the fundamentals common knowledge, and they are neither so weak that an attack succeeds whatever others do nor so strong that it fails whatever others do. Then attacking and not attacking are both equilibria, and restoring common knowledge leaves the choice between them open (our derivation). In a normal version with one public and one private signal, there is a single equilibrium in threshold strategies only when the private signal is precise enough relative to the public one, and a public signal precise enough brings several such equilibria back for some values of the public signal (our derivation; Box 12.2). Selecting the good equilibrium is a separate step from restoring common knowledge (our judgment). Coordination turns on what each trader expects the others to know, so this section drops the root model’s first-order knowledge, the default that traders need to know only the fact, as far as announcements go (our judgment). Chapter 21 takes up bubbles, synchronization risk and global games.

Abreu and Brunnermeier (2003) study events of a different kind, which carry no information about fundamentals and serve as pure coordination devices, arriving at a Poisson rate, in their model of arbitrageurs during a bubble. The authors write that these news events facilitate synchronization, so that, in general, news events may have an impact quite out of proportion to their fundamental content. In this generalized setting there is no hope of a unique equilibrium, and the unlimited supply of potential synchronizing events, they write, suggests that which of them participants treat as relevant is a higher-level synchronization problem, with the possibility of multiple equilibria.

An announcement can serve as a coordination device among arbitrageurs, such as short sellers, only where limits to arbitrage are not too tight (our judgment). Ljungqvist and Qian (2016) studied short sellers who posted detailed reports on the firms they targeted, publicly and for free (working-paper version). By publishing, the authors write, the arbitrageurs remove the synchronization risk, the risk an arbitrageur bears when she does not know when others will act (Section 23.3.3), and the publication is essentially a coordination device, yet they see no increase in shorting (working-paper version). The companies were too hard to arbitrage directly for the reports to act as a coordination device in the sense of Abreu and Brunnermeier’s (2002) work on synchronization risk, because the short-sale constraints on them were quite severe (working-paper version). Where limits to arbitrage are much less likely to bind, Zuckerman (2011) found evidence of synchronized arbitrage involving public announcements (as reported by Ljungqvist and Qian; working-paper version).10

12.10 Costs of public information

Of the four ways in which public information can lower welfare in Section 4.7, three bear on announcements here, each under its own premises; the fourth, Hirshleifer’s distributive risk, arises when the true state is announced before any trading.

In Morris and Shin’s (2002) model, agents take actions appropriate to the fundamentals but also have a coordination motive. A parameter rr gives the weight on this second-guessing motive, which is socially inefficient, being zero-sum; a signal is public in the sense that its realization is common knowledge to all agents; and their results take social welfare to be the sum of individual payoffs (working-paper version). In their model, when the agents have no private information, more public information always increases welfare, and when they also have independent sources of information, its effect on welfare is ambiguous (working-paper version). With rr above 0.5, more precise public information is beneficial only when the agents’ private information is not very precise, and if their private information is very precise, any increase in the precision of public information will be harmful (working-paper version). The authors’ rule of thumb is that when the private sector is already very well informed, the official sector would be well advised not to make more information public unless it could be confident of providing public information of very great precision (working-paper version); in their model a large enough gain in public precision raises welfare again (our derivation; Box 12.2). Their model has no price, and carrying it over to a market needs a model of how prices form (our judgment).

In Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer and Welch’s Result 2, in their example, very noisy public information released before the first decision can leave some people worse off in an ex ante sense (Section 12.8). In Duffie, Malamud and Manso’s (2009) model of searching for others to learn from, with proportional search costs, more public information leads to an unambiguous reduction in the sharing of private information, in some cases with a net negative effect on welfare, judged by the utilities of agents as they enter the market. Public signals make entrants better informed, and they may also reduce the incentive to search for more information, slowing down its percolation.

The three models’ answers on when public information helps and when it can hurt turn on different premises: when the information arrives relative to the first action, how precise private information is, how strong the coordination motive is, and whether private information is costly to gather. None of them shows that announcements in general lower welfare (our judgment).11

12.12 A trader’s checklist

None of these questions says what to buy or sell, and good answers do not make a trade safe (our judgment).

  1. Does the announcement carry news about value, or only news of supply on a set date? Check: in Duffie’s illustrative model of slow-moving capital, where 80% of investors trade only once every 32 periods, a block sale that all investors anticipate makes the price decline before the sale; in the Treasury auctions of 1980 to 2008 he reports, note prices went down as new supply approached and recovered in the days after (as reported by Duffie; Section 12.6).
  2. How many had heard before the announcement? Check: in this book’s model of Figure 12.1, where 1% of a homogeneously mixing population hear at the start, no one hears from outside afterwards, and each person tells two others on average and then no one, the same mean spreading time left 0.588 or 0.325 informed five mean spreading times later, for an exponential spreading time or a top hat between 0.8 and 1.2 (our calculation; Section 12.3).
  3. Is the drift waiting for people to look again, or for the next announcement? Check: for NYSE and AMEX firms from 1974 to 1986, Bernard and Thomas found a disproportionately large fraction of the drift in the few days up to and including the next quarter’s announcement, which this book reads as direct evidence for a cut-off by that announcement; returns aligned only on this quarter’s announcement do not show how much drift the next one ends (our judgment; Section 12.4).
  4. Which mechanism of underreaction is at work, and does its prediction hold? Check: drift that follows the calendar of attention points to distraction, returns that line up more closely with surprises from a forecast ignoring the carry-over of earnings changes point to a wrong model, and no drift after events unrelated to the prior mispricing fits Daniel, Hirshleifer and Subrahmanyam’s model with a fixed confidence level; a reaction at the next announcement that this quarter’s surprise forecasts fits both distraction cut off by that announcement and a wrong model (our judgment; Table 12.1 and Section 12.5).
  5. Is this public signal breaking a cascade, coordinating action or crowding out private information? Check: in Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer and Welch’s binary example, public information less informative than one person’s private signal can shatter a long-lasting cascade (Section 12.8). Coordinating arbitrageurs needs limits to arbitrage that are not too tight (our judgment; Section 12.9). In Morris and Shin’s model, which has no price, with a zero-sum coordination motive of weight above one half, a public signal that is common knowledge, welfare taken as the sum of payoffs and very precise private information, more precise public information lowers welfare unless it can be made very precise (working-paper version; Section 12.10). In Duffie, Malamud and Manso’s model, with proportional search costs, public signals reduce the sharing of private information and in some cases lower welfare judged at entry (Section 12.10).

12.13 Summary

  • Public news that arrives after the start drops one-time injection, and a scheduled announcement informs only those who are watching, so how much of the spread it cuts off depends on the share who are attentive (our judgment; Section 12.2).
  • In this book’s model, with 1% of a homogeneously mixing population hearing at the start, nobody hearing from outside later and each person telling two others on average and then no one, the same mean spreading time left 0.588 informed at a cut-off five mean spreading times later with an exponential spreading time and 0.325 with a top hat between 0.8 and 1.2, though both reach 0.800 in the end (our calculation; Section 12.3).
  • Two mechanisms can end a drift, renewed attention and a cut-off by the next scheduled announcement; Bernard and Thomas’s Table 5, on NYSE and AMEX firms from 1974 to 1986, is direct evidence for the second, and returns aligned only on this quarter’s announcement, as in DellaVigna and Pollet’s study, do not show how much drift the next announcement ends (our judgment; Section 12.4).
  • At least three mechanisms can produce underreaction to public news, distraction, a wrong model of earnings and overweighting of private signals; each comes with a prediction that points to it more than to the other two, while a reaction at the next announcement fits both of the first two (our judgment; Section 12.5).
  • A scheduled announcement that brings news about value differs from a scheduled supply that brings none, which in Duffie’s illustrative model of slow-moving capital, where most investors trade only at intervals, lowers the price before the date (our judgment; Section 12.6).
  • In Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer and Welch’s binary example, public information less informative than one person’s private signal can shatter a long-lasting cascade, while in their example of very noisy public information released before the first decision, some people end up worse off in an ex ante sense (Section 12.8).
  • An announcement can coordinate arbitrageurs only where limits to arbitrage are not too tight (our judgment); in Morris and Shin’s model, which has no price, with a zero-sum coordination motive of weight above one half, a public signal that is common knowledge, welfare as the sum of payoffs and very precise private information, more precise public information lowers welfare unless it can be made very precise (working-paper version; Sections 12.9 and 12.10).

Box 12.2 Public precision and multiple equilibria

Welfare in Morris and Shin’s model. With α\alpha the precision of the public signal, β\beta that of each agent’s private signal and rr the weight on the coordination motive, the authors find that when β>α/[(2r−1)(1−r)]\beta > \alpha/[(2r - 1)(1 - r)], welfare is decreasing in the precision of public information (working-paper version). For 1/2<r<11/2 < r < 1, the case of Section 12.10, the factor (2r−1)(1−r)(2r - 1)(1 - r) is largest at r=3/4r = 3/4, where it equals 1/81/8, so there the condition needs private signals more than eight times as precise as the public one (our calculation). From their equations (3.2) and (3.4) for welfare and its derivative (working-paper version), which we have rederived, we derive that once public precision passes the bound α=(2r−1)(1−r)β\alpha = (2r - 1)(1 - r)\beta, welfare rises with α\alpha and, as α\alpha grows without bound, tends to its highest value, above its value with no public signal (our derivation).

A normal global game. Speculators decide whether to attack a regime whose fundamental is θ\theta. All see a public signal of θ\theta with precision α\alpha, and each sees a private signal with precision β\beta. The regime gives up when the share attacking is at least θ\theta; a successful attack pays 1, and attacking costs tt, with 0<t<10 < t < 1. For every value of the public signal there is a single equilibrium in threshold strategies, in which each speculator attacks when her private signal falls below a threshold, if and only if β≥α2/(2π)\beta \ge \alpha^2/(2\pi), and as β→∞\beta \to \infty the regime’s critical fundamental tends to 1−t1 - t (our derivation). When α\alpha exceeds 2πβ\sqrt{2\pi\beta}, several such equilibria return for some values of the public signal (our derivation). The bound rests on the regime comparing the share attacking with θ\theta one for one and on fixed payoffs. Chapter 21 takes up global games and their premises.13
