Chapter 8 Stochastic Propagation and Hawkes Processes

The midprice of a futures contract is the average of its best bid and best ask prices, the same quantity as the quote midpoint of Section 3.6. Filimonov and Sornette (2012) take the E-mini S&P 500 futures contract from January 5, 1998 to August 29, 2010, and within regular trading hours they count 24,309,652 changes of its midprice. They write that a Poisson process, in which events arrive independently at a fixed average rate, cannot describe the stylized facts of real order flows, the first of which is that orders arrive in clusters. Hardiman, Bercot and Bouchaud (2013) model the arrival of the contract’s midprice changes as a self-exciting Hawkes process, a process in which each event raises the rate of the events that follow.

The branching ratio of such a process is the average number of later events that one event triggers directly. The two papers estimate it for the same contract and reach different pictures. With a kernel, the function that sets how strongly a past event raises the rate later on, that decays exponentially and windows of 10 to 30 minutes, Filimonov and Sornette find the branching ratio rising over the years; with a power-law kernel and two-month periods, Hardiman, Bercot and Bouchaud find that it appears to change little from 1998 to 2011 (Section 8.6). Three limits apply to every estimate. The estimates count changes of the midprice, events at the price layer, where quotes change and trades happen, with no record of who heard a story from whom; the branching ratio is estimated from the same series it is meant to explain; and every value of it depends on the choice of kernel, of window and of the background intensity, the rate at which events arrive that owe nothing to earlier ones.1

Apart from Vosoughi, Roy and Aral’s cascades, the Twitter data to which Weng et al. calibrate their model, Tetlock’s counts of news about firms and the E-mini estimates, the chapter rests on studies known only as others report them and on models with no data behind them (Section 8.7).

8.1 Chance in the spread of news

The root model of Chapter 5 follows the average path of a spread: its equations contain no chance, and contacts happen at fixed rates, evenly in time. Table 5.1 in Section 5.8 lists these two parts together as one default assumption, deterministic mean field, and this chapter drops both. It adds chance, which makes the spread stochastic propagation, a random process whose outcome is a family of possible paths, and in Section 8.3 it lets contacts come in bursts and rates change. Section 8.5 also drops part of one-time injection, the default that the news is present at the start, with a constant external source and no later announcements.

The basic reproduction number R0R_0 is the average number of people one spreader infects directly in a population where no one has the idea yet. Pastor-Satorras et al. (2015) note that R0>1R_0 > 1 is necessary and sufficient for an outbreak in a deterministic model and only necessary in a random model, since chance can end the spread while spreaders are few (Section 6.2).

What chance changes depends on how spreaders stop (Section 6.1). Under permanent spreading, where those who know never stop telling, and homogeneous mixing, where anyone may meet anyone and two who meet have not drawn on the same sources, the spread cannot die out early, and chance only shifts the curve in time (our derivation; Section 5.2). Under spontaneous stopping, where a spreader falls silent after a while whatever the people she meets already know, with constant rates and homogeneous mixing, a cascade, the chain of tellings that starts from one seed, can die out early, with a chance that does not shrink as the population grows (our derivation; Section 6.2). How large that chance is depends on how the number each spreader tells is distributed (Section 8.2).

Under contact-triggered stopping, a spreader stops on meeting people who already know. Daley and Kendall’s (1965) rumor model shows what chance does there, in a closed population under homogeneous mixing where she stops at the first such meeting (Section 6.3). In their random version, with NN people who have not heard at the start, the number who never hear has a distribution with two humps. A small hump near N−1N - 1 is associated with the risk that the rumor will die out almost at once, a chance of about 0.5/N0.5/N. With the small hump removed, the variance is about 0.31N0.31N and the mean number who never hear has the leading term 0.203188N0.203188N, which the authors find very encouraging, since this coefficient is the share who never hear in the deterministic model.

In a large population, then, chance in this model brings fluctuations of the order of N\sqrt{N}, small beside NN, and an early death whose chance is of the order of 1/N1/N, and the deterministic version already gives the leading term of the main hump’s mean (our judgment). The two-humped distribution belongs to contact-triggered stopping, and the early death whose chance does not shrink as the population grows belongs to spontaneous stopping, both under homogeneous mixing; neither result carries over to the other branch (our judgment; Section 6.4).2

8.2 Takeoff and the size of cascades

Whether a story spreads widely and how far it goes are two different quantities. The takeoff probability is the chance that a single seed starts a large outbreak, and in a large population it is one minus the extinction probability, the chance that the cascade dies out by itself (our judgment; Box 6.2). The final size is the share of the population that a large outbreak reaches in the end.

Take the susceptible–infected–recovered (SIR) model, in which spreaders stop on their own and never start again, in a large population under homogeneous mixing, with R0=2R_0 = 2 and one seed, early on while almost no one has heard. If every spreader has the same fixed infectious period, the time she stays able to pass the story on, the number of new spreaders that each one starts follows a Poisson distribution with mean 2, the cascade dies out with a chance of 0.2031879, the smaller root of x=e2(x−1)x = e^{2(x-1)}, and the takeoff probability is about 0.80 (our derivation; our calculation). If instead every spreader stops at a constant rate, so that the infectious period follows an exponential distribution, the simplification Shiller’s SIR makes (working-paper version), that number follows a geometric distribution with the same mean, the extinction probability is 1/R01/R_0 and the takeoff probability is 0.5 (our derivation). The chance that the seed starts no new spreader at all is e−2e^{-2}, about 0.135, in the first case and 1/(1+R0)=1/31/(1 + R_0) = 1/3 in the second (our calculation). With the same mean, the geometric number is more often zero and more often large (our derivation).

The final size is about 0.80 in both cases (our calculation), since in a large population under homogeneous mixing it depends on the infectious period only through the mean number each spreader infects, and a fixed infectious period gives the final-state equation of Box 6.1 (our derivation). With a fixed infectious period the takeoff probability therefore equals the final size, and with an exponential one it is lower (our derivation).

That is the direction of an inequality that Kenah and Robins (2007) proved in a different setting (our judgment), the SIR model studied by Newman on a random network, where bond percolation with the mean probability of transmission overestimates the takeoff probability when the infectious period varies, because all of one spreader’s transmissions depend on her infectious period (Box 6.2; Section 7.3). In that network SIR the takeoff probability is never above the final size, and equals it only when epidemics have probability zero or the infectious period is constant. People talk about a story for different lengths of time (our judgment), but no source this book has read proves that the more the infectious period varies, the lower the takeoff probability, and this section compares two distributions only.3

Figure 8.1 shows how large cascades get, from one seed, when each event has a Poisson number of direct offspring, the events it triggers itself, with the same mean nn, the branching ratio. This is the cluster of one immigrant, an event that comes from outside, together with all its descendants in a Hawkes process (Section 8.5). It is also the early stage of SIR with a fixed infectious period in a large population under homogeneous mixing, while almost no one has heard, with n=R0n = R_0, so that n=1n = 1 is the threshold (our derivation). In a finite population it holds only for cascades much smaller than the population (our judgment).

The size of a cascade counts its events, the seed included. At n=1n = 1, the critical case, a cascade ends with probability one, though its size fluctuates widely. Most cascades stay small, yet the share with at least ℓ\ell events falls only about as 1/ℓ1/\sqrt{\ell}, with about 26% reaching 10 events, 8.0% reaching 100, 2.5% reaching 1,000 and 0.80% reaching 10,000 (our calculation). That fall is too slow for the mean size to stay finite, so the mean has no bound (our derivation).

Below n=1n = 1 each curve first follows the critical one and then falls steeply past a size that grows as nn nears 1, about 5, 43 and 773 events at nn = 0.5, 0.8 and 0.95 (our calculation; Box 8.1). At n=0.95n = 0.95 about 4.1% of cascades reach 100 events and about 0.15% reach 1,000, but only about 7 in 10 billion reach 10,000 (our calculation). The mean size is 1/(1−n)1/(1 - n), which is 2, 5 and 20 events at nn = 0.5, 0.8 and 0.95 (our calculation; Box 8.1). A branching ratio of 0.95 instead of 1 thus cuts the share of cascades that reach 1,000 events about 17 times, from 2.5% to 0.15% (our calculation). The branching ratios estimated in markets (Section 8.6) count changes of the midprice and hold no record of who has heard a story, so the figure does not turn them into distances that news travels (our judgment).4

8.3 Bursts and changing rates

The second part of deterministic mean field has contacts at fixed rates, evenly in time, as in a Poisson process. In the review of Pastor-Satorras et al., the times between social contacts follow heavy-tailed distributions, heavy-tailed and skewed timing being “more the norm than the exception”, which has drawn interest to burstiness, the degree to which events come in bunches. One of the most remarkable effects of such bursts on spreading, the review says, is a substantial slowing down, first seen in a model of email worms spreading among email users, a susceptible–infected (SI) model in which no one stops spreading (as reported by Pastor-Satorras et al.). The review adds that the situation is not completely clear, since other work suggests acceleration, so a model that spaces contacts evenly can be wrong about speed in either direction (our judgment).

Karrer and Newman (2010) note that a constant rate of recovery implies an exponential distribution of the time a person stays infected, whose most probable value is zero, while most diseases last a roughly constant time. In an example of SIR on a network built by the configuration model, which gives each person a number of links and joins the links at random, with a Poisson distribution of links (Section 7.3; Box 7.2), they compare that exponential case with a “top hat”, in which a person becomes infectious a fixed time after infection and recovers at a later fixed time, chosen to give the same mean time of transmission and the same total probability of transmission. The same total probability fixes the same outcome at late times, yet the two courses differ a great deal. Both peak around the same time, but more people are infected at any time in the exponential case, and the epidemic lasts longer. The top hat shows distinct waves of infection, with a period roughly equal to the delay before a new case becomes infectious, because the spreaders fall into separate generations. We read this as saying that news which people must digest before they pass it on, such as a long filing, can spread in waves, reaching as many people in the end while the number who know on a given day differs (our judgment). That timing matters when an announcement cuts the spread short, which Chapter 12 takes up.

Rates can also change with what people already know. In Duffie, Malamud and Manso’s model an agent meets others at a rate proportional to the effort she chooses, and when each extra unit of effort costs more than the last and she takes the efforts of others as given, her best effort falls as her own information becomes more precise (Section 6.6). A rate that falls only with the age of a story is the attention decay of Chapter 6, a way of stopping, and this section leaves it out (our judgment).5

8.4 Cascades of retweets

Vosoughi, Roy and Aral (2018) study the verified true and false news stories that spread on Twitter from 2006 to 2017, classified with the help of six independent fact-checking organizations whose classifications agreed 95 to 98% of the time. A cascade, in their definition, is an unbroken chain of retweets with a common, single origin, and a rumor has one cascade for each time a user tweeted its story independently, so one rumor can have many cascades. Its size is the number of users involved and its depth the number of retweet hops from the first tweet. The data include about 126,000 rumor cascades, spread by about 3 million people more than 4.5 million times.

The truth rarely spread to more than 1,000 users, while the top 1% of false-news cascades routinely spread to between 1,000 and 100,000 users. It took the truth about six times as long as falsehood to reach 1,500 users, and 20 times as long to reach a depth of 10.

Vosoughi, Roy and Aral found the cascades through English-language replies that linked to the fact-checking organizations’ websites, together with the tweets those replies answered and all of their retweets. The authors say that restricting the sample to tweets the six organizations had fact-checked may bring a selection bias, and on rumors that their own annotators agreed on they found results nearly identical to those of the main data. Their size counts the users involved in a cascade, the retweeters, and leaves out someone who saw a tweet and did not retweet it, so these numbers do not count the people who heard (our judgment). The authors describe the spread of falsehood as peer-to-peer diffusion characterized by a viral branching process, and in the main text this book has read they fit no branching ratio. A heavy-tailed distribution of cascade sizes can come from a branching ratio close to 1 or from retweeters whose audiences differ widely in size, as in Weng et al.’s model below, and the shape alone does not tell the two apart (our judgment), so this chapter does not set their distribution beside Figure 8.1 and uses only the numbers printed in their text.

Weng et al.’s (2012) model of Twitter lets one work out the mean number of offspring per post, one message that a user sends. In it a user posts a new meme, a piece of content such as a hashtag that users pass on, with probability pnp_n, which they set to 0.45, the share of posts with new hashtags in their data, and they tune the retweet activity to give 2.5 posts per user per unit of time. Each post then leads on average to 1−0.45/2.5=0.821 - 0.45/2.5 = 0.82 further posts, which only restates the mean of 2.5/0.45≈5.62.5/0.45 \approx 5.6 posts per meme that the calibration sets, since a mean cascade size of 1/(1−n)1/(1 - n) makes n=1−1/5.6n = 1 - 1/5.6, and any finite mean size gives an nn below 1 (our derivation; our calculation; Box 8.1). Weng et al. estimate no branching ratio from their Twitter data.

On a random network with the same numbers of users and links the same model gives far less uneven popularity, which the authors explain by hub users (Section 7.4). We read this as the mean number of offspring being set by the share of new memes and the activity of users, and the heavy tail by how unevenly the offspring are spread across posts, which Figure 8.1 leaves out (our judgment). Why false news travels farther than true news, and what novelty has to do with what people retweet, are questions for Chapter 9.6

8.5 Hawkes processes

In a Hawkes process the intensity, the expected number of events per unit time, rises with every event. Filimonov and Sornette write it as λ(t)=μ(t)+∑ti<th(t−ti),\lambda(t) = \mu(t) + \sum_{t_i < t} h(t - t_i), where the tit_i are the times of past events, the background intensity μ(t)\mu(t) accounts for exogenous events, those that do not depend on the past, and the kernel hh weights how much past events raise the rate of later ones and so sets the strength of the endogenous feedback. The branching ratio nn is the integral of the kernel over all later times (Box 8.2).

Every event is either an immigrant, arriving at the background intensity, or a descendant, and each immigrant triggers a cluster of descendants, the cascade of Section 8.2 whose sizes Figure 8.1 shows. Starting from one immigrant, a cluster ends with probability one when n<1n < 1, the subcritical case, and when n=1n = 1, the critical case, and it may grow without end when n>1n > 1, the supercritical case. In the subcritical case with a constant background intensity, nn is also the share of endogenous events, those triggered by earlier events, among all events, since one immigrant triggers on average n/(1−n)n/(1 - n) descendants. Over a long window the expected share is nn whatever the background intensity, since every event triggers on average nn direct offspring (our derivation); the reading needs the subcritical case and the linear form above, in which each past event adds its kernel to the rate, and an estimate of nn from data also rests on the background intensity the fit assumes, which the estimates of Section 8.6 take as constant (our judgment).

At the event layer, the description that records only when events happen and leaves out who knows what, count as an event each moment at which someone hears, in a large population under homogeneous mixing while almost no one has heard. If every spreader tells others at the same constant rate for the same fixed infectious period, these events form exactly a Hawkes process started by the seed, with branching ratio R0R_0 (our derivation). If the infectious period varies, a Hawkes process with the averaged kernel matches the mean rate of events and the branching ratio but, like any Hawkes process, gives every event a Poisson number of direct offspring, so at R0=2R_0 = 2 with an exponential infectious period it puts the extinction probability at 0.2031879 where the spread’s own is 0.5 and overstates the takeoff probability (our derivation; Section 8.2; Box 8.2).

Filimonov and Sornette note that the Hawkes model had been used for the dynamics of book sales and of views of YouTube videos, in studies that selected blockbusters, a selection bias toward dynamics close to critical (as reported by Filimonov and Sornette). No source this book has read estimates a branching ratio of spreading events from data, and the 0.82 of Section 8.4 comes from Weng et al.’s model (our judgment).

An immigrant can be a new event, such as a new story, a tweet sent independently or a new meme, that starts a cascade of its own, and such arrivals drop the part of one-time injection that has all news present at the start (our judgment). The root model’s constant external source belongs to the default, since it informs each person who has not heard at a fixed rate and always with the same news, while an immigrant arrives at the background intensity however many have heard, so the two match only in form (our judgment). With a constant background intensity no immigrant is scheduled; a background intensity that varies with time, as for the daily pattern of activity, is a different case, and Chapter 12 takes up only its sharpest form, announcements at set times. Under spontaneous stopping with an external source, each cascade that the source starts can die out while the source keeps starting new ones, so the spread as a whole goes on until everyone has heard (our derivation; Section 5.2). In a Hawkes process with n≤1n \le 1, likewise, every cluster ends while immigrants keep arriving. New coins are new events of this kind and come in Chapter 22.

News about firms arrives in clusters too. Tetlock (2011) takes news about S&P 500 firms from 1984 to 2004 and, for each firm, counts the trading days since its previous public news event, a trading day with at least one story about the firm, and does the same for days of extreme returns (working-paper version). Both kinds of event often come close together, roughly 60% within five trading days of the previous one and roughly 90% within 20, which he reads as a sign that the themes, topics and tones of clustered stories may be related (working-paper version). At the event layer a story that follows another can be a new event, which is an immigrant, or a retelling of old news, which looks much like a descendant, and the times of the stories alone do not say which (our judgment). Market reactions to stale stories, those carrying much information that has already appeared, which Tetlock marks by proxies such as another story about the firm in the previous week, partially reversed in the next week, and those to news with new earnings information did not (working-paper version; Section 10.7). A Hawkes model of news counts tells immigrants from descendants by timing alone, so a stale retelling that comes after a quiet spell can count as an immigrant, and an immigrant in such a model need not carry new information (our judgment).7

8.6 Branching ratios in markets

The branching ratios estimated in markets count events at the price layer. Filimonov and Sornette, and Hardiman, Bercot and Bouchaud, take the changes of the E-mini’s midprice in regular trading hours, and in this formulation every change has the same effect on the rate whatever its direction and size and the volumes at the bid or ask. A higher branching ratio means that price changes cluster more, and it says nothing about which way the price goes (our judgment).

Filimonov and Sornette assume a constant background intensity and an exponential kernel and fit them on windows of 10, 20 and 30 minutes. With these choices nn fluctuated around 0.3 from the first quarter of 1998 to the second quarter of 2000, stayed above 0.6 from 2002 and lay between 0.7 and 0.8, with spikes at 0.9, from 2007. They read the later values as more than 70% of price moves arising from positive feedback from past price moves, a reading that holds only within a linear Hawkes process with a constant background intensity (Section 8.5). They also write that their values most likely underestimate the real ones, since the short windows leave out earlier price moves that could trigger changes inside them. They report a standard deviation for their estimate of nn of about 0.13 to 0.18 in 1998 and never above 0.035 after 2002, which covers only the uncertainty from how they spread out events that share the same second (our judgment).

Hardiman, Bercot and Bouchaud take a kernel that decays as a power law and fit it on non-overlapping two-month periods from 1998 to 2011. They find that the kernel integrates to one whatever the period analyzed, so that the branching ratio appears to change little over the fourteen years and fluctuates about the critical value 1. They propose that fitting an exponential kernel on 30-minute windows to a critical power-law process whose cutoff at short times changes over time can give a subcritical branching ratio that rises over time, and on data they generated from a critical Hawkes process with n=1.0n = 1.0 it did.

Hardiman and Bouchaud’s mean-variance estimator needs only the mean and the variance of the number of events in a window; it is zero for a count whose variance equals its mean, as with Poisson statistics, and within a Hawkes framework reads any excess of the variance over the mean as a positive branching ratio. The estimate falls short of the branching ratio in any finite window. With a fixed window it reproduces the rise that Filimonov and Sornette report, and with a window that halves every 18 months, to adapt to ever faster interactions in the market, it stays roughly constant, on data from 1998 to 2013. On E-mini midprice changes in 2010 the estimate tends to 1.0 as the window grows, and letting the window grow without limit, they note, asks a stationary Hawkes model, one whose statistics do not change over time, to explain changes of the event rate on long time scales. They insist that their conclusion of criticality holds only if Hawkes processes represent markets exactly.

Later work by Filimonov and Sornette on kernels that decay slowly shows that outliers, edge effects and nonstationarity can bias such estimates significantly (as reported by Bacry, Mastromatteo and Muzy), and whether the readings near 1 come from such biases is not settled in our sources (our judgment). Read together, the estimates for one contract move with the kernel and the window by far more than the spread that Filimonov and Sornette’s handling of timestamps gives (our judgment). In a Hawkes model the cluster that one exogenous midprice change starts has the sizes of Figure 8.1, where a branching ratio of 0.95 instead of 1 makes clusters that reach 1,000 events about 17 times rarer (our calculation; Section 8.2), so these estimates leave open how large the largest clusters of midprice changes get (our judgment).

Scope. The branching ratio changes with the form of the kernel, the length of the window and how the background intensity is set. An exponential kernel with windows of 10 to 30 minutes reads about 0.3 for 1998 to 2000 and 0.7 to 0.8 from 2007, a power-law kernel with two-month windows reads about 1 from 1998 to 2011, and as the window grows without limit the mean-variance estimate for 2010 also tends to 1, so over the long run the market may be close to critical. The estimates count changes of the E-mini’s midprice at the price layer and come from the same series they are meant to explain. No reading is free of these settings, and only within a linear Hawkes process with a constant background intensity can a reading be taken as the share of endogenous events. Another candidate, that the long memory of trading activity (correlations in its rate that fade too slowly to add up to a finite total) has an exponent close to that of volatility, has not yet been tested and is not a conclusion of this book; Hardiman, Bercot and Bouchaud compared it with published values in one market only, the E-mini, and computed the exponent for activity from the kernel’s exponent, so the activity exponent and the kernel exponent come from the same correlations of the event rate; there are no measurements from several markets.

The times of events alone cannot tell self-excitation from clustering in time that comes from outside (our judgment). A background intensity that changes over time, with news that arrives in clusters as in Tetlock’s data (Section 8.5) or announcements at set times, also raises the variance of the count above its mean, and a fit with a constant background intensity would read that excess as a larger branching ratio (our judgment). Hardiman, Bercot and Bouchaud remove the daily pattern of activity before fitting, since without it the fit would read the slow change of the event rate during the day as the shape of the kernel. One test sets the exogenous events that a fitted model infers beside the times of news and announcements, and another adds a news kernel, a term that raises the rate around the time of each news item (our judgment).

Spreading events and trading events both have a branching ratio, and the two count different things (Table 8.1).

8.8 A trader’s checklist

None of these questions says what to buy or sell, and good answers do not make a trade safe (our judgment).

  1. Will the story take off at all? Check: before reading any number for how far a story goes, ask how its spreaders stop and how long each keeps spreading. In a large population under homogeneous mixing, with spreaders who stop on their own, one seed and R0=2R_0 = 2, the takeoff probability is about 0.80 with a fixed spreading time and 0.5 with a constant stopping rate, and the final size about 0.80 in both (our derivation; our calculation; Section 8.2).
  2. How heavy is the tail? Check: when every event has a Poisson number of direct offspring with the same mean, a branching ratio near 1 gives mostly small cascades and a few very large ones, and one a little below 1 makes the large ones far rarer (our calculation; Figure 8.1). On a network such as Twitter’s, audiences of very different sizes can also make a heavy tail, so the shape alone does not give a branching ratio (our judgment; Section 8.4).
  3. Is it a new event or old news again? Testable: in Tetlock’s news about S&P 500 firms from 1984 to 2004, reactions to stories his proxies mark as stale partially reversed in the next week and those to news with new earnings information did not (working-paper version; Section 8.5). The times of news events alone do not tell new events from retellings, and whether the difference holds after 2004 can be tested (our judgment).
  4. Which kernel, window, period and events? Check: before quoting a branching ratio for a market, write down its kernel, window, period and the events it counts, and refit with another kernel or window to see whether it moves (our judgment). For the E-mini the estimate changes with all of these and with how the background intensity is set, counts midprice changes at the price layer and comes from the same series it is meant to explain (Section 8.6 and its scope box). Only within a linear Hawkes process with a constant background intensity can a reading be taken as the share of endogenous events.
  5. Self-excited or driven by news? Testable: set the exogenous events that a fitted model infers beside the times of news and announcements, or add a news kernel to the model (our judgment; Section 8.6). In our sources no one has made the first comparison for the E-mini (our judgment).

8.9 Summary

  • What chance changes depends on how spreaders stop. Under spontaneous stopping, with constant rates and homogeneous mixing, a cascade from one seed can die out early, with a chance that does not shrink as the population grows (our derivation; Section 8.1).
  • For SIR in a large population under homogeneous mixing, with R0=2R_0 = 2, the takeoff probability is about 0.80 with a fixed infectious period and 0.5 with an exponential one, while the final size is about 0.80 in both (our derivation; our calculation; Section 8.2).
  • When every event has a Poisson number of direct offspring with the same mean nn, cascade sizes have a heavy tail at n=1n = 1, and a branching ratio a little below 1 makes large cascades far rarer (our calculation; Figure 8.1).
  • In Vosoughi, Roy and Aral’s fact-checked Twitter cascades from 2006 to 2017, the largest false-news cascades reached far more retweeters than true ones; the sample may be biased, and the main text this book read fits no branching ratio (Section 8.4).
  • In a linear Hawkes process the branching ratio is the integral of the kernel and, when subcritical, the expected share of endogenous events over a long window; estimates from data rest on the background intensity the fit assumes (our derivation; our judgment; Section 8.5).
  • The E-mini’s branching ratios count midprice changes at the price layer, kept apart from spreading events in Table 8.1, and change with the form of the kernel, the window, the period and how the background intensity is set (Section 8.6 and its scope box).
  • The times of events alone cannot tell self-excitation from clustering in time that comes from outside, such as news that arrives in clusters or announcements at set times (our judgment; Section 8.6 and Table 8.1).

8.10 Boxes for quantitative readers

Box 8.1 Branching processes in symbols

Notation. nn is the branching ratio, the mean number of events that one event triggers directly; elsewhere in the book the letter has other meanings. LL is the number of events in a cascade, the seed included, and ℓ\ell a value it takes. PextP_{\text{ext}} is the chance that the cascade ends, and 1−Pext1 - P_{\text{ext}} is the takeoff probability.

Poisson offspring. Let each event have a Poisson number of direct offspring with mean nn, independently of the others. This is the model of Figure 8.1, in the settings that Section 8.2 gives. A cascade ends when the cascades of all the seed’s offspring end, so PextP_{\text{ext}} is the smallest root in [0,1][0, 1] of x=en(x−1),x = e^{n(x - 1)}, which is 1 for n≤1n \le 1 and lies between 0 and 1 for n>1n > 1 (our derivation). With x=s∞x = s_\infty, the share who never catch the idea in Box 6.1, and n=R0n = R_0, this is the final-state equation of that box, which holds for a fixed infectious period too (Section 8.2), so in this setting the takeoff probability equals the final size when the infectious period is fixed (our derivation).

Geometric offspring. In the same setting, if a spreader stops at a constant rate, the infectious period is exponential and the number of offspring early on is geometric with mean nn, that is P(k)=nk/(1+n)k+1P(k) = n^k/(1 + n)^{k+1} for every whole number k≥0k \ge 0. Then Pext=1/nP_{\text{ext}} = 1/n for n>1n > 1 (our derivation).

Cascade sizes. For Poisson offspring LL has the Borel distribution P(L=ℓ)=e−nℓ(nℓ)ℓ−1ℓ!,ℓ=1,2,…,P(L = \ell) = \frac{e^{-n\ell}\,(n\ell)^{\ell - 1}}{\ell!}, \qquad \ell = 1, 2, \ldots, whose sum over ℓ\ell is PextP_{\text{ext}} (our derivation). For n<1n < 1 the mean is 1/(1−n)1/(1 - n), the seed plus its n/(1−n)n/(1 - n) descendants of all generations. At n=1n = 1, P(L≥ℓ)≈2/(πℓ)P(L \ge \ell) \approx \sqrt{2/(\pi\ell)} for large ℓ\ell, the critical line of Figure 8.1. For n<1n < 1, P(L=ℓ)P(L = \ell) falls about as ℓ−3/2e−cℓ\ell^{-3/2} e^{-c\ell} with c=n−1−ln⁡nc = n - 1 - \ln n, so cascades much larger than 1/c1/c events are rare, with the values that Section 8.2 gives (our derivation; our calculation).

Mean offspring in Weng et al.’s model. One unit of time is as many steps as there are users. The parameter pnp_n sets the novelty that enters the system, the number of cascades, and prp_r the overall retweet activity, the size of cascades; pn=0.45p_n = 0.45 matches a share of posts with new hashtags of about 0.45±0.050.45 \pm 0.05, and the average activity, the number of posts per user per unit of time, is 2.5 in their standard model. Each user thus starts about 0.45 new memes and writes 2.5 posts per unit of time, and every post that does not start a meme copies an existing one. A meme therefore gets 2.5/0.45≈5.62.5/0.45 \approx 5.6 posts on average, and each post leads to 1−0.45/2.5=0.821 - 0.45/2.5 = 0.82 further posts on average (our derivation; our calculation). In the model, then, about 0.45/2.5=0.180.45/2.5 = 0.18 of posts start a new meme, against the 0.45 of posts with new hashtags in their data, so the 0.82 belongs to the model as calibrated and the data do not show it directly (our calculation; our judgment).

Networks. In Kenah and Robins’s network SIR, in a large population, the takeoff probability equals the probability that the seed lies in the giant in-component, the large set of people whose infection would start a large outbreak, and the final size equals the share of the network in the giant out-component, the large set of people a large outbreak reaches. In bond percolation, where each link is open or closed independently and an open link passes the infection both ways, the two sets coincide (our judgment); with a random infectious period they differ, and bond percolation overestimates the takeoff probability.

The rigorous versions of this box’s own derivations are to be given in the appendix.10

Box 8.2 The Hawkes process in symbols

Notation. λ(t)\lambda(t) is the intensity, μ(t)\mu(t) the background intensity, hh the kernel, tit_i the times of past events, nn the branching ratio, as in Box 8.1, and Λ\Lambda the mean rate of events. These letters are unrelated to the meeting rate λ\lambda of Box 1.2, the spreading rate λ\lambda and the largest eigenvalue Λ1\Lambda_1 of Chapter 7, and the μ\mu that Box 6.1 quotes from Pastor-Satorras et al.

Intensity and branching ratio. With the intensity λ(t)=μ(t)+∑ti<th(t−ti)\lambda(t) = \mu(t) + \sum_{t_i < t} h(t - t_i) of Section 8.5, the branching ratio is n=∫0∞h(t)dt.n = \int_0^\infty h(t)\,dt. For n<1n < 1 and a constant μ\mu, one immigrant triggers on average n+n2+…=n/(1−n)n + n^2 + \ldots = n/(1 - n) descendants, so endogenous events make up a share nn of all events.

Three cases. For n<1n < 1 and a constant μ\mu the process is stationary, its statistics unchanged over time, with mean rate Λ=μ/(1−n)≥μ\Lambda = \mu/(1 - n) \ge \mu. For n>1n > 1 it is not stationary and may explode in finite time. For a positive μ\mu the formula gives no finite mean rate at n=1n = 1 (our derivation). Brémaud and Massoulié analyzed this case, and Bacry, Mastromatteo and Muzy report from their work that with no immigrants (μ=0\mu = 0) and a kernel whose tail falls as t−(1+γ)t^{-(1+\gamma)}, with 0<γ<1/20 < \gamma < 1/2, the mean intensity is finite, and that such a process is nontrivial only for γ\gamma in that interval. A stationary Hawkes process with finite mean and variance whose event rate has long memory, an autocovariance with a diverging integral, must be critical.

Spreading events. In the setting of Section 8.5, a large population under homogeneous mixing early on, a spreader who tells others at a constant rate during a fixed infectious period gives a kernel equal to that rate during the period and zero after it (our derivation). Under permanent spreading the kernel never falls to zero and the branching ratio has no bound, so the process grows without end and cannot die out early (our derivation; Section 8.1). Once many have heard, each spreader finds fewer people to tell, the rate is no longer a sum of kernels of past events, and the description stops being a Hawkes process (our derivation).

Estimation. An estimate of nn from fitting a chosen kernel by maximum likelihood depends heavily on that choice. In Filimonov and Sornette’s windows of 10 to 30 minutes, truncation lowers nn and raises μ\mu. Hardiman and Bouchaud’s mean-variance estimator uses only the mean μW\mu_W and the variance σW2\sigma_W^2 of the number of events in a window of length WW, ñ=1−μW/σW2,\tilde n = 1 - \sqrt{\mu_W/\sigma_W^2}, which is 0 for Poisson statistics, where the variance equals the mean, and falls short of nn in any finite window. Here μW\mu_W is unrelated to the background intensity. Bacry, Mastromatteo and Muzy print the ratio under the root the other way up, and this box follows Hardiman and Bouchaud.11
